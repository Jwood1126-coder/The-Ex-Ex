# ACT IV — THE CONTINENT
*(December 1839 – April 1840)*

---

## Chapter 21 — South Again

The four ships lost each other almost immediately. A gale scattered the squadron within days of Sydney, which suited the plan well enough — the rendezvous was set for lonely Macquarie Island and then the ice itself, and down there every vessel would be alone in any case — but it meant the campaign opened as it would run: four wooden ships, out of sight and out of reach of one another, each carrying its own share of the national reputation into the worst sea on Earth.

The *Flying Fish* had the worst of the start, because she had the worst of everything. The little schooner had come out of Sydney short-handed — hard experience of her accommodations had not made volunteers — and she went south with a crew that could barely work her, into seas that had nearly finished her the previous season with a full complement. Her assigned station was the squadron's southern flank. She kept it. Whatever else this book has to say about the expedition's management, the men it sent to sea kept doing things like that: undermanned, in a ninety-six-ton harbor boat, holding formation on a course for Antarctica because those were the orders and she was, after all, the record-holder.

The reshuffling before Sydney had also moved this story's witnesses to their stations. Reynolds went south in the *Peacock* — out from under the flagship's weather, into Hudson's command, where the tone of the quarterdeck let a man breathe — and the transfer put the expedition's best pair of eyes aboard the ship that would need them most, at the masthead where the campaign's first prize was waiting. Charlie went south where Charlie always was: before the mast of the flagship, one of the hands whose fingers and lungs the season would be spent from, close enough to the commander to take his weather daily and powerless to do anything about it but endure and remember. Between the two of them, the ice cruise would be witnessed from exactly the two places that mattered — the masthead that saw the continent, and the deck that paid for it.

The ships could read their own progress south in the sea itself. The water changed before anything else did — the towed thermometers stepping down day by day as the vessels crossed out of the warm Australian current into the cold circumpolar water, the surveyor's version of a border crossing, logged in degrees. Then the escort changed: whales blowing along the horizon in numbers no man aboard had seen, the albatrosses joined by petrels and, at last, by the small hard birds that meant ice was making the weather somewhere below the horizon. New Year's Day, 1840, passed at sea in falling temperatures with the squadron dispersed and running south, every ship's company privately conducting the same arithmetic about the season: eight or nine workable weeks, of which the passage was already spending two.

What the men knew of their destination amounted to a wall and a warning. Cook had circled the whole southern ocean sixty-five years before, been stopped everywhere by the pack, seen nothing he would call land, and written the region's epitaph: whatever lay farther south, the world would get no good of it, and he would not envy the man who went looking. Since then the sealers had nibbled at the Peninsula side of the circle, and a British master named Weddell had found one freakishly open sea; but along the thousands of miles now ahead of the squadron — the quarter of the circle below Australia — the chart was empty in the fullest sense. Not uncharted coastline: *unresolved reality*. Serious geographers maintained every possibility at once — a polar continent, a frozen archipelago, an ice-capped open sea — and the Ex. Ex. was sailing to settle a question about what the planet was.

They crossed into the Antarctic's dominion in the first days of January, and the reception followed the standard order of service. Fog that closed like a hand. Gales with hail in them. The first icebergs — then bergs beyond counting, tabular monsters miles long, flat-topped, fortress-white, drifting out of the murk with surf breaking against their walls. The ships learned to use them: a boat under a berg's lee, men with picks quarrying the blue ice, and the squadron drank the far south melted in its own coppers — water older than the country that was charting it, and the men knew it for a marvel and said so. And the light: this far south in high summer the sun barely set, the nights compressed to a blue dusk, so that the whole campaign would be fought in one long working day — a gift to surveyors, a torment to men who could never truly stand down. Watch succeeded watch in freezing fog, lookouts listening for ice they could not see, and below decks the ships were heated by very little beyond the galley and the men themselves. Wet clothes froze on the man; the rigging grew a hide of ice that came down in sheets when the yards were braced; and the surgeons opened the ledgers — frostbite, rheumatism, rupture, the grinding injuries of heavy work done fast in the cold — that would fill all season. It bears saying one more time, because the men never stopped saying it: the hulls were bare. No doubling, no sheathing, no ice-bows. The plan, stripped of its language, was to sail thin wooden vessels along the edge of the pack for as long as luck held, and chart whatever God allowed.

On about the tenth of January the *Vincennes* met the barrier — the consolidated pack, running east and west out of sight, a white coast of the sea's own making — and turned west along it, and the great traverse began. The *Peacock* and the *Porpoise* found it on their own stations and did the same, three ships strung out along hundreds of miles of ice edge, each probing every bay and lead that opened southward, each throwing back reports of the same teasing signs: the water shoaling where no bottom should be, penguins with stones in their gullets, the ice discolored with earth. Somewhere below the fog and the ice-blink, either there was a continent or there was not. No one alive knew. Within the week, two men in their twenties would.

## Chapter 22 — Land

It came first not to the commander but to the mastheads and the young — which is fitting, in a story that runs on midshipmen.

On January 16, 1840, in clearing weather, Passed Midshipman Henry Eld and Passed Midshipman William Reynolds — our Reynolds, berthed in the *Peacock* for the campaign — went aloft together and saw, beyond the ice field to the south, what neither of them could make into anything but land: two distinct summits, high, solid, holding their shape and their bearing while the bergs drifted, standing over the intervening ice the way mountains stand over a plain. They did what trained surveyors do with a miracle: they stayed aloft and tested it. Watched it against the drift. Took bearings. Called each other's eyes to it and cross-examined what they saw, two men who between them had spent the better part of a decade learning exactly how the sea manufactures false coasts — and it held. They came down glowing and reported it, and here the expedition's temperament committed a small disaster of bookkeeping: the report was received without ceremony, the sighting logged casually where it was logged at all, the ship's position never fixed to the moment with the gravity the moment deserved. Hudson — a superb seaman and a cautious recorder — did not choose to enter a discovery of a continent on the strength of two young men's eyes. The two young men never wavered, then or ever; and when, years later, the date of the continent's discovery became a matter of national honor, courtroom evidence, and international sneering, the casualness of that afternoon's paperwork would cost the expedition dearly, and no one would feel it more bitterly than the two who had been right first and recorded worst. What they had seen from that masthead was the coast of East Antarctica — land no human being, in the whole history of the species, had ever seen before — and the charts eventually said so in the plainest way charts can: the peaks carry their names today, Eld Peak and Reynolds Peak, on the George V Coast. The index entry for the discovery of Antarctica does not generally include the two twenty-somethings who called it first from the *Peacock*'s rigging. This book's does.

Three days later, on January 19, land again — and this time with weight, and in stereo. From the *Peacock*, Reynolds (him again) raised a dark, bold headland standing out of the ice — the cape that carries his captain's name now, Cape Hudson — and this time the sighting went into the ship's record with position and bearings attached; the lesson of the sixteenth had cost three days and been learned. And to the west, out of sight over the ice horizon, the flagship was having the same morning: from the *Vincennes*, Wilkes and his officers made out mountains beyond the pack, fixed them, logged them, and the commander had the date he would defend for the rest of his life with everything in him — the nineteenth of January, 1840, the day the United States Exploring Expedition put land inside the Antarctic Circle on the record in duplicate, from two ships a hundred miles apart, neither knowing what the other had seen. For once the expedition's chronic dispersal had done it a favor: the discovery had independent witnesses who could not have colluded if they'd tried.

He would need to, because of a coincidence so large it reads like fiction. Some hundreds of miles to the west, on those same days, the French expedition under Dumont d'Urville — the rival whose sailing had helped shame Congress into funding the Ex. Ex. in the first place — was raising the same continental coast. The French had earned it the hard way: the *Astrolabe* and the *Zélée* were three years out of Toulon, their companies thinned and yellowed by scurvy and fever, their commander a veteran explorer in failing health making one last thrust south from Hobart because his king had promised a bounty for a high latitude and because he was, in his own upholstered way, as unable to stop as Wilkes. On the twenty-first, d'Urville's men pulled to a bare islet under the ice cliffs, scrambled ashore over the rocks, ran up the tricolor, collected specimens of the continent with their hands, and drank a bottle of Bordeaux to the King of the French in the coldest wine service in history. D'Urville named the country for his wife: Terre Adélie — a piece of gallantry that has outlasted every empire involved, and passed from the coast to its penguins, so that the lady is now chiefly immortal as a bird. When the calendars were later laid side by side, the priority fight that followed was exactly as edifying as such fights always are: logbooks, dates, insinuations, national honor conducted in footnotes. D'Urville himself did not live to fight much of it. Within two years of standing on his islet, France's greatest living explorer, who had survived three circumnavigations, the Pacific's reefs, and the Antarctic pack, died with his wife and son in a burning railway carriage outside Paris — the new machine age collecting, in an afternoon, the man the old dangers never could.

Hold the other image instead, the one under the quarrel. One unpeopled coast a thousand miles wide, two squadrons, two languages — and the human species discovering a seventh of the world's land twice in the same week, like a man patting two pockets and finding the same key in both.

The two expeditions even met. On January 30 the *Porpoise*, working west along the barrier, sighted two strange sail in the ice — the *Astrolabe* and the *Zélée* — and bore up to speak them. What followed was the Río Negro conducted between navies: a fog of misread intentions in which the French appeared, to American eyes, to make sail and stand away; whereupon Ringgold, pride answering pride, hauled his wind in turn; and the two discoverers of Antarctica passed out of each other's sight without exchanging a word, each side's account blaming the other's manners forever after. History had arranged the only meeting of the two fleets that found the last continent, and both parties spent it declining to be snubbed first. The *Independence* affair, it turns out, was never really a Wilkes problem. It was the age, wearing his face.

## Chapter 23 — The Peacock in the Ice

On January 24, the sea called the *Peacock*'s bluff.

Hudson had worked her deep into a bay in the ice, closing the land the mastheads kept reporting, when the pack shifted around her. Attempting to wear clear, she struck — ice under the counter, then ice on the quarter — and the blows found the one part of a ship that cannot be spared: the rudder was wrenched from its fittings, its head shattered, the tiller useless. A six-hundred-fifty-ton ship, unsteerable, embayed in drifting ice, with the weather going gray to windward. What followed was some thirty hours that every man aboard afterward ranked with the worst and the finest of his life. The ice closed and came on; the crew fended floes off the wooden sides with spars and with their own arms; ice anchors went out and parties warped the ship yard by yard through the slackening leads, hauling their home along by muscle. Boats were hoisted out and readied, and every hand understood the actual meaning of boats in that sea — not rescue, only slower arithmetic. She took her worst blow at the stern, the ice coming in over the quarter and carrying away bulwarks, boats' davits, the spare spars — the sound of it, men said, like the ship being opened with an axe. Below, in freezing water and worse light, the carpenters and the armorer fought to unship, rebuild, and rehang a rudder while bergs boomed around the hull like siege guns. And through all of it the ship's routine simply refused to die: watches relieved, meals served, orders given quietly and obeyed at a run. The *Peacock* — the ship whose rot every officer had reported and every economy had ignored — held together, just, and was conned out of the closing bay under a scrap of sail by seamanship that even Wilkes' enemies never stopped praising. When she finally cleared the last of the pack into open water, her people cheered her — cheered the rotten, condemned, magnificent old ship — and Hudson took his wounded, hand-steered command north to Sydney for rebuilding, having been, by the sober estimate of the men aboard her, within one shift of the wind of becoming the Antarctic's first shipwreck: three hundred miles of frozen coast to leeward, no rescue possible, no second act.

Sydney, which had asked the Americans in December whether their wills were made, got its answer in February when the *Peacock* limped back in through the heads: bulwarks gone, stern patched, a ship visibly beaten half to death — and every man of her company alive, and her officers already talking about the land they had seen. The colony's seamen read her hull the way surveyors read a coast, and the tone of the Sydney papers shifted from irony to something near awe. She would spend months rebuilding, out of the campaign for good; Hudson had brought back the ship, the crew, and the sighting of the sixteenth of January, and if the last of these had been logged with the care the first two were handled with, half the battles of 1842 would never have been fought.

Her withdrawal — and the release of the battered *Flying Fish*, which had taken her own mauling and been sent away toward New Zealand — left the campaign to the flagship and the brig, and left Wilkes exactly where his whole nature had pointed since 1828: alone on the *Vincennes*' quarterdeck, in front of the unknown, with no senior officer on the planet and a coastline to win.

What he did with the next month is why his name is on the map, and it deserves to be told with the same directness as his cruelties. Day after day, week after week, he drove the *Vincennes* west along the barrier, standing into every opening. One deep bay ran promisingly south for miles before closing in sheer ice walls; Wilkes named it Disappointment Bay, a rare moment of the chart speaking the commander's actual feelings, and put the ship about and went looking for the next. On January 25 the mountains stood clear again beyond the ice — the ranges behind what is now the Cook Ice Shelf — and there was no more real doubt aboard, whatever the paperwork later pretended: the gray masses holding their bearings week after week were not cloud, not looming, not hope. They were the thing itself.

At the month's end the sea presented its counterargument. Off Piner Bay the *Vincennes* was caught embayed among bergs in a rising gale, and spent a night that the survivors described in the language men keep for the time they did not die: snow thick to blindness, the ship wearing and tacking in a corridor of ice walls taller than the mastheads, breakers audible on floes no eye could find, the rigging sheathed in frozen spray so the ropes ran stiff as iron bars, every officer and man on deck through the dark hours. Twice the ice showed close under the lee and twice the ship came around in time, handled at the outer limit of the art, and when gray morning let them count their situation they were alive by margins no one aboard mistook for anything but luck. Wilkes' own account of that night is the best writing he ever did — terror is the one subject that reliably burned the pomposity out of his prose — and it ends not with retreat but with the course made good: west.

The ship, however, was spending men. The sick list swelled — frostbite, ruptures, lungs, exhaustion — until the surgeons put it on paper: the crew was breaking, and the ship should go north. Wilkes convened his officers and required their opinions in writing, a lawyer's instinct that would serve everyone at the courts-martial. The officers agreed with the surgeons, nearly to a man. Wilkes read the opinions, recorded his contempt for them — cowardice dressed as medicine, was his private verdict — and held his course west, and here the reader meets the whole man in a single decision, because there is no honest way to split it: the officers were right about the men, and Wilkes was right about the coast, and he spent the one to get the other.

On February 12, with the coast now traced from 140°30′ east to 112°16′ east — eight hundred miles of it, mountains and ice cliffs trending on and on with the stubbornness only a continent can manage, rock coming up in the dredge from the seafloor — Wilkes allowed the conclusion into the record under its own name: they had discovered the Antarctic Continent. Not an island. Not a berg-field. A *trend*, wider than Australia is wide. He pressed on west, toward the edge of what is now Queen Mary Land, through a February that gave back nothing easily: the new season's ice already making in the bays, the running rigging freezing in the blocks, the coast walling itself off behind pack the ship could no longer risk. The men before the mast were spent in the particular way that never shows at muster — sound enough to haul, too worn to warm — and the ship herself was scarred from the ice and the gales, her people navigating now on the seamanship equivalent of memory. On February 21, at the headland he named Termination Land — the chart confessing, once again, exactly what the commander felt — with the season closing and the sick list still lengthening, the *Vincennes* finally came about and stood north. Fifteen hundred miles of new coast lay astern by the campaign's full reckoning, charted from an unstrengthened wooden sloop in sixty-odd days below the sixties. The men who had carried the cost of it in their fingers and lungs paid their commander the strange wages such commanders earn: they cursed him without reservation, and were proud of that cruise for the rest of their lives.

## Chapter 24 — What Counts as Discovery?

Wilkes came back to find the *Peacock* alive and under repair in Sydney, and the world already disputing his continent. He would spend the rest of his life defending it, and the defense would cost him nearly as much as the discovery earned.

The vulnerabilities were real, and this book states them plainly because a court-martial will. Antarctic air is an instrument-grade deceiver. Cold, dense layers bend light along the sea, lofting real coasts into view across impossible distances and manufacturing phantom ones — *looming*, the sailors called it — so that an honest man at a masthead can fix mountains where no mountains are. Some of Wilkes' plotted coast was exactly such a mirage, and the man who proved it was the third player in the great southern season. James Clark Ross came down in 1841 with everything the Americans had been denied: the *Erebus* and *Terror*, purpose-built bomb vessels with massive frames, doubled and coppered and internally braced for ice — the Royal Navy's answer to a question the United States Navy had answered with paint. Wilkes, in a gesture that was pure both-of-him, had sent Ross from Sydney a copy of his own track and discoveries — generous beyond any obligation, and impossible to receive as anything but a boast — and Ross repaid it in the imperial style: he broke through the pack into the open sea that now bears his name, found the smoking volcano he named for his ship, met the ice barrier that stopped the world for the next sixty years, and, on his way, sailed his squadron across more than one position where the American chart showed mountains, and reported the open water with relish. (That some ice-free sea had been sighted by a Russian squadron under Bellingshausen twenty years before either of them was a fact the century had not yet assembled; the discovery of Antarctica was an event the nineteenth century managed to hold several times without noticing it was full.) The January 16 sighting had been handled so loosely that its paper trail was thin. The French claim sat on the same square of the calendar as Wilkes' own, and his enemies — by 1842 he possessed a squadron's worth, under oath — would assemble it all into the cruelest charge an explorer can face: that he lied about what he saw.

Time has ruled, and the reader should hear the ruling now, so the trial scenes ahead read as tragedy rather than suspense. The continent is there. The coast the *Vincennes* tracked is the coast of East Antarctica; the peaks the two passed midshipmen called from the *Peacock*'s rigging bear their names on modern charts; and the vast sector below Australia appears on every map printed today as Wilkes Land — the planet's largest monument to a man his own navy convicted, in the end, of something else entirely. He got details wrong the way every first mapper of anything gets details wrong. He got the answer right, first or within a day of first, in ships that had no business surviving the attempt. Cook had left the far south as a question; the joke expedition, under the lieutenant nobody wanted, brought it home as a continent.

The squadron gathered itself through the autumn of 1840 in the friendlier latitudes. The *Porpoise* and the *Flying Fish* refitted at the Bay of Islands, New Zealand, where members of the expedition found themselves bystanders at one of history's hinges: that February, at Waitangi, the British Crown and the assembled Māori chiefs were signing the treaty that founded modern New Zealand, and men of the Ex. Ex. stood in the crowd and watched an empire acquire a country with a pen — watched the speeches, the translations doing their fateful, disputed work between two languages' ideas of what was being given, the chiefs coming forward one by one to sign the paper the next two centuries would argue about. The Americans were surveyors; they knew a boundary being drawn when they saw one; and more than one of them registered, in the privacy of a journal, that the Pacific's blank spaces were closing on every side now, and that the flags doing the closing were rarely their own.

In April the reunited ships lay at Nukuʻalofa in Tonga, paying calls on King Josiah — Aleamotuʻa, the aging Christian king of the coastal establishment — and on the visit's real magnet, the rising power of the group: Taufaʻāhau of Haʻapai, a chief in his prime, physically enormous, politically patient, master already of half the archipelago's future. The Americans measured him the way they measured harbors, and got it right; the century would come to know him as George Tupou I, unifier of Tonga and founder of the only Pacific kingdom that would ride out the entire colonial age with its crown intact. Charts, provisions, ceremony; the surveying machine turning over smoothly now, two years run-in; and beneath the ceremony, the officers quietly absorbing the briefings — from missionaries, from traders, from every white man on the beach — about the archipelago next door.

Then, on the fourth of May, the squadron stood in among the reefs of the group everyone had been half-dreading since the sailing orders were first whispered at Norfolk — the islands the trade charts marked, where they marked anything, with the one word the Pacific used for them. Fiji.

The voyage's warmest waters were ahead, and they held its darkest chapter.
