## Part I — The witnesses' whereabouts

### Reynolds was NOT at Malolo (July 24–26, 1840) — ANSWERED

- Wilkes names Reynolds only **twice** in all of *Narrative* vol. 3, both in June at
  Muthuata (north Vanua Levu) on *Peacock* duty under Capt. Hudson; he appears in **none
  of the Malolo rosters**, which Wilkes lists officer-by-officer. vol. 3 grep: "Reynolds"
  at OCR 12100, 12125 only (pp. 239–240) · E ⚑
- **His Fiji-season assignment:** the *Peacock* under Hudson. ~June 20 he and Midshipman
  Hudson (the captain's son) were sent ashore at Muthuata as hostages for the king's
  visit ("Immediately Passed Midshipman Reynolds and Midshipman Hudson (the captain's
  son) were sent on shore; notwithstanding which, the old king was not inclined to
  venture," p. 239, OCR ~12100). On June 22 he started boat-survey duty in the division
  under Lt. Budd with Midshipman Hudson, from Kie Island off Muthuata ("The same day two
  divisions of boats, the one under Lieutenant Walker and Midshipman Blair, the other
  under Lieutenant Budd, Passed Midshipman Reynolds, and Midshipman Hudson, started on
  surveying duty, the one to the eastward, the other westward from Kie Island, off
  Muthuata, on the north side of Vanua-levu," p. 240, OCR ~12123). Budd's boats came
  alongside ~July 3, out again next morning to bring the survey down to Mbua Bay
  (pp. 248–249). · E — ⚑ Philbrick's ch. 9 apparatus (via summary) says "eastern Vanua
  Levu"/"second-in-command of a two-boat survey"; Wilkes' text has Budd's division going
  westward — direction discrepancy may be a fetch-summary artifact; check Penguin edition.
- **July 16 expedition roster — Reynolds absent.** Wilkes' detached western expedition
  (the one that ends at Malolo), officers named in full: "On the 16th of July, the tender
  and boats being prepared, I ordered the following officers upon an expedition:
  Assistant-Surgeon Fox, Acting-Master Sinclair, Passed Midshipman Eld, and Mr. Agate, to
  accompany me in the tender; Lieutenant Alden and Midshipman Henry in the first, and
  Lieutenant Underwood in the second cutter of the Vincennes; Lieutenant Emmons and
  Midshipman Clark in the first cutter of the Peacock." (p. 262, OCR ~13115.)
  Simultaneously Hudson was ordered to resurvey Mbua Bay, then round to Muthuata ("Orders
  were left with Captain Hudson to resurvey the Bay of Mbua… and after having completed
  this duty, to proceed with the Peacock round to Muthuata, and then return for the
  Vincennes," p. 262, OCR ~13126) — fixing the *Peacock* (Reynolds' ship) off Vanua Levu,
  ~150+ miles from Malolo, through the attack window. Wilkes on July 24: "The two chief
  vessels of my squadron were at a distance…" (p. 282, OCR ~13979). · E
- **July 24 boat parties (the killings):** Lt. Alden + Midshipman Henry (Vincennes 1st
  cutter), Lt. Underwood (the Leopard), Lt. Emmons + Midshipman Clark (Peacock's 1st
  cutter, joined 9 a.m.); interpreters Oahu Sam (Vendovi's barber) and John Sac the New
  Zealander, taken from Alden's boat into Underwood's shore party. Underwood had left
  seven of his ten rifles aboard the brig days before, keeping three. pp. 282–284 · E
- **July 26 attack roster — Reynolds absent.** Landing party of 70 under Lt.-Cmdt.
  Ringgold in three divisions (Ringgold, Lts. Johnson, Maury) from Porpoise and tender
  crews, with Lt. North, Passed Midshipmen Sinclair and Eld, Asst-Surgeon Holmes, and
  Agate attached; water force under Wilkes himself — "I kept under my own immediate
  command, my gig, the first cutters of the Vincennes and Peacock, under Lieutenants
  Alden and Emmons, and the tender's boat, under Midshipman Clark" (p. 290, OCR ~14369);
  Eld earlier despatched with the Leopard before daylight; Acting-Master Totten (sick)
  left commanding the brig; pilot Tom Granby with the grounded tender. No Reynolds
  anywhere. pp. 288–291 · E

### Charlie Erskine at Malolo — per span 3 (see erskine-harvest.md Part II.d)

Erskine never states where he was during the attack itself, and by his own boat-list he
was in neither boat present on July 24. His fight detail is secondhand (most likely via
Joseph G. Clark). He is an eyewitness from the burial onward and an **unambiguous
participant in the July 26 reprisal party** ("At nine o'clock we landed well armed,"
TYBTM p. 176) and the surrender ceremony (pp. 179–181). Most defensible base position:
Charlie was with the squadron/boat pool off Malolo, not at the massacre; present for
burial, burning, and the crawl. ⚑ Erskine dates the attack **July 22** vs the base's
July 24–26 — keep as his error (rule 5); he gives NO native death toll.

### Vendovi's ships — SETTLED (dossier 11 open item CLOSED)

Vendovi was taken and held aboard the **Peacock** at Rewa (the leave-taking with his
brothers and Oahu Sam's assignment as barber both happen on the Peacock's deck); on
**July 5, 1840**, when the Peacock anchored in Mbua Bay an hour before the Vincennes, he
was **transferred to the Vincennes** — "The naturalists were now ordered to return on
board the Vincennes, and the prisoner Vendovi was also transferred to her, and remained
on board of her until the expiration of the cruise." (*Narrative* v3 pp. 248–249, OCR
~12553.) Philbrick ch. 10 concurs: Peacock → Vincennes, with harsher confinement under
Wilkes (fact attributed, no wording). · E ⚑ Dignity-critical follow-up (quarters, mess,
occupations aboard the Vincennes) still open — this settles only the vessel.

## Part II — Wilkes, *Narrative* vol. 3: the Malolo extraction (pp. 281–301, OCR 13940–14956)

### The frame Wilkes builds

- **Grief, retribution, and self-defense in one paragraph run** (pp. 281–282): "One of
  the victims was my own near relation, confided to my care by a widowed mother; I had
  therefore more than the ordinary degree of sorrow… to oppress me." Then: "The blood of
  the slain imperatively called for retribution, and the honour of our flag demanded that
  the outrage upon it should not remain unpunished." Then: "it became my task to restrain
  the desire of revenge within the bounds of prudent action… as it became afterwards my
  endeavour to prevent a just and salutary punishment from becoming a vindictive and
  indiscriminate massacre." The mourner, the avenger, and the defendant in one voice.
  OCR 13946–13993 · V⚑
- ⚑ **The "entirely unprovoked" coexistence.** July 24 trigger sequence (pp. 285–288,
  OCR 14165–14283): a native hostage detained in Alden's boat jumped overboard; Alden
  leveled his musket (hostage slackened, kept going), Midshipman Clark was directed to
  fire over his head; on shore the old chief "immediately cried out that his son was
  killed, and ordered the natives to make fight." Wilkes nonetheless concludes: "this
  outrage was entirely unprovoked, I had no hesitation in determining to inflict the
  punishment it merited, and this, not by the burning of the towns alone, but in the
  blood of the plotters and actors in the massacre" (p. 288, OCR 14282–14285). **His own
  narrative supplies the provocation chain he denies — both readings survive on the
  page** (rule 11). · E⚑
- **Underwood's missing rifles** (p. 284, OCR 14094–14103): seven of ten rifles left
  aboard the brig "under the idea that it would lighten the boat"; natives helping drag
  the grounded Leopard over the reef "got a knowledge of the feebleness of the armament."
  Wilkes flags this "to my surprise" — evidence in his case against the dead officer. · E

### The deaths (pp. 286–287)

- **Wilkes Henry:** called by Underwood to help cover the retreat, answered that "he had
  just received a blow from the club of a native, and would first have a crack at him"
  (p. 286, OCR 14198–14199), pursued the native, cut him down with his bowie-knife
  pistol, and at the water's edge was struck on the back of the head with a short club
  just as he fired and shot a native; fell face-down in the water, stripped. Dunnock puts
  him knee-deep in water, "knocked… down lifeless." OCR 14196–14222 · E/V
- **Underwood:** ordered the men to keep together and retreat facing the natives, struck
  one attacker with his pistol butt, took a spear-wound, fired, ordered his men to fire,
  and after discharging his second pistol was knocked down by a club. Found on the beach
  "partly stripped." pp. 286–287, OCR 14192–14251 · E
- ⚑ **The despatched wounded man** (p. 287, OCR 14250–14252): "On the beach lay
  Lieutenant Underwood, partly stripped, and Midshipman Henry, quite naked, with a native
  close by the latter, badly wounded, who was at once despatched." A summary killing of a
  wounded man, stated without comment in Wilkes' own narrative. · E⚑

### Burial on Henry Island (pp. 288–289, OCR 14287–14339)

Common grave on a sand island ~10 miles from Malolo: "The bodies were now placed in my
gig, side by side, wrapped in their country's flag" (OCR 14301–14303); twenty sailors all
in white; grave "deep in the pure white sand" under a dense ficus grove; Agate read the
service; three volleys; then all traces erased, decoy digging at false spots, leaves
scattered — against exhumation by "condor-eyed savages." The memorial fixed in names
instead: "…to fix a more enduring mark on that place, by naming the island after my
nephew, 'Henry,' and the pretty cluster of which it forms one, 'Underwood Group'"
(OCR 14329–14334). · E/S

### The reprisal (July 26)

- ⚑ **Sualib and Arro — village names settled (PROJECT_NOTES §3 soft spot):** "Upon the
  island there are two towns, Sualib and Arro. The former was on the southwest side, and
  the residence of the principal actors in the massacre. Upon this I intended to inflict
  the heaviest blow." (pp. 289–290, OCR 14351–14357.) Arro's people had fired on the
  boats from the mangroves; Wilkes "determined to burn [it] to the ground."
  (Modern identification: Sualib = Solevu, Arro = Yaro — see
  `research/encountered-world.md`.) · E⚑
- **Orders:** landing force of seventy in three divisions, armed with port-fires and
  rockets ("fiery spirits"): "The party had orders after landing, to move upon Sualib,
  destroying all the plantations they should meet on their way, sparing none except women
  and children." (p. 291, OCR 14404–14413; orders text in Appendix XIII.) · E
- ⚑ **Canoe capture — excited fire, quarter ordered** (p. 291, OCR 14437–14454):
  Emmons's crew, "so much excited," fired several muskets into two intercepted Malolo
  canoes before Wilkes came near enough "to order the firing to cease, and quarter to be
  given." Among swimmers picked up: an Arro chief, a woman, a girl, and an infant; Wilkes
  freed the last three, "telling them we did not war against women and children."
  Both-ways Wilkes: the restrainer and the commander whose operation shoots first. · E⚑
- **Sualib's defenses** (p. 293, OCR 14531–14584): ditch twelve feet wide full of mud and
  water; palisade of coconut trunks with living trees among them; ten-foot wicker fence
  impossible to see through; fresh inner ditch with four-foot earthen parapet; loopholes;
  gate built like a fish-weir; defenders shouting "lako-mai (come on!)"; it "had hitherto
  withstood every attack made by Feejee warriors." · E/S
- **The burning** (p. 294, OCR 14575–14602): after ~15 minutes' resistance a rocket
  struck a thatched roof — "a native sprung up to tear it off, but that moment was his
  last." Sensation core: "The shouts of men were intermingled with the cries and shrieks
  of the women and children, the roaring of the fire, the bursting of the bamboos, and an
  occasional volley of musketry." Heat drove the divisions to a coconut grove; in an hour
  the town was ashes. · E/S
- ⚑ **The child in the ashes** (pp. 294–295, OCR 14569–14616): "Only four bodies were
  found, among whom was that of a child, which had been seen during the conflagration
  apparently deserted, and in a state of danger, from which our men would gladly have
  relieved it, had it been possible." Underwood's cap picked up in the ruins. Women and
  children "as actively engaged as the men" in the defense; bows and arrows "for the most
  part used by the women." · E⚑
- **Expedition casualties** (p. 295, OCR 14618–14626): one man's jacket torn by a ball;
  several arrow wounds; only Samuel Stretch, quarter-gunner, wounded "so severely as to
  cause any solicitude" — Maury's division returned by the beach for his sake. Named
  enlisted man for the Class-2 roster. · E
- **Emmons's canoe battle** (pp. 295–296, OCR 14653–14670): five canoes, ~eight warriors
  each with shot-breastworks, advanced together on Emmons's seven-man boat crew. "After a
  short but severe contest, only one of the canoes escaped; the others were all
  destroyed, together with their warriors" — roughly 25 dead if the acknowledged count
  includes them. Emmons reached the brig with three prizes just before midnight. · E
- **Fijian musket lore** (pp. 285, 293–294): defenders had muskets but loaded them
  "according to the size of the person they intended to shoot at… a larger load to kill
  a large man"; dodging shots at the flash of a gun — the same skill the escaping hostage
  used ("looking over his shoulder, so as to dodge at the flash if fired at"). · S

### The surrender (July 27–29)

- ⚑ **The white cock refused** (p. 296, OCR 14672–14684): at low water the men withdrew
  leaving a young woman alone on the bare reef holding a white cock — the peace emblem —
  with Underwood's and Henry's belongings arranged beside her. Wilkes took the articles
  and refused the bird: "I declined the pacific offering, because I had no idea of making
  peace with them until it should be sued for after their own fashion." · E/S/V⚑
- ⚑ **The extermination ultimatum** (p. 297, OCR 14709–14737): Wilkes told the captive
  Arro chief that if the two boy messengers (aged ~15 and 17, carrying two reeds) failed
  to return at the appointed time, "I should shoot him." The message: "the whole of the
  natives of the island should come to me by the time the sun was overhead, to beg pardon
  and sue for mercy; and that if they did not do so, they must expect to be exterminated."
  "His eyes grew very large, he hesitated, and then spoke very quickly." · E⚑
- **Casuarina vigil — grounding** (pp. 297–298, OCR 14739–14767): six-hour wait (ten till
  four) on the eastern knoll; the island "but a few hours before… one of the loveliest
  spots in creation, was now entirely laid waste"; "A gentle breeze, which was blowing
  through the casuarina trees, gave out the moaning sound that is uttered by the pines of
  our own country, producing a feeling of depression inseparable from the occasion." · S
- ⚑ **The crawl** (p. 298, OCR 14769–14802): "they wound upward, and, in a short time,
  about forty men appeared, crouching on their hands and knees, and occasionally stopping
  to utter piteous moans and wailings." At thirty feet an old man begged pardon: "He
  said, that they acknowledged themselves conquered, and that the island belonged to us;
  that they were our slaves, and would do whatever I desired; that they had lost every
  thing; that the two great chiefs of the island, and all their best warriors, had been
  killed, all their provisions destroyed, and their houses burned." They confessed the
  officers "had done them no harm… killed without the slightest cause" and blamed
  Sualib's dead. All kept heads to the ground while the old man spoke. · E/S⚑
- ⚑ **Death count — Wilkes' own number, RANGE CHECK** (p. 298, OCR 14786–14789): "They
  acknowledged a loss of fifty-seven killed." Wilkes is "rather inclined to believe" the
  ~25 killed in Emmons's canoe fight were included, since later accounts "give the same
  number." So Wilkes' own arithmetic runs **57 (inclusive) to ~82 (if separate)** — sits
  below/overlaps the base's stated **74–104** range. ⚑ Flag for reconciliation; the
  base's range must not be silently narrowed to the defendant's count. Cross-check the
  74–104 sources (Stanton/Philbrick apparatus) before touching act_5 ch. 27. · E⚑
- ⚑ **The offered daughters** (pp. 298–299, OCR 14791–14821): "they had brought two of
  the chief's daughters as a present for the great chief. During the whole time that the
  old man was speaking, they all remained bent down with their heads to the ground."
  Wilkes refused, telling them "we never fought against women or children, and never
  received any gifts or presents" — the terrible clause the base withheld, now sourced on
  the page with his pardon speech ("they might consider themselves fortunate that we did
  not exterminate them"). · E⚑
- ⚑ **The water-carrying terms** (pp. 299–300, OCR 14823–14863): come unarmed to Arro,
  restore all articles, bring provisions, and carry water — "according to their customs,
  that the conquered should do work for the victors." On the 28th about seventy natives
  toiled until nearly sunset filling ~3,000 gallons, plus twelve pigs, yams, ~3,000
  coconuts. "They found this very hard work, and often expressed themselves to the
  interpreters… that it would have been as well for them to have been killed in battle
  as to die of hard work." · E/V⚑
- **The relics returned** (p. 300, OCR 14865–14867): "Among the articles restored, was
  the silver watch of Lieutenant Underwood, almost entirely melted up, and a piece of the
  eye-glass of Midshipman Henry." Grounding objects — the fire wrote itself into the
  watch. · E/S
- ⚑ **Wilkes' closing apologia — both readings** (pp. 300–301, OCR 14896–14951): he
  blames the dead and Alden — had boat-duty precautions been followed "this misfortune
  would not have occurred"; "no absolute necessity for obtaining provisions," Underwood
  ashore an hour and a half "chaffering for two or three pigs." He concedes: "I am well
  aware, that all the officers and men present were not at the time satisfied with the
  punishment inflicted. Many of them even thought that all in any way concerned in the
  murder ought to have been put to death." And rules on himself: "But I felt then as I do
  now, that the punishment was sufficient and effectual, while it was accompanied, as far
  as it could be, with mercy. Some, no doubt, will look upon it as unnecessarily
  severe." · E/V⚑

## Part III — Vendovi at Rewa (pp. 143–145, OCR 7707–7830)

- ⚑ **The seizure** (p. 143, OCR 7726–7735): Ngaraningiou went straight to Vendovi's
  house — "Going in, he took his seat by him, laid his hand on his arm, and told him that
  he was wanted, and that the king had sent for him to go on board the man-of-war. He
  immediately assented, and was preparing to come at once, but Ngaraningiou said, 'Not
  till to-morrow.' They passed the evening and night together, and in the morning
  embarked to come on board." Wilkes: the capture "shows the force of the customs to
  which all ranks of this people give implicit obedience." Ngaraningiou was Vendovi's
  known enemy and rival. · E/V⚑
- **Confession; Hudson's threat** (p. 143, OCR 7737–7751): examined in the Peacock's
  cabin before king, chiefs, and officers, Vendovi "acknowledged his guilt in causing the
  murder of part of the crew of the Charles Doggett, and admitted that he had held the
  mate by the arms while the natives killed him with clubs." Hudson said he "would have
  thought it incumbent upon him to burn Rewa, if Vendovi had not been taken." Then put in
  irons. · E
- ⚑ **The leave-taking tableau — dignity-critical** (pp. 143–144, OCR 7776–7784): "Kania,
  the king, seated himself on the right side of Vendovi, taking hold of his arm, while
  Navumialu placed himself on the left. Phillips walked up and down in front. All shed
  tears, and sobbed aloud while conversing in broken sentences with their brother. The
  natives shed tears also, and none but Ngaraningiou remained unmoved." Then: "The king
  kissed the prisoner's forehead, touched noses, and turned away. The inferior chiefs
  approached and kissed his hands, whilst the common people crawled up to him and kissed
  his feet." Primary source for the farewell scene (grounding debt #1). · E/S⚑
- ⚑ **The manacled blessing** (p. 144, OCR 7784–7790): a young man of Vendovi's
  household, last to quit him, wished to remain but was refused; "he embraced his knees,
  kissed his hands and feet, and received a parting blessing from Vendovi, who placed
  both his manacled hands on his head. The young man then retreated backwards towards the
  ladder, sighing and sobbing as though his heart would break." The single most
  novel-ready image in the sequence — Vendovi in irons still able to bless. · E/S/V⚑
- **Phillips' grief — voice seed** (pp. 143–144, OCR 7758–7764): Phillips, whose brother
  Vendovi helped murder, was "the most dejected of all," explaining: "That as long as
  Seru lived he could be saucy, but after his death he was all alone, just like a stick."
  Wilkes reads it as characteristic Fijian "inconsistency"; the novel can read it as
  grief that ignores the ledger. · V
- ⚑ **Oahu Sam — the connective thread** (pp. 144–145, OCR 7791–7830; p. 283, OCR
  14071–14073): the king's last request to Hudson — that his barber, Oahu Sam (a Sandwich
  Islander, ex-whaleman with some English), accompany Vendovi. Received aboard "as
  Vendovi's barber" — and this same Oahu Sam is the **interpreter on the beach at Malolo**
  when the fatal invitation to the town is delivered, and in Emmons's boat at the canoe
  capture (p. 291). The prisoner's barber threads the two tragedies together. · E⚑
- **Cargill's visit and endorsement** (pp. 144–145, OCR 7797–7820): Mr. Cargill the
  missionary came aboard shortly after the royal party left, reported no disturbance
  ashore; weeks later he told Wilkes the chiefs "were fully sensible that it was just
  that Vendovi should be punished" and praised Hudson's conduct — the mission's blessing
  on the seizure, in Wilkes' self-vindicating retelling. (Missionary-side account:
  `research/encountered-world.md`.) · E
- **Vendovi portrait confirmed in situ** (p. 143 plate; OCR 1207, 7715–7721): list of
  illustrations — "VENDOVI. Drawn by A. T. Agate. Engraved by J. W. Paradise, 143";
  Agate's portrait faces the leave-taking page. · E

## Part IV — "Imprudence of Lieutenant Underwood" (pp. 155–156, OCR 8201–8263)

- ⚑ **Moala** (p. 155, OCR 8201–8218): on Lt. Carr's tender cruise, Underwood "was very
  near involving them in difficulties": seeking hogs and yams at Moala, expressly ordered
  by Carr not to land, he was "unprepared to meet an attack" when natives massed and
  turned hostile; the boats were saved by the beachcomber Charley's warning; when arms
  were displayed "the natives took to their heels." Wilkes' set-up chapter: Underwood
  established as careless before Malolo — the defendant's-memoir framing of the man he
  would bury. · E⚑
- **Charley's warning — voice seed** (p. 155, OCR 8215–8222): "You had better stand to
  your arms, gentlemen; they are after mischief." Charley also reported these islanders
  had recently seized and ransomed a trader's boat crew. · V
- ⚑ **Wilkes' retrospective on boat-duty carelessness** (p. 155, OCR 8222–8230):
  immediately after Moala he generalizes — officers' "over-confidence and carelessness…
  needlessly put in jeopardy the lives of the men entrusted to them. It is now, on
  looking back, a wonder to me that we escaped accident so long as we did, and certainly
  not extraordinary that one did at last happen." Written after Malolo, placed before it
  in the book — the *Narrative* building his defense in advance of the event. · V⚑
- **Second Underwood near-miss — Moturiki** (p. 156, OCR 8248–8263): per Passed
  Midshipman May's account, Underwood's party "imprudently landed" overnight on Moturiki,
  unloading boats into the mbure despite signs of mischief; natives caught secretly
  passing clubs behind a log; Underwood expelled them and the party finished the night
  anchored in the boats. A second at-fault anecdote stacked against Underwood before
  Malolo. · E

1. **Reynolds skylarking with Fuegian youths + same-day retraction** — act_2.md line 140
   (Ch. 15, The Launch). Verbatim from the file: "a party of young Fuegian men and boys,
   six to perhaps twenty-five years old, spent a day with the boat's crew that Reynolds
   recorded with unguarded delight: 'They were lively, and we skylarked among the snow
   together, as if we had been old friends. They were naked and we warmly clad, and I
   just thought that we presented as wide a contrast of person and habit as could be met
   with anywhere in the world.' They were mimics of genius, repeating English 'with
   singular exactness and joining in the songs and dances of the men with a perfect
   observance of note and time.'" The retraction, same line: "And then, warmed by the
   finest day of cross-cultural play in the whole four years, he sat down and reasoned
   himself back into his century, concluding that he was thankful to belong to 'a more
   artificial community,' and would 'waive forever' the notion that those with the fewest
   wants led the more enviable life. Read the whole passage when you build this scene:
   the day argues one thing, the summary argues another, and the space between them is
   the nineteenth century, caught in the act." Attribution: Reynolds' private journal
   (named in-text). Note: the file says "And then… he sat down," implying same day, not
   literally "same-night"; no page number given in-text.

5. **Rio Negro "pre-frightened" passage** — act_2.md line 92 (Ch. 12, Rio Negro),
   verbatim in full: "Where great ships meet small settlements, both sides arrive
   pre-frightened. Each has heard what the other is capable of; everyone's prudence looks
   like everyone else's aggression; and once the first retaliation lands, escalation does
   the rest. At the Río Negro the crossed signals cost nothing but an evening's terror,

8. **The skull in the Patent Office / "every voyage of discovery comes home as a
   museum"** — act_5.md line 80 (Ch. 30), verbatim: "And the day after the anchors went
   down, the voyage supplied its own epitaph. Vendovi — two years a prisoner-passenger,
   the man carried ten thousand miles to be improved by the sight of America — had
   sickened on the last passage, and on June 11, 1842, having lived just long enough to
   raise the country that took him, he died in the naval hospital at New York. The
   expedition kept his skull. It went into the collections, and the collections went on
   display at the Patent Office in Washington, where for years the visiting public could
   file past the assembled wonders of the great national voyage — the corals, the war
   clubs, the bird skins, and the labeled skull of the last king's brother the *Peacock*
   ever arrested. Every voyage of discovery comes home as a museum. It is worth one hard
   look at what, exactly, this one exhibited." Authorial voice; June 11, 1842 and the
   Patent Office display are corroborated (and the skull's afterlife now settled) in

9. **The Tarawa passage** — two linked passages. act_5.md line 62 (Ch. 29), verbatim:
   "And in the same weeks, between the fires, the boats did the work the fires keep
   upstaging: through April 1841 the *Peacock*'s surveyors charted the Gilbert chain
   island by island — Tabiteuea, Nonouti, Abemama, Maiana, Butaritari, Makin — and among
   them a middling atoll called Tarawa, sounded and triangulated and inked like all the
   rest, filed with two hundred sisters in the expedition's portfolio. Remember the name.
   Of every line the Ex. Ex. drew, that chart has the longest and heaviest afterlife, and
   this book will meet it again in its final pages, in November of 1943." Payoff at
   act_5.md line 92 (Ch. 31), verbatim core: "In 1943, planners preparing the amphibious
   assault on a Gilbert Islands atoll found that the best survey of Tarawa in Allied
   hands was still, 'with only slight modifications,' the one the *Peacock*'s boats had
   drawn in April 1841… on the twentieth of November 1943 the guess failed, the tide sat
   down on the reef, and the Marines of the 2nd Division waded eight hundred yards into
   machine-gun fire past the grounded landing craft. The Ex. Ex.'s legacy, whole, in a
   single morning: the chart that was still the best on Earth, and the cost of every

10. **Charlie and the slave brig at Rio** — act_2.md line 60 (Ch. 11, Rio), verbatim
    core: "Charlie went aboard one, a captured brig under British guard, and was on deck
    when they opened the hold. 'Such a cloud of steam and such horrible smell issued that
    it staggered everybody on deck,' he wrote. 'They found only thirty living human
    beings out of three hundred. This was only one of the many horrors of the African
    slave-trade.' Thirty of three hundred. Charlie — a boy whose own back had been opened
    by lawful authority — wrote it down plainly and did not look away, which is more than
    can be said for most of the wardroom; Reynolds, sighting a slaver at sea alongside
    the *Peacock*, spent his ink that day on the American ship's clumsy appearance. What
    the century found remarkable, and what it didn't, is its own kind of record, and this
    voyage keeps both ledgers." Attribution: "he wrote" — Erskine's memoir (PD); the
    Reynolds contrast attributed to his journal by name. No page number in-text — the
    Erskine read-through should pin the page.
