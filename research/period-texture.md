## 1. The world while they sailed (what the newspapers they never got were saying)

- **They sailed out of a depression.** The Panic of 1837 broke the economy the year before
  Norfolk; the slump ran to ~1843 — the whole voyage happened inside it. The Navy's
  economies (the unstrengthened hulls, the withheld promotions, the 100-copy publication)
  have a macro-climate, and the sailors' families back home were living it. → serves: Act I
  false-economy theme; the homecoming's indifference.
- **They left under Van Buren and came home to the third president of their voyage.** The
  log-cabin circus of 1840 (Harrison, "Tippecanoe") happened while they were in Fiji and
  Honolulu — the wildest election of the era, and no sailor aboard could vote in it.
  Harrison died April 4, 1841, a month in office; the news would have chased them across
  the Pacific for months. Tyler — "His Accidency" — received the squadron's results.
  → serves: the base's "two administrations gone"; knowledge-at-the-speed-of-a-ship.
- **The Trail of Tears was happening as they weighed anchor** — the Cherokee removal's main
  marches ran 1838–39. The republic charted the Pacific with one hand that year. → serves:
  ledger context; use with care, narrator's own register only.
- **The *Amistad*** — seized off Long Island August 1839; Supreme Court decision March
  1841. The nation argued about a slave ship's mutiny while Charlie's squadron shared
  harbors with slavers. → serves: Rio slave-brig chapter's national echo.
- **The First Opium War (1839–42) ran exactly the voyage's span.** Britain shot its way
  into the China trade while the Ex. Ex. charted the ocean that trade would cross; the
  Treaty of Nanking came two months after the squadron's return. At Singapore and Manila
  the war was the harbor talk [VERIFY specifics of what they heard — Wilkes v5]. → serves:
  Act V homeward; the Sulu diplomacy's backdrop.
- **Britain's imperial winter:** the Afghan catastrophe — the retreat from Kabul, January
  1842, the army of the Indus destroyed — was fresh horror in the papers as the squadron
  came home. Victoria had been crowned seven weeks before Norfolk (June 28, 1838) and
  married while they were in the ice (February 10, 1840). → serves: the British-navy
  presence at Rio/Sydney; period mood.
- **Technology was racing them home.** The *Great Western* began steam crossings of the
  Atlantic the very year they sailed; Cunard's scheduled steam mail started 1840; American
  railroads roughly tripled during the voyage (~3,000 miles by early 1840s); Morse's
  Washington–Baltimore telegraph line opened in 1844, two years after their return. **The
  world that launched the last all-sail squadron dismantled sail-speed knowledge before the
  charts were even published.** → serves: the "knowledge traveled at the speed of a ship"
  throughline has an expiration date arriving in-frame; coda material.
- **Photography arrived while they were at sea.** Daguerre's process was announced in Paris
  in August 1839 — months *after* Reynolds stood on Corcovado with nothing but a sentence
  to keep it. The base's "the sentence was the camera" is literally periodizable: theirs
  was the last voyage of the pre-photographic eye. → serves: the ch. 11 essay; wonder-aside
  fuel, already half-planted.
- **Science was being named around them.** The word "scientist" was coined by Whewell in
  the 1830s and was still a novelty in 1840 — the crews' "scientifics" was the sound of a
  profession before its name settled. Bessel measured the first stellar parallax (61 Cygni)
  in **1838** — the year they sailed, humanity first measured the distance to a star.
  Darwin's *Voyage of the Beagle* was published 1839 (at sea with them, in effect); his
  coral-reef monograph came 1842 — with Dana's shipboard evidence arriving like a second
  witness. → serves: Act III atolls; the scientifics' world.
- **Their literary shadow was being written in real time.** R. H. Dana's *Two Years Before
  the Mast* was published in 1840, mid-voyage — the book Erskine's title answers across
  fifty years (*Twenty* Years — the fo'c'sle's rejoinder to the Harvard man's two). Melville
  shipped on the whaler *Acushnet* in January 1841, deserted into the Marquesas in 1842 —
  the Pacific literary generation formed in the squadron's wake. And **Dickens toured
  America January–June 1842, departing New York days before the *Vincennes* anchored there
  [VERIFY exact dates — he sailed early June; the squadron arrived June 10].** The country
  that mobbed Boz gave its returning explorers nothing. → serves: the homecoming's
  bitterness, with a name on it.
- **Six months after the return, the Navy hanged boys for talking mutiny.** The *Somers*
  affair (December 1842): a midshipman — the Secretary of War's son — and two sailors
  executed at sea on suspicion of conspiracy. The service that gave Wilkes a reprimand for
  the lash was about to show what it did when it truly feared its crews. → serves: trial
  act context; the discipline climate that made Honolulu legible to the 1842 court.

## 2. Common issues: the sailor's standing miseries

- **Sleep, or the lack of it.** Watch-and-watch = never more than four hours down, cut by
  "all hands" at any moment; add the dog watches' rotation and no man's clock was ever
  settled. → serves: everywhere; see modern-lens §sleep.
- **Wet.** No drying room, no spare everything; wool dried on the body or not at all; in
  high latitudes bedding stayed damp for weeks. → serves: Acts II/IV grounding family A.
- **Food monotony and food fraud.** Salt junk from the harness cask, biscuit and its
  weevils, the purser's markup on slops and tobacco ("purser's pound") — pursers were
  institutionally hated [W]. Fresh provisions at ports were events of real joy. → serves:
  mess dailiness debt #5.
- **Money.** A seaman's pay ran roughly $10–12/month [VERIFY Ex. Ex. rates], often part-
  allotted home, settled fully only at cruise's end — four years of wages riding on
  surviving the cruise. → serves: re-enlistment crisis at Honolulu (what refusing to
  re-ship actually risked).
- **The body's tariff.** "Ruptures" (hernias) from hauling — common enough that trusses
  were standard issue [VERIFY issue practice]; hearing dulled by gunnery; teeth lost;
  rheumatism as a career certainty. The base's Act IV sick-list is the norm intensified,
  not an exception. → serves: grounding family A.
- **Mail famine.** Letters moved only by passing ships and consuls; news from home arrived
  six to twelve months old or never; men learned of deaths and births a year late.
  Reynolds' "our hearts were full of gladness" at a homeward brig is the emotional economy
  of the whole age. → serves: already planted; deepen at ports.
- **Career stagnation (officers' version).** No retirement system, promotion strictly by
  seniority, no war to thin the list: passed midshipmen waited many years for lieutenant.
  Reynolds at 23 was on a decade-plus track — which is why acting appointments (grantable,
  revocable) were such perfect instruments of control. → serves: Braid 1/2 mechanics; the
  wardroom's combustibility.

## 3. What made them happy

- **Mail, above everything.** Then: **grog** (the USN spirit ration, a gill a day in
  halves [VERIFY exact issue]; "splice the main brace" for celebrations — Erskine records
  exactly that on Washington's birthday in the ice). **Tobacco.** **Fresh food** — the
  smell of oranges coming off a bumboat after months of salt junk. → serves: family A/B
  anchors.
- **Music and dancing.** Forecastle fiddles, dog-watch dancing, singing between ships (the
  Rio serenade war in the base). **Yarning** — storytelling as the fo'c'sle's currency.
  **Skylarking** — Reynolds' own word, at play with the Fuegians. **Crossing-the-line and
  holiday rituals** [VERIFY Ex. Ex. Neptune ceremony — check Colvocoresses/Clark].
  → serves: the joy register the base under-uses; Act IV's iceberg play (Erskine) is its
  polar form.
- **Sunday.** Divine service, best clothes, and the afternoon's "make and mend" — the one
  slack tide in the week. → serves: clock family C.
- **Shore.** Liberty in a Pacific port: dance houses, drink, curiosities, tattoos, women —
  handled per the voice and dignity rules, but not erased; it was most of what a sailor
  saved his happiness for. → serves: Tahiti/Honolulu texture; the desertion math.
- **Pride of ship and station.** Racing any sail in sight; the topman's swagger ("the
  never-sweats"); a taut ship cheered *herself* (the *Peacock*'s people cheering her out of
  the ice). → serves: the cheer beats already in the base.

## 4. What made them mad

- **Injustice, not severity.** The fo'c'sle's moral economy accepted brutal punishment *if
  earned*; what it never forgave was unfairness — punishment without hearing, favoritism,
  broken custom. (Charlie's psychology, generalized: "punished as a liar… for an accident,
  without a hearing" is the precise formula.) → serves: everything Wilkes.
- **Broken promises about pay, grog, liberty, and discharge dates** — Honolulu is this
  list, item by item. **Withheld shore leave.** **Make-work harassment** ("working up").
- **Purser's tricks** (short weight, high prices). **Officers' contempt** — being "talked
  to like a dog" mattered more than the rope's end.
- **Insults to the ship** — another crew's sneer, a snubbed salute. The *Independence*
  affair read below decks as an insult to *them*, not only to Wilkes [inference — verify
  against Erskine/Clark]. → serves: Braid 1's fo'c'sle shadow.
- **Being kept in the dark.** The base has this ("destinations doled out a leg at a
  time") — secrecy from above was read as distrust, and answered with rumor. → serves:
  the fo'c'sle telegraph; modern-lens §rumor.

## 5. Normal then, crazy now

- **Children at sea.** Boys of 8–14 as servants and powder monkeys; Charlie flogged at
  fifteen was *ordinary discipline*, and the base is right that the ordinariness is the
  horror.
- **Flogging as public liturgy** — all hands mustered, sentences read, counts called; the
  Ward/Riley/Sweeny order (Wilkes harvest) shows the full theater: gallows-rigged launch
  touring the fleet.
- **Medicine.** No anesthesia (ether's first demonstration: 1846, four years after the
  return); surgery's virtue was *speed*; bleeding, blistering, and calomel — mercury — as
  standard doses. A "healthy" sailor lived with pain levels we'd hospitalize.
- **Everyone drank, daily, by regulation.** The temperance movement was radical fringe;
  the Navy *issued* liquor twice a day and stopping a man's grog was a *punishment*.
- **Hygiene.** Fresh water was for drinking; washing was salt water or nothing; four
  hundred men, fourteen inches per hammock [VERIFY spacing], one open-air head at the bow.
- **Race, afloat vs. ashore.** The antebellum Navy was one of America's few integrated
  workplaces — Black sailors served in the same watches, messes, and tops, on the same
  pay, while ashore most states enforced slavery or exclusion; a Navy circular of 1839
  capped Black enlistment at five percent [VERIFY circular details]. All officers were
  white. This is David Smith's world exactly: present everywhere, permitted a phrase.
  → serves: cast Class-2 context; handle with the dignity mechanics.
- **Superstition as working knowledge.** Flying Dutchman sightings (Erskine logs one),
  corposants, Jonahs, whistling for wind, caul charms against drowning — and many sailors
  never learned to swim [W — attested attitude, partly fatalism]. The wise held science
  and omen in the same hand — the base's "informed enough to marvel" line is the
  period's epistemology.
- **Dueling** among officers: declining but alive; a midshipman's honor culture ran on it.
  → serves: wardroom stakes calibration.

## 6. What they wore, and why

- **The enlisted rig (functional head to toe):** flat-brimmed tarred **tarpaulin hat**
  (waterproof; doubles as a satchel — Charlie's letters); **black silk neckerchief**
  (sweat-rag, tourniquet, mourning lore); **checked or white frock**; **short blue
  roundabout jacket** — short *so it cannot foul rigging*; **wide bell-bottom trousers** —
  wide *to roll above the knee* for holystoning and wading, easy to kick off in the water;
  **barefoot in warm weather** for grip aloft; sheath knife on a lanyard. The first full
  USN *enlisted* uniform regulations arrived **1841 — mid-voyage** [VERIFY]; before that,
  purser's slops enforced rough uniformity. → serves: family A/B; any muster scene.
- **Cold-weather issue (Ex. Ex. specific, [E] in base):** fur suits, the new vulcanized
  India-rubber jackets and trousers, sea boots; below the sixties men wore everything they
  owned at once. Officers: pea coats of pilot cloth, fearnought wool [W].
- **Officers' dress as rank made visible.** Undress vs. full dress, epaulettes, the cocked
  hat — *dress was the org chart*, which is exactly why Wilkes' unissued captain's coat
  and broad pennant were instantly legible to every man aboard as a claim, and why the
  Navy's first substantive communication at homecoming was about the pennant. → serves:
  Braid 1's costume logic.

## 7. Social life: how 400 men actually related

- **The mess was the family** — eight to twelve men who chose each other, ate from one
  kid, rotated the cooking; "messmate before shipmate, shipmate before stranger" [W
  proverb]. Being turned out of a mess was social death. → serves: below-decks scenes'
  basic unit; Erskine p. 17 gives the *Vincennes*' messes as [E].
- **Tribes within the tribe:** starbowlines vs. larbowlines (the two watches); topmen
  over waisters and after-guard in prestige (Erskine's "forties, the never-sweats");
  idlers (carpenters, cooks, sailmakers) outside the watch rotation. Nicknames were near-
  universal (Jim Orange, Old Whittle — the base already collects them).
- **The quarterdeck and the fo'c'sle were separate nations** sharing a hull — different
  food, light, air, futures; a midshipman of sixteen could order a forty-year-old seaman
  seized up. The Ex. Ex. sharpened this: its officers were unusually young and its
  commander's legitimacy unusually thin.
- **The old salt as institution:** sea-daddies taught boys splices, manners, and survival;
  the yarn was the classroom. → serves: Charlie's education (who was *his* sea-daddy? —
  harvest question, Erskine early chapters).
- **Letters and literacy.** Literate sailors wrote letters for shipmates (paid in tobacco
  or grog); **Charlie could not write home at all in 1838** — the mother he nearly killed
  for could receive no word from him unless another hand held the pen [inference from
  [E] illiteracy; harvest question: does TYBTM say?]. → serves: the literacy arc's cost,
  from day one.
- **Religion aboard:** chaplain Elliott's services, Sunday muster, tract societies' reach;
  Dana's devoutness among the scientifics; and grog-shop profanity ten minutes after the
  benediction — both true, same men. → serves: voice calibration; Erskine's own pious
  1890 register vs. his fo'c'sle world.
