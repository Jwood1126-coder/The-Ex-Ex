## 2. ⚑ DIGNITY-CRITICAL — the skull: full provenance, and the repatriation

- **VERIFIED — death and taking.** Vendovi died in June 1842 at the naval hospital,
  Brooklyn, New York, within days of the squadron's arrival (arrival June 8–10, 1842;
  several accounts say he died the day after arrival — June 11, 1842 is the usual date;
  exact date not pinned to a primary in this pass — check Wilkes' *Narrative* vol. 5 or
  contemporary NY papers). His head was removed and, cleaned of flesh, went to
  Washington; **his headless body was buried in a naval cemetery in Brooklyn and was NOT
  part of the 2025 repatriation** (only the skull returned; one outlet's "body in
  Washington" claim contradicts the better-attested Brooklyn accounts). Sources: Fabian,
  *Commonplace* 8.2 (2008); Islands Business, Dec 11, 2025
  (https://islandsbusiness.com/news-break/captured-fijian-chief-heads-home-from-the-united-states/);
  Fiji Sun, Dec 10, 2025
  (https://fijisun.com.fj/living/culture/remains-of-rewan-chief-to-finally-be-brought-home-after-183-years).
  Period tone datum, secondhand: the New York Herald, June 1842, attributed his death to
  "consumption in consequence probably of having no human flesh to eat" (as quoted in
  Fabian — verify against the original Herald issue before print use).
- **VERIFIED (path documented) / LEAD (specific accession).** Per Fabian: displayed in
  **case 37 of the Great Hall of the U.S. Patent Office** (the National Institute's
  "national cabinet"); later **specimen 292** in the Army Medical Museum's List of
  Specimens by 1880 (Fijian press consistently cites the "Specimen 292" tag). The AMM
  transferred its crania to the Smithsonian (2,206 skulls May 1898; a second transfer
  January 1904, feeding the new Division of Physical Anthropology under Hrdlička) —
  that Vendovi's skull specifically traveled in those transfers is inference (Fabian
  places it at AMM in 1880; 2025 press places it at the Smithsonian); Adler 2014 or the
  NMNH Repatriation Office would confirm the specific accession. Sources:
  https://commonplace.online/article/one-mans-skull/ ;
  https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_sic_12577.
- **VERIFIED — REPATRIATED, December 13, 2025.** The skull was returned from the
  Smithsonian Institution to Fiji: flown home Saturday, December 13, 2025, accompanied by
  Fiji's Ambassador to the US, Ratu Ilisoni Vuidreketi (reported as a descendant),
  received by the Roko Tui Dreketi, Ro Teimumu Kepa, and **interred at the chiefly burial
  grounds, Lomanikoro Village, Rewa** (Fiji Times: "at Muaiqele in Lomanikoro," the
  sautabu; other reports: "Narusa" chiefly burial grounds — possibly the same site
  described differently). Planning reportedly began ~July 2025 between the Smithsonian,
  descendants, and the Fijian government. Multiple independent Fijian/Pacific outlets
  agree: Fiji Times (Dec 21, 2025,
  https://www.fijitimes.com.fj/discovering-fiji-ratu-veidovi-returns-home/); Fiji Sun
  (Dec 10, 2025); PMN News (Dec 15, 2025,
  https://pmn.co.nz/read/pacific-region/after-186-years-a-fijian-chief-comes-home);
  Islands Business (Dec 11, 2025); San Juan Preservation Trust (Mar 17, 2026,
  https://sjpt.org/a-long-journey-home/). **⚑ Any manuscript language treating the skull
  as still held in Washington is now out of date** (touches act_5 ch. 30–31 and the
  museum theme). NOT FOUND: a Smithsonian press release naming the specific unit (NMNH
  Repatriation Office near-certain).
- **VERIFIED — the name.** Modern Fijian usage (press, government, the repatriation
  ceremonies) renders him **"Ro Veidovi,"** fuller form "Ro Veidovi Logavatu" — "Ro"
  being the chiefly title used in Rewa (Burebasaga confederacy). "Vendovi" is the
  expedition-era anglicization (Fijian "d" is prenasalized /nd/ — see the Hale
  orthography section below, which documents the same phonology from 1846); Adler 2014
  keeps "Vendovi" only in quotation marks as the expedition's label. Recommended: Ro
  Veidovi (Logavatu), with "Vendovi" noted as the expedition's spelling. Sources:
  https://fijisun.com.fj/living/culture/ro-veidovis-remains-returned-interred-in-chiefly-burial-grounds-at-lomanikoro ;
  https://fijisociety.org/fijian-chief-ro-veidovis-remains-return-home-after-nearly-200-years/
- **Scholarship spine (LEADS, facts-only, never words):** Antony Adler, "The Capture and
  Curation of the Cannibal 'Vendovi': Reality and Representation of a Pacific Frontier,"
  *Journal of Pacific History* 49:3 (2014), 255–282, doi:10.1080/00223344.2014.914623
  (paywalled; citation confirmed via Ingenta) — the dedicated scholarly treatment of
  capture, portrait, and curation, and the counterweight on how the "cannibal Vendovi"
  image was constructed (the 2025 press still repeats the "convicted" framing). Ann
  Fabian, "One Man's Skull" (2008) and *The Skull Collectors* (Chicago, 2010).
- **Side note — Vendovi Island.** Wilkes named Vendovi Island in the San Juan Islands
  (Washington State) after the captive chief in 1841; now a San Juan Preservation Trust
  preserve (https://sjpt.org/how-an-island-in-the-san-juans-was-named-after-a-fijian-chief/).

## 3. The missionary seam — Cargill, Rewa, and the squadron (Calvert 1858, PD)

- **Documented negative:** "Memoirs of Mrs. Margaret Cargill" (David Cargill, 1841/1855)
  is NOT on Archive.org under any search tried; the only Cargill-Fiji items there are in
  copyright (Dickson 1976; Schutz 1977 diaries, lending-restricted). The memoir is real —
  Walter Lawry's PD 1852 volume cites "the Memoir of Mrs. Cargill, by her husband"
  (archive.org in.ernet.dli.2015.88877, OCR 230–236). Try HathiTrust or Google Books.
  **VERIFIED negative.**
- **Best PD substitute obtained:** James Calvert, *Fiji and the Fijians*, vol. 2
  ("Mission History," ed. G. S. Rowe, 1858, Cornell scan cu31924088426204; local copy
  scratchpad `sources/fiji_fijians_v2_calvert_1858_djvu.txt`, 994 KB) — full Rewa chapter
  (ch. V) built partly on Cargill-circle records, directly covering the American
  squadron, the Vendovi seizure, and Mrs. Cargill's death. **VERIFIED.**
- ⚑ **Vendovi seizure, missionary-side** (Calvert ch. V, p. 161, OCR 7699–7710): "About
  this time the 'Peacock,' United States exploring ship, in command of Captain Hudson,
  called at Eewa [Rewa — OCR], and took away Veindovi, the King's brother, who was the
  principal in the murder of **eight Americans in 1832**." ⚑ CONFLICT: Wilkes'
  *Narrative* dates the Charles Doggett killings to 1834 (and Erskine says eleven
  killed) — a range/discrepancy to state, not clean up (rule 5). And the mission's
  verdict on Hudson: "Captain Hudson spoke much to the King and Chiefs on the truth and
  importance of Christianity, and by his blameless conduct, and refusal to indulge the
  criminal licence which most foreigners had sought, exerted an influence on behalf of
  religion which has been most helpful to the Mission ever since." **VERIFIED (PD,
  quotable).**
- **Mrs. Cargill's death immediately after the squadron's visit** (Calvert ch. V,
  pp. 158–162, OCR 7560–7750): sequence at Rewa 1840 — early 1840 catastrophic
  storm/flood; April, Cargill delirious with "inflammation," near death; during his
  illness a riot of the king's assembled warriors sent musket balls onto the mission
  premises — "Poor Mrs. Cargill feared for the children, and placed one behind a chest of
  drawers filled with clothes, and the others behind the large posts of the house, to
  shelter them from the balls"; "about this time" the Peacock took Veindovi; Mrs. Cargill
  "broke down under the pressure of the recent trials and alarms," "died 'in the Lord' on
  the second of June, and on the next day was buried with her baby of five days old."
  Hunt sailed nearly 200 miles from Somosomo to console Cargill, who left with four
  children by schooner, reaching the Colonies Sept 2. Year 1840 implied by chapter
  chronology; corroborated by Waterhouse's visit "July, 1840" finding Hunt at Rewa
  (OCR 2119–2125). Her grave at the mission station **Zoar** was still visited in
  1851–52 by Calvert and the then-king. **VERIFIED (PD).** — The double-ledger gift: the
  Vendovi arrest and Mrs. Cargill's collapse share a season on the same riverbank.
- **Wilkes at Somosomo, the missionaries' record of him** (Calvert ch. II, pp. 39–40,
  OCR 2085–2117): early 1840 Wilkes offered the Hunt/Lyth families a vessel to relocate
  anywhere in Fiji and removal of their goods; they declined; the missionaries recorded
  "the great kindness of the United States officer." Calvert quotes Wilkes' own
  *Narrative*: "I know of no situation so trying as this for ladies to live in,
  particularly when pleasing and well-informed, as we found these at Somosomo." (Locate
  in Wilkes vol. III for the page cite.) **VERIFIED (PD) — rule 11 fuel.**
- **Rewa texture** (Calvert ch. V, pp. 152–163, OCR 7280–7790, 8580–8615): the Rewa king
  (Tui Dreketi) friendly to the lotu while remaining heathen — returned stolen mission
  cases, gave chapel ground and a whale's tooth, sent swimmers offering refuge in the
  flood, gave his storm-ruined yams to the missionaries: "The gods of Fiji are false and
  weak; and as they have not prevented the earth from being washed away from my yams, I
  will not present these yams to them, but present them to the ambassadors of the true
  God." Opposition led by his brother "Ratu Nggara-ni-nggio, ('cave of a shark,')" —
  i.e., Qaraniqio — who stoned Christians and had the mission fired on; Veindovi another
  of the King's brothers. Mission premises across the river from the town; station named
  Zoar. **VERIFIED (PD).**
- **Cargill biography anchors:** Lawry's roll of missionaries — "David Cargill, M.A.
  Removed to Feejee, 1835. Visited England, 1841. Re-appointed to Friendly Islands, 1842.
  Died at Vavau, April 25th, 1843." (Lawry 1852, OCR 20204–20206.) Calvert: Cross and
  Cargill appointed from Tonga, embarked Oct 8, 1835, reached Lakemba Oct 12; Cargill
  compiled the first Fijian vocabulary and grammar (Lakemba dialect) and revised the
  first press output (first Wesleyan Catechism printed March 1839; then Mark).
  **VERIFIED (PD).**
- **Orthography note:** Cargill and Cross fixed a Fijian alphabet at Tonga in 1835,
  revised it at Lakemba "after very full examination on the spot" into "the alphabet as
  it now stands" — ancestor of modern Fijian spelling (b=mb, c=th, q=ngg). The 1858 book
  itself prints names phonetically (Mbau, Thakombau, **Veindovi**, Ratu Nggara-ni-nggio,
  Lakemba) — so "Veindovi" is this tradition's spelling, a third variant beside
  Vendovi/Veidovi. **VERIFIED.**
- ⚑ OCR flags in the Cornell scan: "in 1849… removed from Lakemba to Rewa" and "reached
  Rewa in July, 1849" are almost certainly 1839 (chronology + Australian Dictionary of
  Biography); pervasive R→E OCR ("Eewa," "Eev."). Verify years against page images.
- **Bonus PD lead:** Mary Wallis, *Life in Feejee* (1851, archive.org
  `lifeinfeejee00wall`) — an American trader's wife in Fiji in the 1840s; likely
  Rewa/Bau texture and Vendovi aftermath. **LEAD, contents unchecked.**

## 4. Hale's *Ethnography and Philology* (1846, PD) — the expedition's own most dignity-compatible volume

Source: archive.org `Ethnographyphil00Hale` (backup `cihm_35669`); local copy scratchpad
`sources/hale_ethnography_1846.txt` (1.8 MB, 67,867 OCR lines). All VERIFIED from the PD
text unless marked.

- ⚑ **Veidovi as informant aboard.** Hale collected the Fijian great-flood tradition
  (Waluvu levu) **directly from Veidovi** — "the following account was given by Veindovi
  (the chief captured at Rewa), and confirmed from other sources" (pp. 54–55, OCR
  2944–2959). Veindovi also told Hale that Fijians formerly kept large canoes laid up
  against another flood. The captive chief serving as ethnographic source aboard the
  squadron; Hale treats him purely as an authority, with no comment on the capture —
  usable as characterization: the philologist who takes a flood myth from the prisoner
  and cites him like a library.
- **Method — named informants, graded reliability:** beachcomber James O'Connell (met
  1837; vocabulary "very scanty" though cultural knowledge "clear and satisfactory…
  since fully confirmed," p. 81, OCR 4205–4222); Rotuman chief Tui-Rotuma, "an elderly
  man… a petty chief, who had been two voyages in a whaler," met when natives came
  aboard at Tongatabu April 1840 (p. 104, OCR 5361–5370); honest failure admitted: "Of
  their religion I could obtain but little information… my informant, whose knowledge of
  English was limited to the most ordinary terms, could not explain" (pp. 105–106, OCR
  5428–5435).
- **Method — knowledge without guns (Fakaafo/Bowditch Island):** first contact all
  barter and language, no force — canoes came alongside chanting, "no inducements could
  prevail upon them to venture on board. Our interpreter was a Samoan native, whom we
  shipped at Oahu…" (pp. 149–150, OCR 8225–8243), with the field-note footnote that a
  slight dialect change confuses a native speaker while "a foreigner, who has a general
  smattering of one dialect, can usually accommodate himself without difficulty."
- **Method — sources for the Vitian grammar** (p. 363/365 area, OCR 26255–26282): five
  sources — Cargill's Lakemba grammar abstract; Hunt's Somusomu grammar; Cargill's
  dictionary revised by Hunt "at the request of Captain Wilkes, by whose care the copy
  which we possess was procured"; missionary gospel translations; and "a large
  collection of words and sentences, taken down from the pronunciation of the natives…
  principally at Ovolau, Rewa, Mbua, and Mathuata." Missionary manuscripts + Wilkes'
  institutional reach + Hale's own ear-work — a rare note of Wilkes actively aiding the
  philology.
- **Method — captive seamen as flawed sources** (Tobi section, OCR 33477–33497): "The
  situation in which the captive seamen were placed, was such as to deprive them of all
  desire of acquiring a better knowledge of the language of their inhuman masters, than
  was absolutely necessary" — then he still squeezes grammar from Horace Holden's
  word-list.
- **Fiji as a states-system** (pp. 58–60, OCR 3070–3200): three orders (turanga chiefs,
  matanivanua landholders — "the true owners of the land" — kai si commoners);
  independent chief cities; "**Mbau, Rewa, and Naitasiri, are the Sparta, Athens, and
  Thebes of Viti.**" The mbati protected-ally relation (compared to the Confederation of
  the Rhine and British India), the vasu nephew-right ("A vasu has nearly as much power
  in the state to which his mother belonged as in his own"), tau-vu ties that "lighten
  the evils of war." Tanoa's exile and restoration narrated, with a Wilkes footnote on
  Fijian statecraft (pp. 59–60, OCR 3129–3175); Tanoa's mother a daughter of a king of
  Rewa, making him vasu to Rewa.
- **The double ledger inside Hale** (pp. 57–58, OCR 3060–3072): he pivots from
  cannibalism material (footnoted to "the Rev. David Cargill's account") with "From
  these atrocities we willingly turn to a consideration of the system of civil polity
  which prevails in these islands" — atrocity and statecraft in one chapter, then the
  Sparta/Athens/Thebes praise. Structurally parallel to rule 6.
- **Hale's silence on Malolo is itself the datum.** NOT FOUND: Hale nowhere discusses
  the Malolo attack, the deaths of Underwood and Henry, or any of the expedition's armed
  conflicts. "Malolo" appears only as a geographic item in the Asaua (Yasawa) chain list
  (pp. 47–48, OCR 2572–2573) and as a vocabulary word; Veidovi's capture only in the
  flat parenthetical; Wilkes only on the title page and in two cooperative footnotes.
  (Grepped for Malolo/Wilkes/Underwood/Veindovi variants; "Vendovi" spelling absent —
  Hale uses "Veindovi.")
- **The migrations argument** (pp. 117–118, OCR 5977–6058): west-to-east peopling "may
  be deduced merely from an examination of the comparative grammar and vocabulary of the
  various dialects"; trade-winds objection demolished with the expedition's own weather
  data — twenty days wind-bound at the Navigator Islands by northwest winds, Feb 1840:
  "A canoe driven off from that group at this time, would, in all probability, have
  brought up on some one of the Society or Hervey Islands."
- **The Savaiki key-word** (p. 121, OCR 6155–6172): sound-shift table deriving all the
  Hawaiki names from one original (Savaiki → Savai'i, Havai'i, Hawaii, Avaiki, Havaiki,
  Hawaiki): "It will be found that this is, so to speak, the key-word, which unlocks the
  mystery of the Polynesian migrations." (⚑ OCR diacritics imperfect in the table.)
- **Orthography — the novel's naming crux, from 1846** (pp. ix–xii, OCR 242–395;
  pp. 365–366, OCR 26295–26340): one sound, one character (after John Pickering);
  continental vowels; and for Fijian — "The b is always preceded by m, the d by n, and
  the g by ng." The missionaries first wrote nd/mb/ngg, "found, however, that the
  natives would not recognise the elements thus separated," and switched to d=nd, b=mb,
  g=ngg (mission Bau/tabu vs. sound Mbau/tambu); Hale deliberately reverts and writes
  the combined sounds in full. So Hale's Mbau/Mbengga/Ndengei/**Veindovi** = modern
  Bau/Beqa/Degei/Veidovi — his spellings encode the actual sound, and justify whichever
  spelling the novel standardizes on.
- **Fiji baseline numbers** (pp. 47–49, OCR 2560–2586): Malolo among the largest islands
  of the Asaua chain; lowest population estimate for the group ~130,000 souls, estimates
  "differ considerably."

## 5. Malolo from the Fijian side — modern identity, memory, and the missing count

- **VERIFIED — the two villages, then and now:** in 1840 two villages existed on Malolo
  Levu — **Solevu (Wilkes: "Sualib") on the southern coast** facing Malolo Lailai, and
  **Yaro (Wilkes: "Arro") on the north-eastern coast**; both names survive as the
  island's two present-day villages. Source: Dirk H. R. Spennemann, "Groundwater, Graves
  and Golf: Layers of Heritage Tourism on a Fiji Resort Island," *Sustainability* 2021,
  13, 5863 (open access, CC-BY), https://doi.org/10.3390/su13115863; corroborated by
  Fiji Times features naming Yaro and Solevu as current villages. (Settles the
  PROJECT_NOTES §3 village-names soft spot together with Wilkes v3 pp. 289–290 — see
  `research/fiji-malolo.md`.)
- **Casualty figure in modern scholarship:** Spennemann states the retribution left "57
  dead Fijians, including two leading chiefs" (citing his own CSU monograph, *Malolo
  Lailai Island… during the Nineteenth Century*, 2021); Wikipedia "Battle of Malolo"
  gives 74–104; the "Malolo" article (citing Stanton 1975, pp. 205–215) gives 87; Fiji
  Times gives "about 80" and elsewhere 57. **All figures are documentary-derived (US
  sources); no independent Fijian count found. Keep the range as the base does.**
  **VERIFIED as a survey of the literature.**
- **Fijian oral-historical accounts of the 1840 attack: NOT FOUND as documented oral
  history.** The most substantial Fiji-published treatment — Fiji Times "Discovering
  Fiji: The Malolo massacre — Skull and death of Ro Veidovi" (John Mitchell, 21 Nov
  2021, https://www.fijitimes.com.fj/discovering-fiji-the-malolo-massacre-skull-and-death-of-ro-veidovi/) —
  relies entirely on Western sources and carries an editor's note acknowledging that "a
  group's version of events may not be the same as that held by another group,"
  explicitly inviting village accounts. Village-level oral tradition may exist
  unrecorded — field contact via the Fiji Museum or the mataqali of Solevu/Yaro would be
  needed. **VERIFIED negative for the online/published record.**
- **LEAD — divergent local-popular account:** a Fiji Times feature on Qalito (Castaway)
  Island (Siteri Sauvakacolo, 12 Nov 2017, https://www.fijitimes.com.fj/qalito-island/)
  reports, via a resort "Sense of Place" write-up, that during the food negotiation
  "Wilkes' men abducted and killed the chief's son" — divergent from the documentary
  record (the hostage escaped, fired over/at). Unsourced; possibly an echo of local
  tradition, possibly resort-history error. **Valuable as evidence of how the event is
  remembered/retold locally; do not use as fact without a village source.**
- **VERIFIED — the "old man from Malolo" is NOT oral history:** the old man who "spoke
  for Malolo" comes from Wilkes' own 1845 *Narrative* (the surrender scene); Wikipedia's
  "Battle of Malolo" cites no Fijian or Pacific-studies scholarship at all.
- **Modern scholarship on the Fiji side:** Roberta Sprague, "The United States Exploring
  Expedition in Fiji" (Univ. of Hawaii Pacific Islands Studies working paper OP31,
  pp. 12–49, https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/15538/1/OP31-12-49.pdf)
  narrates the attack and explicitly notes that published works "do not deal with the
  significance of this American contact in terms of its consequences for Fijians" — her
  sources are Western. ⚑ Her attack sequence (Yaro reached first, found
  deserted/burned) differs from some accounts putting the main assault at Solevu first —
  cross-check against Wilkes v3 before using sequence details. Spennemann's two 2021
  works are the only located modern scholarship treating Malolo's own landscape and
  heritage. **No Fijian-authored scholarship on the 1840 attack was found.** Best
  unsearched venue: the Fiji Museum's journal *Domodomo*; also Routledge's *Matanitū*
  and Clunie's *Fijian Weapons and Warfare* (in copyright — facts as leads only).
- **Reconciliation/commemoration: NOT FOUND.** Repeated targeted searches (apology,
  ceremony, soro, descendants, embassy visit, memorial) surfaced no US–Malolo
  reconciliation or commemoration event. The only commemoration found anywhere is
  American: the **1843 marble cenotaph to Underwood and Henry at Mount Auburn Cemetery,
  Cambridge, Mass.** — "To the memory of Lieutenant Joseph A. Underwood and Midshipman
  Wilkes Henry, United States Navy, who fell at the hands of savages, while promoting
  the cause of science and philanthropy at Malolo, July 24, 1840" (verify wording
  against a photograph of the stone before print). A gift to the double ledger: America
  memorialized its two dead in marble as martyrs to "science and philanthropy," while
  the 57–87+ Fijian dead have no located monument.
- **Malolo's own geography of meaning (adjacent oral tradition, VERIFIED as reported):**
  Yaro's living tradition includes **Vatu Tagi** (the Crying Rock, a ringing stone used
  as checkpoint/signal), **Na Ki ni Wai** ("Keys to the Ocean," installation site of the
  Tui Lawa, paramount chief of Malolo), and the ancient village Yaro Lawa formerly at
  Likuliku; the mataqali Ketenamasi of Yaro are traditional landowners of the Likuliku
  area. Source: Fiji Times, "The Crying Rock" (Siteri Sauvakacolo, 19 Nov 2017,
  https://www.fijitimes.com.fj/the-crying-rock/; single-informant feature). Useful for
  rendering the encountered world as subjects with their own geography of meaning.
- **LEAD — village continuity:** a search-level claim (apparently from Spennemann's CSU
