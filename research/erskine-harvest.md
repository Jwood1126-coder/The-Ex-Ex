## Part I — Verified extracts (with corrections they force)

### 1. Bowditch, the errand, the flogging — pp. 10–12  ·  E ★ / S / V

Erskine's own sequence (OCR lightly cleaned; verify at Wikisource before any `fact:` commit):

> "…my old brig *Porpoise*, Captain Charles Wilkes commander, on the coast survey. After
> surveying Georges Banks and Nantucket Shoals, we returned to Boston. The day after our
> arrival at the Navy Yard I was sent over to the city to the office of Mr. Bowditch, author
> of the 'American Navigator,' on State Street. He said that our charts were the neatest he
> had ever seen. He seemed to take quite an interest in me, and gave me some good advice in
> a fatherly way, which came just in time, for I had made up my mind that I would disappoint
> every one, and be somebody. From here I went to the office of the navy agent, where I
> received a number of letters, which I put into my hat — in those days sailors wore
> tarpaulin hats. I had been told by the captain to hurry and be quick, and had obeyed
> orders in good shape so far; but I could not go by my home a second time without stopping
> to see my mother. She was very glad to see me, and I shall never forget her fond embrace,
> and the 'God bless you, my darling boy!' when I left her. As I crossed the bridge, there
> was a schooner going through the draw, and while I was waiting, my hat was knocked
> overboard. I immediately jumped into the schooner's boat and recovered it, but of course
> the letters were wet. I met the captain near the dry dock, explained my adventure, and
> told him how the letters got wet. He gave me a look dark as a thunder-cloud, and ordered
> me on board. I went straight to the landing where the boat was, and the crew told me that
> the captain was as mad as a hornet. As I passed over the gangway, Lieutenant Boyle ordered
> the boatswain to 'introduce me to the gunner's daughter.' I was seized and placed over the
> breech of a sixty-two-pound Paxon gun, and whipped with the colt so severely that I could
> not sit down with any comfort for several weeks. The colt is a piece of rope about three
> feet long and half an inch thick. The boatswain and his mates always carry one in their
> hats for immediate use. I worked my right hand behind me and received several very painful
> cuts over the knuckles. All this time we were lying not more than a quarter of a mile in a
> straight line from where my mother lived, and if she had been at an open window at the
> front of the house she could have heard my piercing cries…" (pp. 10–12)

**Corrections this forces on the base (`fact:` candidates):**
- **F1. The letters-wetting mechanism is wrong in act_1 ch. 5.** Base: "a small schooner
  bumped his boat and the letters went into the sea." Erskine: he was *on foot crossing a
  bridge*; his tarpaulin hat (letters inside) was knocked overboard while a schooner passed
  the draw; he jumped into the schooner's boat to recover it. (p. 11)
- **F2. He explained himself ashore, face to face, before boarding** — met Wilkes near the
  dry dock; the thunder-cloud look happened there; the flogging order was waiting as he
  crossed the gangway. Base has the look at the ship's side. (p. 11)
- **F3. Lieutenant Boyle** gave the "gunner's daughter" order to the boatswain — the base's
  unnamed "a lieutenant" now has a name. (p. 11)
- **F4. Bowditch wording and page.** Reported speech, not direct: "He said that our charts
  were the neatest he had ever seen" — the base quotes it as direct ("the neatest charts I
  have ever seen"). Also settles the soft-spot page: **~p. 10**, not ~p. 224; office on
  State Street; "author of the 'American Navigator.'" (p. 10)
- **F5. The gun.** "Sixty-two-pound Paxon gun" is Erskine's own wording [sic — almost
  certainly Paixhans; keep his spelling in quotes, use "the breech of a gun" in narration].
  (p. 11)

**Grounding gold (S):** letters carried in the hat; the colt carried *in the boatswain's
mates' hats* "for immediate use"; the quarter-mile-to-mother's-window measurement is
Erskine's own.

### 2. The belaying pin — pp. 19–20  ·  E ★★ / S / V

> "It was now the 22d of August. We were in the Gulf Stream… On the 25th we set our course
> towards Madeira. For several days the weather was fine. On the 29th we crossed the Tropic
> of Cancer, longitude 4° west, at eight bells — twelve o'clock midnight. I had just
> relieved the lookout on the lee quarter. Except for a slight roll of the ship, silence
> reigned supreme. I am now about to reveal a secret that has been smothered in my breast
> for fifty long years. Would to God I could blot it from my memory! Through all these
> years it has been known to none save myself and to Him whose all-seeing eye is ever upon
> us. As I was looking down the cabin skylight, I saw Commodore Wilkes, the man who had
> ordered me to be flogged, sitting at a table tracing out a chart. I remembered my oath,
> and even then felt the sting of the boatswain's colt. I realized the situation, and the
> devil took possession of me. I watched my opportunity, and as the officer of the deck
> walked forward I grabbed an iron belaying-pin from the rack. In an instant it was
> suspended over the commodore's head, while I paused a moment, waiting for the ship's
> weather roll. At that instant I saw, or fancied I saw, the upturned face of my mother.
> 'My God! what does this mean?' I gasped under my breath. The belaying-pin was soon
> replaced in the rack, but it seemed to me that I had a death grip upon it. It was some
> time before I could take my hand from it. I felt as if I had committed the act, and were
> a criminal in thought, if not in deed. If I looked down into the blue sea or up into the
> depths of blue above, there I saw the face of my mother… At eight bells — four A.M. — the
> watch was relieved and I went below, but not to sleep… At sunrise the lookout from the
> fore-topsail yard reported a wreck. 'Where away?' was the cry. 'Two points on the weather
> bow,' came the answer…" (pp. 19–20; the "wreck" resolves as the cottonwood tree, p. 21)

**Corrections this forces (`fact:` candidates — these touch the crown jewel; Jake's call):**
- **F6 ★★. The scene's geometry.** Base ch. 1 puts Charlie *in the mizzen-top* with Wilkes
  *pacing the quarterdeck* below, a deck skylight between. Erskine: he was **on deck, at the
  lee-quarter lookout**, looking **down the cabin skylight** at Wilkes **seated at a table,
  tracing a chart, in the cabin below**; he took the pin from the rack when the officer of
  the deck walked forward, and held it over the skylight **waiting for the weather roll** to
  carry it true. (He was a mizzen-topman *by station* — p. 19, "one of the forties, that is,
  the 'never-sweats'" — which likely seeded the conflation.) The true geometry is better in
  every way: closer, more deliberate, the waiting-for-the-roll detail is seamanship-as-
  murder-method, and the officer-walks-forward beat gives the scene its clock.
- **F7. Date and position.** August 29, 1838, Tropic of Cancer, long. 4°W, at eight bells
  (midnight) — the base's "eleven days out" arithmetic holds (Aug 18 + 11), and now has an
  hour, a latitude line, and a watch.
- **F8. The cottonwood is the next sunrise**, sighted from the fore-topsail yard, "two
  points on the weather bow" — the sleepless night after the pin ends with the floating
  forest. Base (act_2 ch. 8) has it "a few days out," unconnected. The true sequence is a
  gift: the boy who didn't kill stands his sleepless watch and the sea hands him a world.
- **F9. "I remembered my oath."** There is an earlier sworn-revenge plant in Erskine that
  the base doesn't carry — locate it (likely pp. 12–13, right after the flogging; anchor
  table below). If confirmed, the oath is the missing link between ch. 5 and ch. 1.

**Grounding gold (S):** the small-stores list drawn against the purser (tea, sugar, pepper,
soap, tobacco, needle/thread/wax — p. 18–19); starboard/larboard watch division and stations
(forecastle, tops, waist, after-guard); "the forties, the never-sweats" as mizzen-top slang;
the officer of the deck's "Bright lookout fore and aft!" through the speaking-trumpet and
the answering chain from every lookout station; eight bells at both midnight and 4 A.M. in
one passage (the watch clock demonstrated); sunset-and-moonrise on the Gulf Stream eve.
**Voice (V):** "Old Adam" wiring — Wilkes' nickname for Charlie (per Jake's timeline, to be
page-confirmed) names "the evil side of human nature," and Erskine's own confession speaks
its language: "the evil one had taken possession of me" (p. 19), "the devil took possession
of me" (p. 20). The commander named the boy's devil before the boy met it. [NOVEL] wiring,
free, from the record.

**Harvest notes (span 3, verbatim):**

> THE MALOLO QUESTION (answered from TYBTM pp. 173-181): Erskine NEVER states where he was during the attack itself, and by his own boat-list he was in neither boat present — the first cutter was under Lt. Alden with Midshipman Henry, the Leopard under Lt. Underwood, with Emmons' Greyhound arriving for the recovery. His account of the fight is third-person throughout (Henry's and Underwood's deaths, the dodged shot at the hostage), with no scene of how or when he learned the news — the memoir cuts straight from the tortoise-shell trade to 'On the 22d of July.' His first-person 'we' first appears at the tail of the recovery ('We soon got under way and pulled for the ship,' p. 174 — plausibly memoir-collective rather than literal), becomes solidly eyewitness at the burial procession behind the Flying Fish carrying the bodies ('on our melancholy errand,' p. 175), and is unambiguous participant-witness for the punitive assault ('At nine o'clock we landed well armed,' p. 176: the rocket that fired the roof, the ditch where 'the cap of Lieutenant Underwood' was picked up, the burning) and the surrender ceremony on the hill (pp. 179-181). Since his ship was the Vincennes, the most defensible base-narrative position: Charlie was with the squadron/boat pool off Malolo, not at the massacre; he is an eyewitness only from the burial and reprisal onward, and his massacre detail is secondhand — most likely via Joseph G. Clark, whose lip Gilchrist sewed and whose poem ('I saw from his eye flash the heroic fire') Erskine prints. TWO FLAGS: (1) Erskine dates the massacre July 22, vs the base's July 24-26, 1840; (2) he gives NO native death toll — only the old chief's 'head chiefs and most of their wives had been killed' — so he neither supports nor contradicts the 74-104 range. PUNISHMENT FRIDAY (standing sub-question): Erskine gives no date and no explicit 'I saw,' but the launch-gallows was towed alongside the Vincennes 'when the rigging was manned,' making him a mustered spectator by naval routine; his close procedural detail (spun-yarn wrist lashings, shot-box, the quarter-gunner lifting the shirt 'with his thumb and finger') and the panorama of nine whale-ships' rigging 'covered with a mass of human beings' read as eyewitness. He names Riley (16), Sweeney (8), Ward (13 — 'a baker's dozen'); the two marines were tried for refusing duty claiming their time was up; Sweeney was the Englishman shipped at New Zealand, drummed out to the Rogue's March. Erskine quotes Wilkes' own justification verbatim and answers it: 'Where is our Christianity? Where is our civilization?' MINOR: signature context confirmed pp. 203-204, but the purser's receipt is printed dated May 7th, 1841 — chronologically loose against the Sept-Oct 1840 muster scene; note also the adjacent (p. 128, just outside range) Sydney flogging of the stowaway drummer-boy 'Oliver Twist' — sentenced 105 lashes, received ninety-three, 'five of them applied after the breath had left his body' — prime flogging-theme material one page before the assigned range.

**Harvest notes (span 4, verbatim):**

> Standing question — what does Erskine give the trial season, and how does the book handle Wilkes at the end? He gives the trials NOTHING: no mention anywhere of the 1842 courts-martial of Wilkes or his officers (whole-book grep confirms). The narrative's last sight of Wilkes is the final muster off the Battery, June 10, 1842 (pp. 265–266): Wilkes thanks the crew, predicts Government reward, the 26-gun salute is fired, his broad pennant is hauled down, and 'the commodore then left the ship and proceeded to Washington' — the sentence ends exactly where the court-martial begins, and the book never follows him. After that Wilkes exists only in the crew ditty's heroic tribute (pp. 266–268: 'Bold Captain Wilkes'; Cook and Ross 'must stand like mounds of dross beside our captain's name'; 'Columbus and our chief'). Erskine's judgment is displaced rather than absent: the flogging-reform outburst (pp. 264–265, 'My God! is this our civilization?'), the flogging-through-the-fleet chapter where he quotes Wilkes' own Narrative and asks 'Where is our Christianity?' (pp. 208–210), and the Somers forecastle conversation (pp. 304–305) condemn the system while never once naming Wilkes as its defendant. The memoir ends the Wilkes ledger on thanks, a salute, and a poem.
