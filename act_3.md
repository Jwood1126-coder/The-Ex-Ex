# ACT III — PARADISE AND POISON
*(April 1839 – December 1839)*

---

## Chapter 16 — The Sea Gull

The squadron's orders for the winter dispersal were simple. On April 17 the *Vincennes* and the *Porpoise* sailed for Valparaíso. The two schooners were to wait at Orange Harbor ten days for the overdue *Relief*, then follow; if the storeship never showed, they were to carry her stranded scientifics up the coast themselves. In the last days of April the *Sea Gull* and the *Flying Fish* gave up the wait and put to sea together, and almost at once the Cape Horn weather came on. The two little ships ran for shelter, and at midnight on April 28, riding out the gale in the lee of Staten Island — the same shattered coast Reynolds had flown past at thirteen knots in February — the *Flying Fish* had her last sight of her sister's lights against the black of the land.

The *Flying Fish* clawed her way north and stood into Valparaíso on May 19, nine days behind the squadron. The *Sea Gull* never stood into anywhere again.

At first her absence was merely lateness, and lateness was the squadron's oldest joke. Then the days at Valparaíso became weeks, and the joke curdled watch by watch. For a while the officers told themselves the stories crews tell: blown far to sea, dismasted, jury-rigged, limping for some Chilean roadstead — a hundred survivable explanations, dying one at a time on the empty horizon. Men found reasons to be on deck when anything showed in the offing. A vessel was eventually sent down the coast to search the bays and ask at the anchorages. It found nothing: no wreck, no boat, no body, no plank — the Pacific coast of South America, interrogated for a hundred-and-ten-ton schooner, returned a blank. The best guess the seamen could construct afterward was that her foremast had gone by the board in the gale off the Horn and torn up the deck as it went, opening her to a sea that finished the rest in minutes. She had been a New York pilot boat — built to dash out of the Narrows in dirty weather and put a pilot on an inbound ship, bought by the Navy in a hurry and sent around the world because she was cheap and available — and she had turned out to be the best sea boat in the squadron, and none of it mattered. Fifteen men and boys, under Passed Midshipman James Reid, with Passed Midshipman Frederick Bacon beside him, neither yet twenty-five, had been subtracted from the world in an unwitnessed hour, somewhere off the coast they had already survived twice.

Sit with what that did to the men who had sailed in her, and to one man in particular. Reynolds had just finished writing the *Sea Gull* the finest love letter a sailor ever gave a ship — the vessel "endowed with human sagacity," the little craft that rode a Cape Horn gale with her helm lashed and no hand on deck, the deck where he had stepped "from danger into the midst of companions and security" and slept sweet as a child. Every word of it was true, and the sea took her anyway. Took her *first* — before the rotten *Peacock*, before the plodding *Relief*, before any of the ships the wardroom joked about. There is no lesson in that, which is the lesson. The Southern Ocean does not audit virtue.

The squadron mourned the way ships must — quietly, and while working. The officers took up a collection among themselves for a monument to the lost, and the stone they eventually raised stands today in Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts: the names of the *Sea Gull*'s officers cut into it, with room left, as it turned out, for two more names the Pacific had not yet collected. Fifteen dead at a stroke — more than half of all the men the expedition would lose in four years, gone in its first eight months, without a witness, without a grave, without a story. The voyage's most articulate tragedy is the one that left no account at all.

Wilkes grieved in his fashion, which is to say officially, and pressed on. That is one of the genuinely hard things about the man: nothing stopped him — his great flaw, and exactly the quality the enterprise could not have done without. The ships were in the Pacific now. The work was ahead.

## Chapter 17 — Valparaíso and Callao

The squadron spent the late autumn of 1839 on the coast of Chile and Peru — Valparaíso first, where the *Peacock* rejoined on May 10 and the *Flying Fish* came in with her hard news; then north to the island anchorage of San Lorenzo, off Callao, the port of Lima, on June 6. Valparaíso was the Pacific's front door in those years, the first real port above the Horn, its steep amphitheater of hills stacked with the flags and warehouses of every nation that traded on the ocean, its anchorage crowded with the men-of-war of three navies keeping watch on each other and on the wars of the young republics ashore. The Americans were entertained in the style of the place — Wilkes himself went up over the coast range to Santiago, dining with ministers and being received in the capital as the commander of a great national enterprise, a taste of the standing he believed his uniform should have announced on its own — while the observatory went ashore and the pendulums swung and the chronometers were rated. Callao, in its turn, gave them Peru: the shattered old port under its fortress, the road up to Lima with the viceroyalty's grandeur going gently to seed at the end of it, a city of bells, balconies, and earthquake cracks where the officers gaped at churches sheathed in colonial silver and the scientifics filled boxes. Two months of observatory duty, reprovisioning, and repair, on a pleasant coast, in good weather. It was here, with no ice or gale to absorb him, that the commander turned his full attention to his own squadron, and the expedition began quietly eating itself.

The *Relief* arrived first, trailing an epic. Her cruise toward the Strait of Magellan with the scientifics had ended in a night that aged every man aboard: caught in a gale on a lee shore off Noir Island, on the drowned southwest coast, the slowest ship in the United States Navy had watched the rocks come up through the murk and done the only thing left — anchored, in the open sea, in the teeth of it. Through the dark the cables parted or the anchors dragged, one after another, each loss a step down toward the breakers, until she rode to her last ground tackle and her people waited to learn which would fail first, the iron or the wind. The wind failed first. She crawled away from that coast stripped of her anchors and her crew's illusions, and made her way alone into the Pacific.

Wilkes' verdict on her was cold arithmetic, and correct: the *Relief* had taken a hundred days to cross to Rio — a squadron record for slowness that stood unchallenged — and a storeship that cannot keep station is cargo, not company. He emptied her provisions into the other ships and, on June 21, sent her home. Her commander, Andrew Long — a Jones-era appointment, never one of Wilkes' own — went with her, and so did a small first shipment of the commander's discards: men whose faces no longer fit. The squadron was five ships now, and leaner in more ways than tonnage. Every officer watching the *Relief* stand out of Callao understood the demonstration. There was now a way home, and the commander held the manifest.

The suspensions had already begun — officers relieved of duty over trifles, correspondence demanded and dissected, the commander's warmth narrowing to a small circle the wardroom could name on one hand. The young men who had sailed from Norfolk half in awe of Lieutenant Wilkes were comparing notes now, and somewhere on that coast their private consensus crossed a line it never re-crossed: the problem was not the commander's moods. The problem was the commander.

Then, in the first weeks of the great Pacific crossing, the missing promotion finally detonated — not in Washington, but on the flagship's quarterdeck, on an ordinary morning at sea. The squadron had left South America on July 12, outbound into an ocean where the odds of meeting another American man-of-war were as near zero as the age allowed. Wilkes had done that arithmetic too. One August morning the officers of the *Vincennes* came on deck to find their commander wearing the uniform of a captain in the United States Navy — the epaulettes of a rank no one had given him — and shortly thereafter the broad blue pennant of a commodore broke out at the masthead, the signal that told every ship on the sea a squadron commander sailed here. Hudson, by arrangement, put on a captain's coat of his own aboard the *Peacock*. Not a line of authority from the Navy Department stood behind any of it. Nothing was announced, because there was nothing announceable; the uniform simply appeared, and the officers stared at it, and saluted it, and carried the staring below decks where it turned into the conversation that never afterward stopped.

His reasoning was not madness. A squadron entering seas governed by ceremony and force needed a commander whose rank read correctly to British post-captains and island kings, and Washington had sent him out naked. But every officer who saluted that pennant knew what it was, because they had watched the same man come apart for days over a letter correctly addressed to *Lieutenant* Wilkes. He had lived in the gap between his command and his rank until he could not bear it, and then he had closed the gap with cloth and bunting. A command whose central symbol is a known fiction is a command holding itself together by dares. There were three years left to go.

## Chapter 18 — The Dangerous Archipelago

The Pacific does not really submit to description, but the crossing supplies numbers, and the numbers do some of the work. The ocean the squadron now entered covers more of the planet than every continent combined; its deeps run down as far as airliners fly up; its farther shore was, for these ships, the better part of a year away. Scattered across its middle latitudes lay the islands the expedition had actually been built for — volcanic high islands and, above all, the coral rings called atolls, thousands of them, most fixed on the charts by rumor if at all. The whalemen's graveyard. The blank third of the world's paper. The orders, boiled down: turn as much blankness as possible into soundings before the season sent them south to the ice.

Their first study was the Tuamotus — the low archipelago east of Tahiti that generations of sailors had named, with feeling, the Dangerous Archipelago. An atoll is a ring of coral rubble and palms rarely more than a few feet above the sea; from a deck it is invisible until it is nearly unavoidable, announcing itself as a line of breakers or a smudge of treetops apparently growing out of open ocean. How such a thing could exist at all — a living ring standing on nothing, in mid-ocean, over depths no line could sound — was in 1839 a genuinely open question at the front edge of science. A young Englishman named Darwin, home from his own voyage, had just proposed an answer almost too elegant to trust: that every atoll is the gravestone of a drowned volcano, the coral growing upward for ages as the mountain beneath it sank. Aboard the *Peacock*, the expedition's own young geologist, James Dwight Dana, was about to spend two years gathering, reef by reef, much of the evidence that would prove it. The sailors sounding along those breakers knew only that the bottom fell away under the boat like a cliff; they were taking depth-casts on one of the century's great scientific questions, and the answer was directly beneath the lead.

On August 13 the squadron raised its first atoll — Reao, the charts' Clermont-Tonnerre — and the true labor of the Ex. Ex. began: the labor no engraving ever showed. Boats down at dawn. Junior officers and crews rowing and sounding along reef after reef under an equatorial sun, triangulating from ship to boat to shore, sketching, recording, while the big vessels stood off and on. Reynolds served his full apprenticeship at it. "This was the fifth day that we had passed in the boat hard at work," he wrote in one stretch, "and even my bones were sore from the short, quick motion, the hardness of the thwarts & the cramped & uneasy position that I was obliged to remain in. To quail or shrink or tire would not do, & we struggled on, I taking notes & observations whenever necessary." Multiply that entry by two years. That is the expedition — the tedium that became the charts that became, a century on, the difference between a landing craft finding a reef or being found by it.

First contacts ran the whole register, and the register opened badly. At Reao the people met the survey boats at the water's edge with spears and clubs and an unmistakable message, delivered in a tongue even the expedition's Tahitian interpreter only half-held: *go*. The boats tried patience, presents, pantomime. Wilkes, unwilling to log a first island as unexamined, finally ordered the beach cleared with bird-shot — small stuff, meant to sting rather than kill, and the islanders fell back and the surveyors did their work and no body lay on the sand afterward. But the arithmetic should be written the way the men understood it: the first shots fired by the United States Exploring Expedition were fired at the people it had come to chart, over a landing they had plainly refused. Other atolls balanced the ledger — trade eager across the gunwales, medals and fishhooks against pearl shell and mats; at one island the boats were warned off and Reynolds noted only regret, "particularly because the other islanders had been so hostile," the regret of a young man to whom hostility was still a curiosity. And once, coasting a lagoon island on a Sunday afternoon, he wrote the Pacific's enchantment down whole: several hundred islanders appearing and vanishing among the trees "as a screen, a drop curtain," while from aloft "the transparent blue of the lagoon and its rich green frame lay beneath the eye, looking as if it could have been created for no impurer use than to yield up the pearls that abound in its depths." The Dangerous Archipelago was also the most beautiful place any of them had ever seen. The Pacific would keep insisting on both truths at once, all the way to Fiji.

Tahiti, on September 11, was the legend made landfall — the island of Cook and Bougainville and the *Bounty*, the green pyramid rising out of the sea trailing cloud, the landfall every man aboard had grown up hearing lied about. What they found was the legend two generations into the missionary era: the island of the forecastle stories overwritten with chapel-going and covered cloth, Sabbath law and printed hymnals, its famous ease regulated now by a native monarchy and its English advisors. The squadron raised its observatory at Point Venus, on the very ground where Cook had measured the transit of Venus seventy years before — surveyors' ground, consecrated — and spent a month at the work: charts of the harbors and the girdling reef, audiences with the government of the young queen Pōmare, wooding and watering and the endless provisioning arithmetic of four ships, canoes alongside all day trading oranges and breadfruit and pigs against fishhooks and cloth. Parties went into the interior and came back half-drunk on it — valleys walled in green a thousand feet high, waterfalls dropping out of the clouds, a landscape that made the scientifics write like poets and the poets among the sailors give up entirely. The sailors went looking for the old Tahiti of the stories; the missionaries stood guard over the new one; and the Tahitians navigated both sets of visitors with the practiced patience of a people long expert in managing Europeans — a nation that had seen every kind of white man the ocean could deliver, and had learned to price them all. The scientifics, at least, were in heaven — new species for Pickering and Peale, reef sections for Dana's growing case, and languages for Hale, who was quietly assembling, island group by island group, the linguistic map that would let him draw the great migration routes of the Pacific peoples across the chart like currents: the expedition's least sung achievement, done by its youngest scientific, a philologist barely out of Harvard reconstructing the greatest navigation feat in human history from verb endings. The collections swelled toward the tonnage that would one day require a national institution.

They sailed October 10, westward, and somewhere in those weeks the squadron's inner weather turned another degree. The commander's reprimands — the "bitter doses," in Reynolds' phrase — had become the wardroom's chronic climate, and Reynolds caught himself brooding on it in the journal and tried, touchingly, to legislate his own peace: "But enough of this! I had intended never to introduce such themes among these pages..."

The intention did not survive Samoa.

## Chapter 19 — Samoa

The Samoan islands, through October and into November, gave the expedition some of its finest work and its commander some of his smallest hours — and they are where the voyage's second witness finally stopped extending credit.

The work first, because it was real. The boats surveyed Tutuila and charted the great harbor at Pago Pago — and Pago Pago deserves a sentence of its own, because it is one of the strangest and finest anchorages on the planet: a drowned volcanic crater with one wall broken open to the sea, so that a ship threads a slot between green cliffs and finds herself floating inside a mountain, in still water, ringed by walls that climb straight out of the harbor into cloud. The surveyors mapped it as a fortress waiting for a flag, and their judgment stood: the United States Navy would be coaling, and then fueling, and then flying jets out of that crater for the better part of the next two centuries, on the strength of a chart drawn by men in rowing boats. On Upolu the expedition negotiated commercial regulations with the chiefs and paid its respects to Malietoa; Hale filled notebooks; the surveys ran clean. There was harder business too, of the kind the orders required: American sailors had been killed in these islands in years past, and the squadron carried a warrant, in effect, for a chief named Opotuno, whom the whalemen's testimony named for several of the murders. Wilkes put a price on him. Opotuno declined to be caught — he outlasted the expedition's patience without difficulty, being on his own island among his own people, screened by kin no reward could buy, and the squadron learned what every navy of the age kept learning and unlearning: a warship's writ ran exactly as far as her guns, and the guns could not tell one man from a village. The failed manhunt settled nothing. Eight months on, in Fiji, the same arithmetic would be tried again with the whole squadron behind it, and the bill for solving it the other way would be the darkest page in the log.

Off duty, the islands gave the young officers nights they kept for the rest of their lives. Reynolds, ashore on survey with "the valuable attendance of my black valet, David Smith" — one of the expedition's Black sailors, present everywhere in this story and almost never allowed by its documents to be more than a phrase — was guested in a chief's house for an evening of dance he recorded with the same care he gave coastlines: the dancers in girdles of leaves moving through "an infinite variety of motions & gestures without quitting their places... The changes were rapid but were always made together. Commencing slow, the motions became violent by degrees, until the dance terminated from the exhaustion of the artistes" — renewed after rest, kept up late into the night. He was twenty-three, half a world from Lancaster, being shown a civilization's art form by firelight, and the journal glows.

Then, Tuetila. An old chief of Leone — one of the principal men of Tutuila — had shown Reynolds' surveying party sustained kindness, and promised to cross the island's mountains to pay his respects to the great chief of the ships. He kept the promise: an elderly man of rank, walking over an island to honor a foreign captain. Lieutenant Joseph Underwood carried word of his arrival to Wilkes, with an account of the old man's attentions to the American officers.

Wilkes refused to see him.

No reason given; none, that the officers could discover, existed. "Much to our surprise & mortification," Reynolds wrote. "Why, we could not tell! 'Twas not politic & was rude & insulting." The junior officers did what juniors could — loaded the chief and his son with presents from their own stores until the visitors departed "rich & happy & with a high opinion of our generosity" — privately repairing, out of their own sea chests, the damage their commander had inflicted for nothing. The symmetry with the *Independence* affair closes like a trap: the man who had come apart for days over an omitted honorific dealt the identical wound to a chief who had walked mountains for him, and never registered the transaction at all. Wilkes' radar for slights swept in one direction only. And the officer standing in the doorway with the refused request in his hands is worth a second look. Underwood had a way of being posted at the exact spot where the commander's failures with island dignitaries came due. At Malolo, in eight months, the account would be settled through him.

Reynolds' own turn came within days. Mustering with the ship's company, he was — by the count of the two brother officers he immediately polled — not five minutes late, and found himself suspended from duty and confined below, the punishment arriving stripped even of its customary courtesies. He turned it over in the journal with the baffled care of a man checking his own instruments — "I could not lay my suspension to any other cause" — and something finished changing. The entries about Wilkes stop reading like weather reports and start reading like a verdict being drafted. The admiration of the Depot of Charts, the letters that had talked the brilliant lieutenant up to Lydia — all of it stood fully converted now, and the exchange rate was brutal. Aboard the flagship of the United States Exploring Expedition, its most gifted writer was at war, in secret pages, with its commander. Neither the war nor the pages would stay secret forever.

## Chapter 20 — Sydney

The squadron raised Australia at the end of November, and Wilkes introduced himself to the British Empire with the single most audacious piece of seamanship of the entire voyage. Rather than lie off till morning like everyone since the First Fleet, he took his ships straight in through Sydney Heads in the dark of night, November 29 — no pilot, no local knowledge beyond the printed charts, the leadsmen chanting in the dark and the town's own scattered lamps for leading lights — threaded one of the great harbors of the world, and anchored quietly off the town. The colony woke to find an American squadron sitting in its lap, arrived past every lookout, every battery, and every regulation, unseen and unhailed. The newspapers took the point at once, and said so in print: a hostile force could have done the same, and Her Majesty's outpost would have learned of the war at daybreak, from the guns. It was concentrated, essential Wilkes — superb professional skill, real audacity, and a showman's instinct for the gesture that announces *I am not who you think I am*, performed for an audience that had never thought about him at all.

Sydney itself fascinated the Americans as a mirror world: an English-speaking, church-building, horse-racing society founded as a prison, where the line between the transported and the free structured everything and could be mentioned nowhere. Reynolds toured it with his collector's eye out: "There are a few, a very few, honest people there, but you must be cautious in your mode of speech. It is dangerous to whisper *transportation* in the street... I saw a fellow dashing by, driving tandem. He was wealthy, but his term had just been out three weeks. One man built a palace on the ground where the gallows had been erected for him, & from which he had been reprieved when the rope was around his neck! (3 times, as the story goes.)" A society of second chances enforced by silence. The republic's officers found it half admirable and wholly strange, and were feted throughout it — dinners, balls, the full colonial hospitality of people starved for new company.

The hospitality produced the explosion. By the code of the age the Americans owed entertainment in return, and the officers proposed the natural thing: a fête aboard the flagship. Wilkes refused it — refused the ship, pleaded the government's economy, proposed half-measures no gentleman could put his name to — and in the collision that followed he told his assembled officers, in substance, that he was glad of the quarrel, for he was not dependent upon them. Reynolds went below and wrote the entry that is the sound of the voyage's soul splitting, and it should stand here exactly as his pen left it:

"He was unreasonable & proposed measures that no one could think of adopting... insulted the whole body of his Officers, & made himself despised & contemptible... The foul scamp — he should be hung. I have not patience to speak of him with decency, neither does he deserve I should. Mercy, how he is hated. Four sycophants cling to him—"

Fifteen months earlier the same hand had told Lydia that Lieutenant Wilkes was a wonder. Plot the two entries as fixes and the line between them is the truest track the expedition made — steeper than any course made good over the ground.

And beneath the social wars ran the real current. Through December the ships stored and refitted for the south, and this time everyone knew it was no reconnaissance: the orders were the campaign itself, a full season along the ice to settle what the ice concealed. Sydney was a port full of professional seamen who had watched whalers fit out for high latitudes all their lives, and they looked over the American ships and asked the Americans the obvious questions. Where was the doubled planking? Where were the reinforced bows, the sheathing, the ice gear any Sydney master would demand before pointing a hull at the pack? The visitors had no answers, because there were none: the hulls were as Norfolk had sent them, and Norfolk had sent them thin. Some of the colonists, the story ran through the squadron afterward, asked the officers whether their wills were made. The *Peacock*'s condition was by now an open secret in her own wardroom — her officers had formally represented the rot, and the representations had changed nothing, because there was no money, no time, and no commander alive less likely to turn back over a survey report. The economies of a decade had finally reached their destination: the wooden side of a tired ship, bound for the one sea on the planet that specifically punishes thin wooden sides.

On December 26, 1839 — Christmas spent in port, the last one some of them counted on seeing, and no one had to say so aloud — four vessels stood out through the heads they had ghosted past a month before: *Vincennes*, *Peacock*, *Porpoise*, and the little *Flying Fish*, the record-holder, going back for more. They turned south. Ahead lay the question the whole decade of the enterprise had been aimed at: whether the ice at the bottom of the world was a wall around an ocean, or the shore of something no one had ever named.

They had a continent to find, and about eight weeks of summer to find it in.
