# ACT I — THE MAKING OF A COMMANDER
*(1828 – August 1838)*

---

## Chapter 1 — The Pin

Eleven days out of Norfolk, on a quiet night in the North Atlantic, a sixteen-year-old boy stood in the mizzen-top of the flagship *Vincennes* and considered killing his commander.

His name was Charlie Erskine. He was a cabin boy from Boston, blue-eyed and well-liked, and he was supposed to be standing his watch. Below him, on the dark quarterdeck, Lieutenant Charles Wilkes paced among his charts — studying them, revising them, the way he did at all hours, as if the lines of soundings and coastlines were the only company he trusted. Between the boy above and the man below there was a skylight: a single pane of glass.

Racked beside Charlie in the top were belaying pins — iron, heavy, made to hold a ship's rigging fast. Things fall from rigging. Every sailor knew it. Blocks, marlinspikes, men. An accident aboard a ship is the most believable death in the world.

Charlie's hand found a pin. He unclasped it from the rack and held it out over the glass, and he watched the commander's slow circuit of the deck, and he calculated — the drop, the shatter, the skull. He meant to do it. He said so himself, years later, when he finally told anyone at all: he fully intended to kill the man. As Wilkes passed beneath him, something intervened — Charlie called it a vision of his mother, and took it for a sign from God. But the body was slower to relent than the soul. "It seemed to me that I had a death grip upon it," he wrote. "It was some time before I could take my hand from it."

He set the pin back in its place. The commander walked on, studying his charts. The *Vincennes* creaked eastward through the dark, carrying both of them — and three hundred forty-four other men, and the scientific hopes of a young republic — toward the far side of the world. The voyage would last four years. It would kill twenty-eight of them, sink two of the six ships, chart a continent, and end in a courtroom. And for decades, no one aboard would know how close it came, on the eleventh night, to losing the one man it could not sail without and could barely sail with.

To understand what a boy who loved his mother was doing in the rigging with murder in his hand, you have to go back — past the launch, past the flogging, past the ten mutilated years of politics that produced this voyage — to a newspaper editor from Ohio who believed the Earth might be hollow.

## Chapter 2 — A Hole at the Bottom of the World

Every great voyage needs a prophet, and prophets are rarely respectable. The Ex. Ex. got Jeremiah N. Reynolds: newspaperman from rural Ohio, lecturer, promoter, true believer — a man who began his career barnstorming the country on behalf of John Cleves Symmes' theory that the Earth was hollow and open at the poles, and who never entirely stopped sounding like a man selling a hole at the bottom of the world.

Give him this: he went and looked. In 1829 Reynolds helped raise money for a private sealing-and-exploring venture to the far south — the *Seraph*, the *Annawan*, and the little *Penguin* — and sailed with it out of New York aboard the *Annawan*. The expedition got far enough to meet the monolithic wall of ice that guards the Antarctic — and got no farther. On the return, at Valparaíso in Chile, the crew had had enough of terror and mutinied, putting Reynolds and an expedition artist named John Frampton Watson ashore and sailing on without them. The two Americans were stranded in Chile for the better part of two years, until the frigate *Potomac*, homeward bound the long way around the world from a punitive cruise to Sumatra, took Reynolds aboard as the commodore's secretary and finished his circumnavigation for him.

For a boy raised in early-1800s Ohio, it was the adventure of ten lifetimes, and Reynolds came home fluent in it. He had stared at the ice. He had heard, in the ports of Chile, the whalemen's stories — including one about an old white bull sperm whale off Mocha Island, a monster the harpooners spoke of by name. In 1839 Reynolds would publish that story as *Mocha Dick: or the White Whale of the Pacific*, and a former whaleman named Herman Melville would read it and not forget it. Reynolds' polar enthusiasms had already reached another writer: Edgar Allan Poe, who followed Reynolds' speeches and reports with something close to obsession and spun them into his only novel — *The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym*, a voyage into the white silence of the far south. The Ex. Ex. is the rare expedition that helped inspire two of the strangest masterpieces in American literature before it ever left the dock. It would eventually help inspire a third institution, stranger still: but the Smithsonian comes later.

Reynolds' true gift was volume. Through the late 1820s and 1830s he made himself the loudest voice in America for a national exploring expedition — buttonholing congressmen, publishing memorials, and in April 1836 delivering an address before Congress itself on the glory and profit awaiting the nation in the Pacific. He found a listener at the top: President John Quincy Adams, who saw in a voyage of discovery exactly the kind of national self-improvement he believed in. On May 21, 1828, the House resolved to send a ship to the Pacific, and Adams ordered the Navy to ready one.

Then the money died in the Senate, and the idea entered the wilderness — where it would wander for a decade.

## Chapter 3 — Ten Years of Almost

The case for the expedition was never really about wonder, and its advocates knew better than to pretend otherwise. It was about whales.

By the 1830s the United States owned the largest whaling fleet on Earth, and the whale fishery lit the country — literally. Northern industry rendered whale oil into the fuel of American lamps, and the whales were not off American shores. They were in the Pacific: off Chile and Peru, off Japan, in seas that appeared on American charts as guesses, rumors, and blanks. Whaleships sailed into those blanks and, with grim regularity, some of them did not come back — wrecked on reefs no chart admitted existed, or cut off at islands whose people had their own accounts, sometimes well-earned, with visiting ships. Every loss was a mariner's tragedy and an investor's argument. Good charts would lower the risk; lower risk meant higher profit. Sealers told the same story from farther south, where a collapsing seal population had driven them down among the peri-Antarctic islands, discovering coastlines by nearly dying on them.

Here is the strange part, the part that tells you what kind of country this was: private industry, which stood to gain the most, would not pay for it. The returns were too diffuse, too slow, too uncertain — a chart benefits every ship equally, including your competitor's. Exploration was, in the language of a later century, infrastructure. Only a government could afford to want it. So the United States government — prodded by Adams, embraced by his successor and opposite Andrew Jackson, harangued throughout by Jeremiah Reynolds — decided to look at the ocean as a long-term business stimulus, and on May 18, 1836, Congress finally authorized and funded a United States Exploring Expedition.

Authorization, it turned out, was the easy part. What followed were two years so botched they nearly strangled the voyage in port — and they are worth lingering on, because they forged the resentments the squadron carried to sea.

The Secretary of the Navy, Mahlon Dickerson, was ostensibly in charge of preparations. Dickerson was a cultivated man who professed a love of science, and he proceeded to smother the expedition with the particular thoroughness of an official who does not want a thing to happen but will not say so. He was sixty-six and ailing; he dithered over ships, over scientists, over everything. Jackson, impatient, bolted on a parallel authority: Commodore Thomas ap Catesby Jones, an old comrade from the Battle of New Orleans, to command the squadron and pick its officers — with Jeremiah Reynolds, at Jackson's insistence, involved in the planning. The arrangement guaranteed friction and delivered it.

Meanwhile the Navy's officer corps looked at the expedition and, with few exceptions, wanted nothing to do with it. There was no war in it, no prize money, no glory of the kind the service understood — only four years of what one might call babysitting scientists and cartographers at the end of the world. Chasing pirates in the Caribbean was a career; surveying was a chore. Senior officers declined the command or accepted and drifted away. Sailors recruited for the voyage waited so long at their moorings that enlistments began expiring before the anchors ever came up. The nation's newspapers turned the "Deplorable Expedition" into a running joke.

Then came 1837: financial panic, a new president in Martin Van Buren, and word from across the Atlantic that concentrated minds wonderfully — the French were sending Dumont d'Urville south, and the British were preparing James Clark Ross. The bottom of the world was about to be claimed in someone's name, and it was starting to look like the name would not be American. In November 1837, Commodore Jones, his health and patience destroyed by a decade of almost, resigned the command in disgust.

The expedition now had ships, funding, instruments, scientists — and no commander, because every senior officer in the United States Navy had refused it. Van Buren, done with Dickerson, handed the whole mess to a man with a reputation for finishing things: his Secretary of War, Joel Poinsett — the traveled, capable former minister to Mexico whose name survives on a red Christmas flower. Poinsett looked down the Navy list, past all the captains and commanders who had said no, and stopped at a forty-year-old lieutenant whom nobody liked and nobody could do without.

## Chapter 4 — The Chart-Maker

Charles Wilkes was born in New York City in 1798, a great-nephew of the radical English politician John Wilkes. His mother died when he was three. The aunt who took him in, Elizabeth Ann Seton, would convert to Catholicism, found an order, and become — long after — the first American-born saint of the Catholic Church. The future saint raised the future most-hated man in the United States Navy. When Seton was widowed and overwhelmed, the boy was sent to boarding school. Columbia College followed, then the Navy in 1818 as a midshipman: a motherless, sent-away child who grew into a man pathologically alert to any hint that he did not belong where he stood.

What Wilkes had — and it was real — was the science. He trained in mathematics under Nathaniel Bowditch, the self-taught Salem genius whose *New American Practical Navigator* had corrected thousands of errors in the world's tables and become the sea's bible. He learned triangulation from Ferdinand Hassler of the Coast Survey, geomagnetism from James Renwick. In 1833, on the strength of his survey of Narragansett Bay, the Navy put him in charge of its Department of Charts and Instruments — the modest Washington office out of which the Naval Observatory and the Hydrographic Office would eventually grow. He belonged to the Columbian Institute alongside presidents and generals. He was, in a navy that promoted by seniority and prized gunnery, that suspect thing: an intellectual.

He had been orbiting the expedition almost from the start. Dickerson recommended him early; Jones distrusted him as green. Wilkes made himself useful anyway — organizing, listing, corresponding, assigning midshipmen, working late into the nights at the Depot with a small crew of young officers who found his energy infectious. One of them was a Pennsylvania midshipman named William Reynolds — no relation to the Ohio prophet — who thought the lieutenant a marvel and said so in letters to his sister Lydia. Remember that admiration. The whole voyage lives in the distance between what Reynolds wrote about Wilkes in 1838 and what he would write by 1839.

In 1836 Dickerson sent Wilkes to Europe to purchase the expedition's scientific instruments — five months among the elite of world science, London and Paris, the instrument-makers' shops. He came home with superb theodolites, chronometers, and pendulum apparatus for measuring the shape of the Earth itself — and with almost nothing for anyone else's science. The expedition's naturalists inventoried the crates with mounting disbelief: instruments fit for the Royal Society on the astronomical side, and on theirs, not so much as a microscope. It was the first public glimpse of the pattern: Wilkes' devotion to science was real, and it was devotion to *his* science; the man could not reliably tell the difference between the mission and himself.

The scientists' fury cost him. When the corps was organized, Wilkes was offered a place on the expedition as its astronomer — a subordinate berth, under men who had just finished denouncing him. He refused with heat. He had given the project years; he would take what he had earned or nothing. And then, instead of sulking, he did the shrewdest thing of his career. In the spring of 1837 he took the brig *Porpoise* to sea and surveyed the Georges Bank and Nantucket Shoals — treacherous, commercially vital waters off New England that everyone wanted charted and no one wanted to chart. It was dangerous, valuable, visible work: a demonstration, addressed to the Navy Department, of exactly the skills the expedition lacked at its head. He even brought his new European instruments along, field-testing his own purchases and vindicating them.

The survey made his name with the Cape Cod fishermen, with the merchants, with the politicians who counted the fishermen's votes. In Boston, Wilkes presented his finished charts to the great Bowditch himself — the leading authority on Earth, his old teacher — who examined them and pronounced them "the neatest charts I have ever seen," and came back days later to praise them again. It is not too much to say that this was the finest moment of Charles Wilkes' life: the one unqualified blessing from the one unimpeachable judge. Everything he did afterward — every insistence on a title, every rage at a slight — can be read as a man trying to hear that sentence again from a world that kept declining to repeat it.

There was a boy present for the blessing, holding the charts. Bowditch noticed him too, and "gave me some good advice in a fatherly way," the boy remembered, "which came just in time, for I had made up my mind that I would disappoint everyone, and be somebody."

His name was Charlie Erskine, and within the week, Wilkes would put a mark on him that lasted the rest of both their lives.

## Chapter 5 — The Gunner's Daughter

Charlie Erskine grew up in Boston without a father, with a mother he adored, and went to sea as a boy because that is what poor Boston boys did. Aboard the *Porpoise* on the shoals survey he served as Wilkes' cabin boy, and something in the arrangement fit: the fatherless boy, the commander who noticed him. Charlie later admitted plainly that he looked to Wilkes as a father. On that survey, Wilkes was trying out a command style to match — friendly, familiar, an officer who got along with his people. The crew liked him. Charlie loved him.

At the survey's end, with the *Porpoise* lying at the Boston Navy Yard, Wilkes gave Charlie a last errand: carry letters across town, and return to the ship. Charlie's mother lived in Boston, close by. The day's real work was done; the boy could not see the harm. He went to her. The visit was brief and it ended the way such visits end — "God bless you, my darling boy!" — and Charlie started back. In the harbor, a small schooner bumped his boat and the letters went into the sea. He snatched them out before the water ruined them, but the recovery cost time, on top of the time the visit had cost, and when Charlie came over the side, late, the friendly commander was gone. "He gave me a look as dark as a thunder-cloud," Charlie remembered, "and ordered me on board." The crew told him the lieutenant was "mad as a hornet."

What happened next was, by the Navy's lights, ordinary. The ordinariness is the horror of it. Wilkes had discovered the problem with friendliness: it borrows against authority, and the debt comes due. An officer who lets the men close must, when the moment comes, punish harder, so that no one mistakes warmth for weakness — so that being a friend, as it were, never gets in the way of getting results. Wilkes concluded that Charlie had taken advantage of him, stolen a holiday in Boston on his commander's trust. He did not ask. He did not believe the story of the schooner and the letters, or did not care to test it. He ordered the boy flogged.

A lieutenant gave the order in the language of naval tradition: "Introduce Charlie to the gunner's daughter." The gunner's daughter was a cannon. Charlie was bent over the breech of one of the brig's guns and whipped with the colt — three feet of half-inch rope — while the crew that liked him stood witness, because witness was the point. He screamed. He believed, he wrote later, that his mother could have heard him a quarter mile off if her windows were open. The colt cut through his clothes and soaked them with blood, and when he reached back instinctively with his right hand, the colt bloodied that too, across the knuckles. He could not sit for weeks.

The weeks healed. Nothing else did. Charlie was not destroyed by the pain; sailors absorbed floggings the way factory hands absorbed lost fingers, as the tariff of the trade. What broke was the belief. He had been punished as a liar by the one man he had chosen to trust, for an accident, without a hearing — by his father, as far as his heart was concerned. The shame and the hatred set like a fracture badly healed, and he would carry them, mostly hidden, through everything that followed: through four years and eighty-seven thousand miles under the same man's pennant, up into a mizzen-top on an August night with an iron pin in his fist.

Weeks later, when the great expedition finally wanted crews, a somewhat reluctant Charlie Erskine signed on anyway. Perhaps it was Bowditch's fatherly advice still working in him; perhaps a poor boy's arithmetic; perhaps the simple gravity of the biggest adventure his country had ever mounted. He drew comfort from one detail: he was rated to the *Porpoise*, not to the flagship. Whatever the voyage held, at least he would not have to serve directly under the commander he now despised.

The Navy would fix that soon enough.

## Chapter 6 — The Interview

Poinsett moved fast. His first choice for command was Captain Joseph Smith — a popular, capable officer — offered the expedition on one condition of Smith's own: that Wilkes come along as his surveyor. Wilkes, summoned to Washington in March 1838 fresh from the shoals, learned of the arrangement and declined it. He would have had command of a vessel, but under Smith, with officers senior to himself aboard his own ship — the same subordination he had already refused once. Some in the service smelled a deeper game: decline every lesser role, and the top role has nowhere else to go. Rumors said Wilkes had schemed for the command all along. "I never thought of such a thing," Wilkes insisted afterward. "I was too young an officer to aspire and did not dream of it." Believe as much of that as you like. Smith's health failed; the arrangement collapsed; and Poinsett, running out of Navy, was forced to consider the lieutenant on the merits.

The merits were awkward and real. Wilkes was junior — a lieutenant of forty, twenty years in grade-time behind the men who had refused the job. He had not held a sea command since his twenties. He was also, verifiably, the best surveyor in the United States Navy, endorsed by Bowditch himself, and — this mattered most — he actually wanted it. On an evening in the spring of 1838, Poinsett had Wilkes to his home and, in everything but name, interviewed him. Wilkes talked the way he worked: fluently, totally, as if the expedition were a chart he had already drawn. He wanted a young squadron — junior officers in acting commands he could shape, rather than seniors who would fight him. He wanted the force restructured: keep the big ships for sea-keeping, add small vessels that could work the shoal lagoons of the Pacific where the surveying would actually happen. To pay for it, the scientific corps would be cut — from the grand planned twenty-odd down to a working handful, with Wilkes himself absorbing the astronomy, the surveying, the physics. Poinsett asked whether all this could truly be organized in months, after ten years of failure. Wilkes assured him it could. Poinsett then told him the command was his.

Wilkes, by his own account, was speechless — overwhelmed. It passed. That night he slept soundly, he tells us, while his wife Jane lay beside him beginning the arithmetic of four years alone. The appointment came in April 1838, and the United States Navy erupted. Letters of outrage poured into the War Department. Senior officers who had spent a decade refusing the expedition now discovered, upon seeing who had it, that the honor of the service demanded they be given it instead. The administration held. The rules of seniority, Poinsett reasoned, were rules for war; this was not a war command — a formula that soothed exactly no one and happened to be true.

The junior commander now had to find officers to serve under him, which meant officers junior to a lieutenant — a near-impossibility the Navy's stunted rank structure made worse. (The service's highest actual rank was still captain; "commodore" was a courtesy for a captain commanding a squadron. Other navies had admirals; the United States had arguments.) For his second-in-command and the *Peacock*, Wilkes wanted William Hudson: forty-four, a superb seaman with no surveying experience, and a close friend — who happened to slightly *outrank* Wilkes. A junior officer commanding his senior was unheard of; Hudson agreed only after Poinsett formally classed the enterprise as non-military. The storeship *Relief* went to Andrew Long, a holdover choice from the Jones era, who agreed on the condition that he outrank the *Porpoise*'s commander; the *Porpoise* went to Cadwalader Ringgold. The schooners went to young lieutenants and passed midshipmen. Freshly minted officers were handed acting appointments above their rank — a squadron of the promotable and the pliable, junior enough to command, young enough to shape, and owing everything to Wilkes. It was rational, and it planted a minefield: half the wardroom held temporary titles the commander could revoke, and the other half remembered being chosen by Jones and Reynolds rather than by Wilkes — a distinction Wilkes never forgot either, and never forgave.

Then the scientists. Cutting the corps was the most delicate butchery of the preparation, and Wilkes performed it on a simple principle: he eliminated everyone whose work he believed he could do himself. Astronomy, surveying, physics, meteorology — his. What remained needed to be the best in the country, and, to his credit, largely was: Titian Ramsay Peale, naturalist, painter, crack shot, son of the great museum Peales of Philadelphia; James Dwight Dana, twenty-five, whose *System of Mineralogy* was already a standard text — a newly devout young man in search of the big picture, who would find more of it on this voyage than anyone; Charles Pickering of Philadelphia's Academy of Natural Sciences, naturalist of omnivorous brilliance; Horatio Hale, a Harvard prodigy, philologist, collector of languages; Joseph Pitty Couthouy, a Boston sea-captain turned conchologist; William Rich, botanist; William Brackenridge, the working Scots horticulturist. With the artists Joseph Drayton and Alfred Agate — nine civilians where Europe's grandest voyages had sailed with two or three. An American answer to the *Beagle*, whose own naturalist had lately returned to England and begun, very quietly, to reorganize life on Earth.

All that remained was the promise. Poinsett had assured Wilkes and Hudson they would be made captains — acting appointments, at least — before the squadron sailed; a squadron commander needed rank the way a mast needs stays. Then Poinsett fell gravely ill. The Navy Department, under its new secretary, discovered reasons the promotions belonged to someone else's authority. The president himself came down to Norfolk that summer to review the squadron — white trousers, blue jackets, a thundering gun salute in place of band music, a grand party thin on senior naval officers, who mostly declined to attend — and the appointments everyone expected to be announced that day were not announced. Wilkes sailed as a lieutenant, commanding commanders, wearing (soon enough) a captain's coat he had not been given and flying a commodore's pennant he had promised himself. The Navy had sent its most insecure officer to the far side of the world with a grievance for a commission.

Every disaster of the next four years is already present in that sentence. So, in fairness, is the continent.

## Chapter 7 — Six Odd Ships

The squadron that gathered at Norfolk in the summer of 1838 was, as even friendly observers admitted, an odd assortment — six vessels chosen for six different reasons, about to be asked for the same impossible thing.

The flagship was the *Vincennes*: a 780-ton sloop-of-war, eighteen guns, already once around the world and the finest ship of her class in the service — Wilkes' seat, and Charlie Erskine's fate. Her consort was the *Peacock*, 650 tons, Hudson's command — nominally the *Peacock* of 1813 made new in 1828, in practice a hasty reconstruction hastily refitted, carrying rot in her bones that the yard's economies had painted over. Officers who inspected her said so, in writing. It would take three years for the sea to agree with them.

Then the *Relief*, a purpose-built storeship of 468 tons — capacious, sturdy, and slow in a way that sailors found almost supernatural; the *Porpoise*, the 230-ton, 88-foot brig of the shoals survey, quick and handy ("She sat like a duck on the water," Charlie wrote, "and looked as trim and neat as a young lady in her Sunday rig. I must confess that I was fairly carried away with her bewitched and rakish looks"); and, Wilkes' signature addition, two New York pilot schooners bought into the Navy and renamed *Sea Gull* and *Flying Fish* — 110 and 96 tons, two guns apiece, little harbor greyhounds meant to dart through island lagoons and shoal water where the big ships dared not go. No one proposed to sail ninety-six tons against the Antarctic ice. That is worth writing down now, plainly, because in eighteen months they would do it twice.

The ships were made ready for extremes with a mixture of ingenuity and false economy that exactly mirrored the whole enterprise. Wilkes worried about stability in the great Southern Ocean seas and lightened his vessels aloft — even fighting the Navy Yard (which resented him too much to cooperate gracefully) to replace iron water tanks with wooden casks, a request that finally went, absurdly, to the President of the United States, who granted it and told the harassed lieutenant to come straight to him thereafter. Galley boilers could melt ice for drinking water. The men were issued clothing for every climate on Earth, including suits of fur and the newest miracle material, vulcanized rubber — India-rubber jackets and trousers that must have looked, on deck in a polar fog, like costumes from a book not yet written. The Ordnance Department contributed a genuinely strange weapon designed for the expedition: the Elgin cutlass pistol, a .54-caliber single-shot with an eleven-and-a-half-inch Bowie knife fixed beneath the barrel — a gun for a boarding action against the unknown; about a hundred and fifty were made. There were rockets for signaling and for awe.

And there was the omission. For all the fur and rubber and rocketry, the hulls themselves — the wooden walls that would twice be sent among the icebergs — were never strengthened for ice. No doubled planking, no reinforced bows of the kind whalers gave ships bound for high latitudes. The economies of a decade of budget cuts had to land somewhere, and they landed on the part of the ship that meets the iceberg. Every officer who later stood on the *Peacock*'s deck listening to the pack grind against her sides understood precisely what had been saved, and on whom it had been spent.

The crews were young — Wilkes had wanted them young — three hundred forty-six men and boys, a floating cross-section of the seaport poor, the ambitious, and the restless, leavened with the nine civilian "scientifics" whom the sailors regarded as cargo that talked. The officers included the small circle from the Depot of Charts, among them Passed Midshipman William Reynolds, twenty-three, who had written his sister Lydia that he could not resist volunteering, and who boarded the *Vincennes* still lit with admiration for the commander at the center of it all. And the crew of the *Porpoise* included one cabin boy from Boston with fresh scars under his shirt, keeping a careful distance from the flagship.

On August 18, 1838 — after a decade of almost, after the review without promotions, after final orders came out in the tenders' mailbags — the squadron weighed anchor at Hampton Roads and stood down the channel. The breeze was light; it took until the next morning to clear Cape Henry and discharge the pilots; and then the small fleet was standing out into open sea, six ships in loose company, bound around the world.

Only Wilkes and a few planners knew the full itinerary. The crews had been told the least the service could get away with — destinations doled out a leg at a time, changes of plan not to be questioned. To a sailor this was half mystery and half menace, and it worked on the imagination in both directions: somewhere ahead were palm islands and ice walls, cannibals and sea monsters, riches and graves, in unknown proportion. One of the young officers on the flagship, beginning the government journal that regulations required of him — and, privately, already inclined to keep his real thoughts elsewhere — wrote down the day in words that can still put wind in a reader's hair:

"Let the day be registered & remembered; the Ships are off! We are 'once more upon the water.' Farewell home! Welcome the stirring scenes of strange Lands. May God in his watchful care, preserve us from all dangers, and return us in safety to those, whom we love better than all the things of Earth."

God's watchful care would get twenty-eight of them home short. But that August the sea was fair, the squadron was young, and the largest voyage of discovery the Western world had ever launched was finally, improbably, under way — commanded by a lieutenant, provoked by a man who believed in a hollow Earth, and carrying in its rigging a boy with a grievance and, in its wardroom, a writer with a secret.
