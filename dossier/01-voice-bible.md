# 01 — Voice Bible  ·  [BOTH]

> **Status:** AUTHORED — Fable pass 2, 2026-07-07. Living document; the voice-pass recipe at
> the end is the runnable artifact.
> **Purpose.** The earned-move-vs-reflexive-tell diagnostic: how to keep the prose sounding
> like a storyteller *said* it, not like a machine *assembled* it.
> **Boundary.** This file owns how sentences **sound**; `02-grounding-engine.md` owns what
> sentences **touch**. Where a tic's root cause is an un-rendered image (the curation
> directive above all), the fix order is: ground first per 02, then apply this file.
> **Conventions.** Real lines quoted with act/chapter refs. All rewrites are labeled
> demonstrations — `voice:` commit candidates for Jake's hand, never silent replacements.

## Part I — The earned moves: protect these, and do MORE of them

The style guide names five keepers. The manuscript as written contains seven — two of the
book's best recurring moves have never been codified, which puts them at risk from any
well-meaning later pass. All seven, with the manuscript's own evidence:

**1. The wonder-aside answered across time.** The signature. Best instance in the book — the
bioluminescence (act_2, ch. 9): "We can name it now — dinoflagellates… and the naming
subtracts nothing. But stand where those men stood." Second best: Darwin's atoll theory
directly beneath the sounding lead (act_3, ch. 18), where the aside is *load-bearing science
plus dramatic irony* — the sailors bored by the exact cast that answers one of the century's
great questions. The rule holds (one per chapter, filtered through a witness when possible).
Do more of: the witness-filtered form specifically — *Reynolds wonders, the narrator answers.*
The unfiltered form (narrator lecturing) is where the essay-drift risk lives.

**2. The scale shock.** "Its deeps run down as far as airliners fly up" (act_3, ch. 18); the
*Thunderer* carrying "more force in one hull than the visiting Americans could assemble in
six" (act_2, ch. 11). Rare, physical, modern. Correctly rationed at present. Note for the
novel: scale shocks are a *narrator's* instrument — never put one in a character's head.

**3. Dry irony, never underlined.** The book's deepest reflex and its most trustworthy:
Porter waking from his hanging worried about his ration (act_2, ch. 8); "the ostriches died
aboard; the armadillos were eaten; Reynolds kept a shell" (act_2, ch. 12); "sailors being
sentimental about exactly two things, and the other one is pay" (act_5, ch. 30); "the
discovery of Antarctica was an event the nineteenth century managed to hold several times
without noticing it was full" (act_4, ch. 24). One lapse in 30,000 words — see Part II.5.

**4. The cold stinger.** *Uncodified until now.* The short declarative that ends a movement by
cocking the next one: "The Navy would fix that soon enough." (act_1, ch. 5); "There were three
years left to go." (act_3, ch. 17); "She was eleven days from the end of her story, and no one
aboard the departing ships would ever see her again." (act_2, ch. 15); "It lacks only the
glory." (act_2, ch. 9). This is the book's best chapter-ending technology and it should be
named, protected, and used at the *beat* level in the novel, not only at chapter ends. Budget:
it works because the sentences around it are longer — a stinger needs a body to sting.

**5. The honest shrug.** *Uncodified until now.* The narrator's way of handling unverifiable
self-report without hedge-stacking: "Believe as much of that as you like." (act_1, ch. 6,
on Wilkes' "I never thought of such a thing"). One sentence of attitude replacing three of
"perhaps." This is the correct house style for every contested claim the trial scenes will
raise — the shrug, not the hedge, and never the laundered assertion.

**6. The both-ways portrait.** Rule 11 executed in prose: "This Wilkes was as real as the one
with the spyglass. The tyranny and the rapture were the same appetite" (act_2, ch. 14); "the
officers were right about the men, and Wilkes was right about the coast, and he spent the one
to get the other" (act_4, ch. 23). The move is essential and currently *over-subscribed as a
sentence shape* — see Part II.3 for the metronome audit. The portrait stays; the template
rotates.

**7. Deck-level telling.** "The gunner's daughter was a cannon." / "three feet of half-inch
rope" (act_1, ch. 5); "the rigging grew a hide of ice that came down in sheets when the yards
were braced" (act_4, ch. 21). The full treatment is `02-grounding-engine.md`; it is listed
here because it is also a *voice* fact: the book sounds most like itself when it is touching
something.

**Direct address, audited.** The style guide licenses it sparingly, and the manuscript's
address-proper is within budget and earning its keep: "And so, the voyage began" (preface);
"But stand where those men stood" (act_2, ch. 9); "and you are holding some of them" (act_5,
ch. 31 — the Ship Universal payoff, the best single use of "you" in the book). The problem is
not address. It is the *directive* — the imperative that outsources bookkeeping — which is a
different animal wearing address's clothes. Part II.1.

## Part II — The tics: machine version → storyteller version

Each entry: the manuscript's own line, the diagnosis, and a labeled demonstration fix. These
nine instances (II.1) plus the budget items (II.2–II.6) are the complete audit; everything not
cited here survived inspection.

### II.1 — Curation directives (STYLE_GUIDE tell #1) — the census, and a test  ·  [BASE]

Ten instances. The ruling principle first, because the fixes all follow from it:

> **The directive test.** If the sentence orders the reader to do the book's bookkeeping —
> *remember, hold, mark, file, sit with* something for a payoff that comes later — it dies:
> placement and weight must do that work. If the sentence *enacts its content in the moment
> it is spoken* — the address completes the thought rather than deferring it — it may live,
> charged against the direct-address budget.

The condemned, with demonstration fixes:

1. "Remember that admiration." (act_1, ch. 4) → Delete. The next sentence — "The whole voyage
   lives in the distance between what Reynolds wrote about Wilkes in 1838 and what he would
   write by 1839" — *is* the storyteller version, already present. The directive is
   scaffolding left standing after the arch was built.
2. "That is worth writing down now, plainly, because in eighteen months they would do it
   twice." (act_1, ch. 7) → *"No one proposed to sail ninety-six tons against the Antarctic
   ice. In eighteen months they would do it twice."* The fact, then the fuse. Nothing else.
3. "Pause on him there, on the ride down, because this is the thing his whole journal keeps
   teaching:" (act_2, ch. 11) → Delete the clause; open directly at "There was no photograph
   to take…" The essay that follows is one of the book's finest moments and needs no usher.
4. "Read the whole passage when you build this scene:" (act_2, ch. 15) → Delete unconditionally.
   This is a workbench note to the future novelist, published in the reader's text. (Flagged
   in pass 1; repeated here because it is the one *bug* as opposed to tic.)
5. "Sit with what that did to the men who had sailed in her, and to one man in particular."
   (act_3, ch. 16) → Open the paragraph at "Reynolds had just finished writing the *Sea Gull*
   the finest love letter a sailor ever gave a ship…" The paragraph already sits with it; the
   instruction to sit is redundant grief-stage-management.
6. "Hold the other image instead, the one under the quarrel." (act_4, ch. 22) → *"Under the
   quarrel is the other image:"* — fronted declarative, same pivot, no imperative.
7. "and here the reader meets the whole man in a single decision" (act_4, ch. 23) → *"and the
   whole man is in the decision"* — the observation without the tour-guide.
8. "Remember the name." (act_5, ch. 29, Tarawa) → Delete. The sentence that follows ("this
   book will meet it again in its final pages, in November of 1943") is the licensed
   load-bearing book-meta for this act and does the whole job. Two directives on one atoll is
   an index card stapled to an index card.
9. "But mark the scene, because the law eventually would:" (act_5, ch. 28) → Delete the frame;
   begin at "Of everything Charles Wilkes did in four years…" The payoff sentence *is* the
   marking.

The licensed survivors, argued:

- "The reader may hold the chart in one hand and the ledger in the other; the book's only
  demand is that neither hand be allowed to open." (act_5, ch. 27). This is the book's moral
  contract, stated once, at the exact nadir, and it *enacts* — the reader performs the
  double-ledger in the act of parsing the sentence. It is also the single place the book is
  entitled to say "the book." Keep, and let it be the reason the nine above must go: a
  license this important cannot afford cheap siblings.
- "Stand Charlie Erskine next to that verdict." (act_5, ch. 31). Passes the test — the
  juxtaposition happens *as commanded*, in the same paragraph; nothing is deferred. Keep,
  pending Jake's ratification.

### II.2 — Book-meta over budget (STYLE_GUIDE tell #4)  ·  [BASE]

Style guide: "this book will return to…" at most **twice per act**, at load-bearing joints.
Census: preface 3 (licensed — orientation is a preface's job), act_1: 0, act_2: 2 (at limit,
both earn: "the writer this book leans on," "why this book exists"), act_3: 0, **act_4: 3 —
over**, **act_5: 5 — over**.

Act 4 demonstration fix (the weakest of its three): "Time has ruled, and the reader should
hear the ruling now, so the trial scenes ahead read as tragedy rather than suspense. The
continent is there." → *"Time has ruled. The continent is there."* The placement announces
itself; eighteen words of stage direction removed and the verdict hits harder. (The other
two — "this book states them plainly because a court-martial will" and "Whatever else this
book has to say about the expedition's management" — are stronger; keep both, and the act is
back at budget once the ch. 24 fix lands.)

Act 5 triage (five instances → keep two): keep "this book will meet it again in its final
pages" (Tarawa — the plant the whole 1943 payoff rides on) and "where this book began" (ch.
31, the frame audibly closing). Demote or rephrase: "the machinery this book flagged at the
Río Negro" → *"the machinery the Río Negro had rehearsed at the cost of an evening's
fright"* — the callback without the librarian; "the oldest pattern in this book ran once more
at national scale" → *"the oldest pattern in his life ran once more at national scale"* —
truer anyway: it is Wilkes' pattern, not the book's; "the one form of accounting in this book
that closes clean" → *"the one account in the whole story that closes clean."*

### II.3 — The ledger metronome (STYLE_GUIDE tell #2, at scale)  ·  [BOTH]

The balanced-antithesis shape — *X, and Y, both true, neither yielding* — is the double
ledger's sentence-level signature, and it is firing too often in exactly the acts where the
ledger lives. Census of the explicit form: "Wilkes had the sense to grab him with both hands"
(innocent); "the voyage's two darkest stories were both waiting" (innocent); but then, as
rhetoric: "It has to be written both ways, because both are true and each is a lie alone"
(ch. 27); "pure both-of-him" (ch. 24); "both London and Washington knew it" (ch. 29); "and
both of those clauses go on the stone" (ch. 31); plus the same-appetite family ("the tyranny
and the rapture were the same appetite," ch. 14; "his great flaw, and exactly the quality the
enterprise could not have done without," ch. 16; "he was never only one man," ch. 28).

The two anchors are untouchable: **"It has to be written both ways…"** (Malolo — the thesis)
and **"both of those clauses go on the stone"** (ch. 31 — the account closing; note it matches
the style guide's own banned template, "It was X, and it was Y. Both facts are permanent,"
and survives *as the licensed single instance* because it is the book's final accounting —
the license is the exception that proves the ban). Everything else in the family rotates
through the variation strategies, which are, in order of power:

1. **Scene, then one dry line** — the style guide's own preference, currently underused.
2. **Asymmetry with silence** — give one side the sentence and the other side *nothing*, and
   let the nothing scream: "Malolo's dead have no stone." (ch. 27) is the book's own perfect
   model — four words carrying what a balanced antithesis would have flattened.
3. **Deferral** — balance across pages, not within the sentence; plant the achievement,
   land the cost a scene later (the Tarawa chart → 1943 is this, working at book scale).
4. **Plain figures** — the ch. 31 side-by-side accounting: balance as arithmetic, rhetoric
   withheld.

Budget: the explicit two-hands sentence ≤ 1 per act. (Full anti-repetition discipline belongs
to `06-double-ledger.md`; this entry is its voice-level enforcement arm.)

### II.4 — The two syntactic carriers of verdict-density  ·  [BOTH]

Pass 1 diagnosed verdict-density as the disease the blacklist doesn't name. At the syntax
level it travels in two vehicles, both greppable:

- **The dash-verdict:** an em-dash appending judgment to a completed fact: "— a formula that
  soothed exactly no one and happened to be true"; "— a distinction Wilkes never forgot
  either, and never forgave." Dash density is uniform across the acts (~15–21 per 1,000
  words — it is the voice, not a bug), but the *judgment-appendix* subspecies should run ≤ 2
  per chapter; past that, every fact arrives pre-chewed.
- **The universalizing aside:** "as crews do"; "as terror does"; "as such fights always are";
  "the way you chart any hazard." The move converts instance to law — it is the aphorism's
  small change, and in dense runs it makes the narrator sound like he has seen everything and
  felt nothing. Budget ≤ 1 per chapter, and never two in a paragraph.

Neither is banned — both are house moves. The budgets exist because each is the verdict's
delivery vehicle, and the earning rule from 02 (no verdict more than ~two sentences from
touchable matter) applies to every instance.

### II.5 — Explained irony (STYLE_GUIDE tell #5): one lapse  ·  [BASE]

"There is no evidence the irony landed on Wilkes. It is at least in the record for the rest
of us." (act_5, ch. 30, Peacockville.) The first sentence is the joke, played perfectly dry.
The second sentence taps the reader's elbow. → Delete the second sentence; the style guide's
own rule ("if the sentence after the irony explains the irony, delete the sentence") applies
verbatim. That this is the *only* instance in 30,000 words is itself worth recording — the
restraint elsewhere is the book's spine; protect it.

### II.6 — Paragraph rhythm (STYLE_GUIDE tell #6): one hot spot  ·  [BASE]

Act III runs long uniform blocks — the Tahiti paragraph (ch. 18) is a single ~340-word unit
covering a month, and three consecutive paragraphs in ch. 17–18 open on the same
subject-verb-declarative shape ("The squadron…", "The *Relief*…", "The Pacific…"). The style
guide's rebreak rule applies; the deeper fix is pass-1 grounding (Act III is aerial *and*
rhythmically flat for the same reason — nothing is being touched, so every sentence is the
same kind of sentence). Fix order: ground per 02, then rebreak what remains uniform. One
one-sentence paragraph per chapter of Act III would change its pulse measurably.

## Part III — Certifications: what is NOT broken

Recorded so no later model fixes phantoms. Checked by grep and by read, 2026-07-07:

- **Thesaurus elegance (tell #7): clean.** Zero instances of the banned vocabulary in the
  narrator's voice. ("Myriads of them, like flights of swallows" is Reynolds, quoted — his
  word, licensed.) Do not "elevate" the diction anywhere; the plain-nouns register is the
  book.
- **Not-X-but-Y reflex (tell #3): under control.** Occasional, varied, unpatterned. List
  lengths vary. No action.
- **Hedge-stacking (style guide cut #1): nearly extinct.** One borderline: the triple
  "perhaps" at act_1, ch. 5 ("Perhaps it was Bowditch's fatherly advice… perhaps a poor
  boy's arithmetic; perhaps the simple gravity…") — deliberate anaphora at a genuine unknown,
  arguably the honest shrug in triplicate. Keep or thin to two; Jake's ear decides. No other
  instances.
- **Aphorism quality: high.** The problem named in pass 1 and II.3–II.4 is *density and
  spacing*, never quality. Cull for position, not for craft.

## Part IV — The voice pass (runnable recipe)  ·  [BOTH]

Run on any act or chapter; output is a table of findings with line refs and minimal proposed
edits, delivered as `voice:` commit candidates. Never rewrite wholesale; never touch quotes.

1. **Grep the mechanicals.** Banned vocabulary (tell #7); "this book" / "the reader" (budget:
   ≤2 per act, load-bearing only); imperative openers *Remember / Hold / Mark / Pause / Sit
   with / File* (directive suspects); "both" within rhetorical sentences (ledger shape);
   "as … do/does" and "the way …" (universalizing asides).
2. **Apply the directive test** to every imperative aimed at the reader: enacts now → lives on
   the address budget; defers bookkeeping → dies, and check whether the image beneath it needs
   grounding (02) before deleting the crutch.
3. **Count the budgets per chapter:** wonder-asides ≤1; explicit two-hands ledger sentence ≤1
   per act; dash-verdicts ≤2; universalizing asides ≤1; direct address ≤2 per act.
4. **Run the earning rule** (from 02) on every verdict-sentence: matter within two sentences,
   or ground it, or cut it.
5. **Read three consecutive paragraph openings aloud.** Same shape three times → rebreak one.
6. **Check the certifications first** (Part III) and do not re-litigate them; if a certified
   category shows a *new* violation, fix it and note that the certification needs re-dating.
7. **Deliver findings smallest-diff-first**, quoting the current line exactly, with the act
   and chapter ref, so every fix is a one-commit decision for Jake.
