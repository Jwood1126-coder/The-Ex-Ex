## The rule this file adds

**Telling is a distance, not an altitude.**

The base narrative is allowed to compress time; it is not allowed to evaporate matter. "The
colt was three feet of half-inch rope" is a telling sentence — it covers no scene-time, admits
no dialogue, and it lands on the reader's skin. The one rule ("base tells, novel shows")
governs *pace and mode*, and it has been quietly misread as a rule about *height*. This file
exists to correct that misreading and to make the correction repeatable.

The style guide bans the symptoms of machine prose. It contains no positive rule requiring the
narrator to render the physical world in his own sentences. This is that rule:

> **The narrator owns a body. Quotes are witnesses, not eyes.**

## Part I — Diagnosis: why the prose reads aerial

### 1. The evidence

Measure the manuscript's imagery against its quotation marks and one pattern accounts for
nearly everything (word counts via script; quoted spans of ≥4 words):

| act | words | inside quotes | Charlie/Erskine mentions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preface | 761 | 0% | 2 |
| I | 5,330 | 3.8% | 25 |
| II | 7,841 | **19.6%** | 11 |
| III | 4,822 | 7.8% | 0 |
| IV | 4,422 | **0.0%** | 2 |
| V | 6,378 | 0.3% | 9 — mostly in the codas |

Act II is the most vivid act, and it is the most quoted: the storm crockery, the glowing sea,
the *Sea Gull*'s gale, the Fuegian family — Reynolds' journal was open on the desk, and his
sentences carry the sensory load. Act IV is the most *physical* stretch of the entire voyage —
frostbite, warping, a rudderless ship in closing pack — and it contains not one quoted line,
and it is the most aerial act in the book. The vividness tracks the availability of
transcription, not the demands of the story. **When the quotes run out, the narrator does not
pick up the camera. The prose just climbs.**

This is not an argument for fewer quotes. Reynolds' pen is a *theme* — "the sentence was the
camera" — and Act II proves what the book sounds like when a witness's eyes are working. The
disease is that the narrator's vividness does not survive the witnesses' absence. The two
sections that most need a body — the ice and Fiji — are the two where the transcribed record
ran thin, and the prose visibly runs on verdicts there.

And the second number is the costliest one: **Charlie, the witness whose entire narrative
function is the body, appears zero times in Act III and twice in Act IV** — both positional
("Charlie went south where Charlie always was"), neither sensory. The book's designated
nervous system is offline from Valparaíso to the codas. The flogging in Act I is the most
grounded passage in the manuscript, and it is no accident that it is also the emotional core:
that is what the book feels like when Charlie's skin is in it.

### 2. The three roots

**Root one: the provenance rule has been over-applied to sensation.** The repo's holiest law —
no fact without provenance — governs *events*. Somewhere in the drafting it began governing
*senses*: the only risk-free way to be vivid was to quote a witness, so vividness was
outsourced to the two men with journals, and the narrator's own sentences retreated to
analysis, which needs no source. But what a colt is made of, what frozen manila does to hands,
what a berth deck smells like after four months at sea — these are not events. They are the
period's furniture, as checkable as a ship's tonnage, and the narrator is entitled to them
without a witness's permission slip. Part II builds the license.

**Root two: "telling" has been implemented as "explaining."** Summary can be sensory. A
sentence can cover six weeks and still touch three things. The manuscript already knows this —
"the rigging grew a hide of ice that came down in sheets when the yards were braced" is
summary; "a ship threads a slot between green cliffs and finds herself floating inside a
mountain" is summary — but these deck-level tellings arrive roughly once per chapter, where
the book's ambitions need them once per beat.

**Root three: the verdict-sentence is the voice's home key, and it is self-sufficient.** The
retrospective aphorism — "The Southern Ocean does not audit virtue"; "It was the age, wearing
his face"; "A command whose central symbol is a known fiction is a command holding itself
together by dares" — is the best thing in the manuscript and its most dangerous, precisely
because it needs no world to stand on. Under any pressure (pace, thin sources, a hard
transition), the prose modulates home to the verdict. One verdict per movement is a voice;
verdict as the default texture is an essay. The style guide bans stacking two aphorisms in a
row; it says nothing about *density across a chapter*, and density is the actual problem.

The earning rule, added here as a diagnostic (not a mechanical law — great beats overrule it):

> **No verdict lands more than about two sentences from matter the reader can touch.** If the
> aphorism floats free of anything held, heard, smelled, or hauled, either ground the beat
> beneath it or cut the aphorism. The verdicts are the book's treasure; grounding is how they
> keep their value.

### 3. A tell the blacklist doesn't name

The manuscript's ~10 curation directives ("Remember that admiration," "Sit with what that
did," "Hold the other image," "Remember the name," "mark the scene," "The reader may hold the
chart…") are already banned by STYLE_GUIDE tell #1 — but note *why* they keep firing: **a
curation directive is what a narrator writes when the image has not been rendered strongly
enough to hold the reader unaided.** They are compensations. Ground the beat and the
instruction becomes unnecessary; that is the order of operations (render harder first, then
delete the directive). The full audit belongs to the voice pass (`01`); one instance is simply
a bug and can go now: "Read the whole passage when you build this scene" (act_2.md, the
Fuegian skylarking passage) is a note addressed to the *author of the future novel*, not to
any reader. [BASE]

### 4. What stays

For the record, so no later pass "fixes" the wrong thing: the wonder-aside, the scale shock,
the dry irony, the sparing direct address, and the verdict-sentence itself are the book's
signature and they stay. This engine does not sand the voice down; it puts ground under it.
The fix is earning-distance, never deletion-by-category.

## Part II — The fact-gate: the narrator's license  ·  [BOTH]

Every concrete detail in a grounding pass is one of three kinds. Classifying before writing is
what makes vividness lawful. This gate is the whole trick; everything else in the file is
elaboration.

**[E] Event-facts.** *What happened*: dates, positions, actions, casualties, who was aboard,
what was said. Require provenance (public-domain primary or Jake's transcription), enter the
base only by `fact:` commit, and may be redeployed anywhere once established. The base
narrative is itself a fact-checked reservoir of [E] — grounding often needs nothing new, only
redeployment downward (category → instance).

**[W] World-facts.** *What the period was made of*: what a grating is and where it was rigged;
that manila stiffens when it freezes; that the weather side of the quarterdeck belonged to the
captain; that formal punishment used the cat and all hands witnessed it; that larboard was the
word until 1846. These need *accuracy*, not event-provenance: tag [VERIFY] until checked
against period sources, then use freely in the narrator's own voice. **The base has been
starving itself of [W]. This layer is the unlock.** A [W] detail must still be period-true —
the gate's question is "could any ship of 1840 attest this?" rather than "did this ship log
it?"

**[I] Invented specifics.** *What the record is silent about*: which hand a man used, the
unrecorded thought, the unnamed quartermaster, invented dialogue. **Banned in [BASE], without
exception.** Licensed in [NOVEL] under three conditions: (1) the record is genuinely silent —
[I] never contradicts a source and never fills a gap a read-through could fill; (2) it stays
in character and in period — no anachronistic interiority, no modern vocabulary of mind;
(3) it is flagged in the draft's margin notes until the relevant primary passage has been
checked, so an [I] that turns out to collide with the record dies by commit. Invented dialogue
additionally: never placed in a named person's mouth against their documented character or
whereabouts, and never dressed as quotation from the record — the novel's readers will take
quoted speech as reconstruction, so reconstruction had better be the honest word for it.

**The quote-test** (which quotes keep their jobs). A primary quote earns its place when it
delivers at least one of:
1. **Witness** — that *he* said it is the point. The "foul scamp" entry; "I never thought of
   such a thing"; Porter's ration. Untouchable.
2. **Voice** — period texture no honest narrator could counterfeit: "a bone in her mouth,"
   "God bless you, my darling boy!"
3. **Testimony under the ledger** — words the narrator must not paraphrase because dignity or
   the double ledger requires the source to speak: the slave-ship hold, the submission crawl.

A quote doing none of these — a quote that is merely *scenery on subcontract* — marks a place
where the narrator's own eyes were shut. The test in practice: paraphrase it mentally; if you
lose Reynolds, keep the quote; **if you only lose the picture, the narrator should consider
taking the picture back** — keep the fact, return the words, render it fresh, cite the source
in the commit. (Act II should mostly stand: Reynolds is a character there and the quotes are
witness. But the *dependence* the habit built is what starved Acts III–V.)

## Part III — The anchor taxonomy  ·  [BOTH]

The field kit: six families of anchor, each with period instances this book has already
verified or can cheaply verify. The full warehouse belongs in `03-world-bible.md`; this list
is deliberately short enough to run a pass from. Every instance below marked [W] is offered as
period-general and carries an implicit [VERIFY] until a read-through or reference confirms it;
instances marked [E] are already in the fact-checked base.

**A. The body** — the reader's proxy; the family the manuscript starves most.
Hands: tar, rope-burn, split knuckles in frost, fingernails torn on frozen canvas [W]. Feet:
bare on deck in the tropics, sea boots in ice [W]; ʻaʻā-shredded boots [E]. Back and
shoulders: the colt and the cat [E]; hauling, and the difference between sound-enough-to-haul
and too-worn-to-warm [E — Act IV's own line]. Gut: biscuit and its weevils, salt junk from the
harness cask, the grog ration [W]; Porter's worry for his ration [E]. Balance: everything at a
heel, fiddles on the mess tables, hammocks fourteen inches apart [W — verify spacing];
proprioception is the master sense at sea — a ship's motion should be *felt in the knees*, not
reported as weather.

**B. The ship speaking** — sound, smell, surface, by location.
Sound: bells every half hour; the bosun's calls; pumps clanking; ladders creaking [E — via
Reynolds]; gun-trucks rumbling; holystones grinding at dawn; canvas slatting in a calm;
rigging humming in a blow [all W]. Smell: tar, hemp, and pitch; bilge when the pumps work;
galley smoke; oil lamps below [W — verify lamp fuel]; livestock forward [E — the Río Negro
menagerie]; wet wool and unwashed men — fresh water was for drinking [W]; vinegar between
decks after sickness [W]; powder smoke [W]. Surface: holystoned decks white as bone; brass;
greasy falls; wet manila versus dry [W]. Below-decks geography: berth deck forward and dark,
headroom under six feet [VERIFY for *Vincennes*]; wardroom aft; steerage for the mids; the
camboose [VERIFY term in USN use]; the head at the bows, to weather your misery [W].

**C. Clock and light** — scene-time is watch-time.
The bells and the watches, the dog watches, the middle watch (midnight–4 a.m., the book's
witching hours — the pin lives there) [W]. Hammocks piped up and down; quarters; grog at
fixed hours [W — verify USN times]. Light: the binnacle lamp; battle lanterns; below-decks
perpetual dusk; candle rations [W]. Latitude signatures the book already owns: the Antarctic
summer's no-night [E], the tropics' vertical noon, daylight to after eight at the Horn [E].

**D. Rank and space** — the ship as a stratified world; who may stand where.
Weather side of the quarterdeck: the commander's alone [W]. Going "to the mast" with a
grievance [W — verify USN usage]. Officers aft, men forward, as absolute moral geography; a
boy's stations; where a passed midshipman sleeps versus where a lieutenant does [W]. The
punishment apparatus, since this book keeps needing it: gratings rigged at the gangway, all
hands mustered to witness, seizing by the wrists, the boatswain's mates relieving each other,
the count called aloud, the surgeon standing by [all W — standard practice; verify against
Erskine's own flogging accounts during read-through].

**E. The sea's weathers** — one signature per latitude band, pressing on bodies, never
performing for the prose.
The trades' steady push and flying-fish weather [E]; the doldrums' slatting calms; the
Southern Ocean's long swell — seas that outrun the ship [W]; ice-blink on the cloud over pack,
and water-sky, the dark streaks that mean open leads [W — period polar terms]; fog that beads
on wool [W]; williwaws [E].

**F. The encountered shore** — each landfall has its own sensorium, under the dignity rule.
Peat smoke and kelp and the carried fire of Tierra del Fuego [E — the fire; W — the rest];
Fiji's reef water and its colors, scented coconut oil [VERIFY], the yam trade across the
gunwale at noon [E]; Honolulu's whaling-port crowd and trade-wind rain [W]; Sydney's
coal-smoke Englishness [W]. Rule: a shore's sensorium is made of *human activity* — work,
speech, cooking, music — never of people rendered as landscape. Shore sensation comes from the
record or stays general; ethnographic specifics are [E]-class, never [I].

**The harvest template.** These six families double as the read-through collection scheme.
As Jake reads Erskine and Reynolds, mark passages in the margin: **E** (event — feeds
`fact:` commits and the scene bank), **S** (sensation — feeds this taxonomy and the world
bible, filed by family A–F), **V** (voice — dialogue seeds and period idiom for the novel).
The read-throughs are already the next milestone; with three marginal letters they refuel this
engine at no extra cost.

## Part IV — The grounding pass  ·  [BOTH]

The recipe. Eight steps; each carries a base/novel dial. A later model should be able to run
this on any passage without further instruction.

**0. TRIAGE.** Not everything gets grounded — the base's virtue between landings is speed.
Ground (a) beats where the chapter turns, (b) beats where a cost is paid — death, punishment,
fear, hunger, (c) beats where a verdict-sentence currently carries weight it hasn't earned
(run the earning rule from Part I). Leave the connective tissue aerial; that is what telling
is for. *Base dial: one to three grounded beats per chapter. Novel dial: every scene, by
definition.*

**1. CAMERA.** Whose body is present? Charlie (the body), Reynolds (the pen), a generic sailor
(the forecastle's collective body — usable in base without naming), or the ship herself as
proxy when no witness serves. If the answer in a physical chapter keeps coming back
"nobody's," that is a cast problem wearing a prose problem's clothes — flag it (see the Act IV
note in VI.4).

**2. CLOCK.** Fix the hour and the light before rendering anything else — watch, bell,
mealtime, dark or dusk. *Base dial: one clause. Novel dial: the scene's spine.*

**3. ANCHOR.** Choose the fewest anchors that carry the beat's meaning — never more than three
in base mode. Anchors are load-bearing or they go: the colt's three feet of rope carries the
flogging; the weather that day does not. Vary the family: the manuscript is sight-and-sound
rich and smell-taste-touch starved, so when in doubt, pick from family A or the smell shelf of
B.

**4. GATE.** Classify every candidate detail [E]/[W]/[I] per Part II. [W] gets a [VERIFY] tag
until confirmed. [I] in base mode is refused, full stop — see step 7 for what to do with the
want.

**5. RENDER.** The narrator writes it in his own voice — concrete nouns, exact verbs, the
sailor's vocabulary — inside whatever syntax the mode allows: compressed summary for the base,
scene for the novel. Then run the quote-test on any nearby quotation: keep witnesses, reclaim
scenery.

**6. COST.** Land the grounded beat on what it costs somebody — a body, a fear, a debt, a
relationship. Grounding without stakes is set-dressing. (This is the style guide's
chapter-ending rule, applied at beat scale.)

**7. SETTLE THE BOOKS.** Two ledgers close every pass. *The altitude ledger:* each grounded
beat added pays a tax — one abstraction or verdict nearby is deleted or demoted, so the prose
trades height for ground instead of gaining weight (base target: net growth ≤ +5% per
chapter). *The debt ledger:* every place the pass wanted a detail the gate refused becomes a
written **grounding debt** — what's wanted, which primary probably has it — appended to the
debt list in VI.4. The pass thereby feeds the read-throughs instead of feeding on them.

## Part V — Anti-patterns: how grounding fails

1. **The inventory.** Three sentences of period nouns is a museum case, not a deck. Anchors
   carry meaning or they go; if a detail could be deleted without the beat losing anything, it
   already should be.
2. **Costume detail.** Quaintness for its own sake — hardtack mentioned to prove research was
   done, rather than because someone is hungry *now*.
3. **Anachronism of word or mind.** No adrenaline, no seconds-precision, no trauma-vocabulary,
   no twentieth-century interiority. The period's own physics only. (The wonder-aside is the
   licensed exception, and it is a *narrator's* move, never a character's.)
4. **Purple weather.** Weather exists in this book to press on bodies and ships. If a storm is
   performing for the prose — all spectacle, no cost — cut it back until someone is wet.
5. **Grounding the corridor.** Slowing the connective summary that exists to move months. The
   base is allowed to fly *between* landings; that is the mode working, and it is what keeps
   the grounded beats loud.
6. **Unearned interiority.** Narrator claiming the sensations of a named man the record
   doesn't support — [I] leaking into [BASE]. The generic sailor's body is available for
   exactly this; use the forecastle, not a name.
7. **Sensory monoculture.** All eyes. Force the rotation through the families, especially
   smell and balance — the two the manuscript uses least and a ship supplies most.
8. **The compensating directive.** If you catch yourself writing "hold this image" — the image
   failed. Render harder, then delete the instruction. (Cross-ref `01-voice-bible.md`.)
9. **Grounding the atrocity.** The ledger beats are the exception to everything above. Malolo
   is currently rendered at a deliberate distance — "Speared and clubbed in the shallows,"
   "they crawled" — cost and consequence, never choreography, and that distance is
   *load-bearing*: it is the difference between accounting for violence and serving it as
   spectacle, against people who left no counter-account. **Ground the cost, not the
   killing.** The engine's full sensorium is for the voyage; the ledger keeps its own
   discipline (cross-ref `06-double-ledger.md`).

### VI.1 — [BASE] Immediacy inside telling-mode: the Honolulu gratings (act_5, ch. 28)

**Why this beat:** it is a load-bearing ledger beat — the one act a court-martial ever made
stick — currently handled entirely in legal-argument register ("past the mark," "the legal
ceiling," "the statute"). The chapter's whole downstream payoff (the ch. 31 verdict, "Stand
Charlie Erskine next to that verdict") lands harder if this beat has a body in it. Triage says
ground it.

**Before (current text):**

> The worst: for the core of refusers — men whose contracts were simply *finished*, asking
> only the discharge the law owed them — Wilkes had the ringleaders, as he saw them, seized to
> the gratings and flogged, and flogged past the mark. Twelve lashes was the legal ceiling
> absent a court-martial, written in the statute; men at Honolulu took double and more, by the
> commander's sole order, for the offense of insisting their signed terms meant what they said.

**Demonstration (telling-mode, grounded):**

> The worst: for the core of refusers — men whose contracts were simply *finished*, asking
> only the discharge the law owed them — the gratings went up at the gangway, and all hands
> were mustered to witness, because witness was always the point. A man was seized up by the
> wrists, bare to the waist, and a boatswain's mate laid on the cat while the count was called
> aloud — and at Honolulu the count did not stop at twelve, the ceiling the statute set on a
> commander's own authority. It went past it, to double and more, by the commander's sole
> order, on men whose offense was insisting that their signed terms meant what they said.

**Gate audit.** Every addition is [W] — standard naval punishment procedure: grating rigged at
the gangway, all hands to witness, seizing by the wrists, stripped to the waist, the cat for
formal punishment, the count called aloud [VERIFY each against Erskine's flogging passages and
period practice during the Erskine read-through; if Erskine's own account of Honolulu supplies
specifics, they upgrade to [E] with page cites]. Zero new event-facts: "double and more" is
retained as the sourced figure; no names, no exact counts asserted. Mode check: still summary —
no scene-time, no dialogue, one habitual act rendered as pattern ("A man was…"), which is
telling. **The echo:** "witness was always the point" deliberately repeats ch. 5's flogging
line at three years' distance — a plant/payoff rhyme, not redundancy; Jake's call whether the
motif earns the repetition. **Altitude tax paid:** the demonstration deletes "flogged, and
flogged past the mark" (rhetorical doubling) and lets the rendered count do that work; the
paragraph's existing aphorism ("It answered in the short run, as terror does") now lands on
matter. Net: +36 words for the chapter's heaviest beat.

### VI.2 — [NOVEL] Full scene-grounding: the *Peacock* in the ice (from act_4, ch. 23)

**Why this beat:** the master prompt names the ice epic as compressed-to-summary, and this is
its centerpiece — thirty hours the base gives one (excellent) paragraph. Reynolds was aboard
[E], so the camera assignment is clean, and the base paragraph already supplies a spine of
verified event-facts. **Deliberately not chosen:** the belaying-pin prologue. The pin is the
book's crown jewel and the first thing Jake will draft; a demonstration there would anchor his
own version before he writes it. Demos belong on second-rank scenes (position logged in `12`).

**Window:** the worst hour — the stern blow — through the routine reasserting itself. Third
person, limited to Reynolds. ~550 words.

**Demonstration:**

> The second blow came at the stern, and Reynolds felt it through his boot-soles before he
> heard it — a jolt that traveled up the deck planking into his knees, and then the long
> grinding afterward, wood complaining down the whole length of the ship at a pitch he had
> never heard wood reach. The floe had come in over the quarter on the lift of the swell and
> taken a length of the bulwark with it: a section of rail simply gone, with the boats'
> davits and the spare spars that had been lashed there a breath before, dragging alongside
> now in the slush or under it. Men who had been fending at that station scrambled inboard; a
> petty officer counted heads at a shout; the count came back whole.
>
> There was no wheel to steer by. That was the fact his mind kept walking back to and
> touching, the way the tongue finds a broken tooth. The rudder had been wrenched off its
> fittings in the first hours, the head of it split — he had seen the tiller swing loose and
> useless, like the arm of a man with a broken shoulder — and the wheel stood abandoned
> amidships, a quartermaster still beside it out of habit, or piety, his hands not on the
> spokes. Six hundred and fifty tons of ship was being steered, so far as she was steered at
> all, by her sails and by men's arms: the braces manned without relief, ice anchors carried
> out ahead and the ship warped up to them yard by yard, the hauling lines coming inboard wet
> and freezing as they came, so that the men were gripping ice that had lately been rope.
>
> Below, through the deck, he could hear the carpenters. He had gone down once — a berth deck
> awash to the ankles, dark as a mine but for lanterns, the carpenter's crew and the armorer
> rebuilding the rudder head in water that found its way into everything, men working first to
> the wrists in it and then to the elbows — and once had been enough; the sound was worse for
> knowing. Hammer-strokes, spaced and patient, under the random artillery of the bergs
> somewhere out in the white. The pack ground along the ship's sides, and he gave up looking
> for a comparison; there was nothing in his experience to lend one. The ship was talking to
> herself the whole length of her hull, and what she was saying was arithmetic.
>
> The boats stood swung out and provisioned. He had done that sum too, without meaning to:
> three hundred miles of frozen coast to leeward that no ship had ever anchored on, and the
> nearest help a season away. The boats were not a plan. They were a slower way of drowning,
> with rowing in it.
>
> At the change of the watch the bell was struck as it had been struck every half hour since
> Norfolk, and the men came up from below as if the day were ordinary, and took their stations
> among men who had not left theirs. The cook got a meal to them in the middle of it, salt
> beef eaten standing, mittens off for the minute it took. That, he thought, was the thing no
> one at home would believe: not the ice. The bell.
>
> She came clear on the second day under a scrap of sail, conned out through the last of it by
> voice and eye, and when open water stood plain ahead the ship's company cheered her —
> cheered the old condemned *Peacock*, rot and all — the sound going out flat over the ice and
> coming back from nothing.

**Gate audit** (the teaching part — this is how every novel scene should be built):

- *Event-facts [E], all from the fact-checked base paragraph:* the stern blow carrying away
  bulwarks, davits, spare spars; rudder wrenched from its fittings, head shattered, tiller
  useless; ice anchors and warping by muscle; carpenters and armorer rebuilding the rudder
  below in freezing water and bad light; boats readied, and what boats meant in that sea;
  routine persisting (watches relieved, meals served); conned out under a scrap of sail; the
  cheer; the ship's rot on record; 650 tons; Reynolds aboard for the campaign.
- *World-facts [W]:* a rudderless ship steers by sails; rudder fittings (pintles/gudgeons —
  kept generic as "fittings"); manila freezes stiff; bells struck each half hour; berth-deck
  darkness; salt beef; mittens [VERIFY against the expedition's actual cold-weather issue —
  act_1 records fur and rubber clothing as [E]].
- *Invented-within-silence [I], each flagged, each lawful only in the novel:* Reynolds' deck
  position and everything felt in his body; the petty officer counting heads (no man was lost
  [E]; the counting is invented routine); the quartermaster beside the dead wheel; "broken
  tooth," "broken shoulder," "arithmetic," "the bell" — his unrecorded thoughts, kept inside
  his documented temperament (the man who did exactly this kind of sum on the *Sea Gull*'s
  deck).
- *Two deliberate refusals, which teach more than anything included:* (1) The base's simile
  "like the ship being opened with an axe" is attributed to "men said," and its provenance is
  not yet page-confirmed — so the scene does not reuse it; it renders fresh noise as [I]
  rather than laundering an unconfirmed quote. If the read-through confirms the axe line, it
  upgrades to [E] and belongs in the scene. (2) The draft wanted "larboard bulwark" — the
  period word, irresistible — but the record as held in this repo doesn't say which side, and
  an [I] that a primary source could contradict is not silence-safe. It stays sideless until
  Reynolds' January 24 entry is read. **Both refusals go on the debt list below.**
- *Dialogue:* none quoted, by policy — the record supplies no lines for this scene yet.
  Ambient orders are rendered as reported sound. If the read-through yields real fragments,
  they enter as seeds via `07-scene-bank.md`.

### VI.3 — Drill cards: the smallest units of the pass

Three one-sentence conversions, each demonstrating a different lawful source of ground.

**Card 1 — convert the category to its instance (fuel: [W]).**
*Before (act_3, ch. 17):* "Two months of observatory duty, reprovisioning, and repair, on a

## Addendum (2026-07-07, Jake's directive) — THE LANDSMAN'S EYE

**The rule.** The reader has never been aboard. Every working term, position, and piece
of the ship earns its picture on first use, or it does not appear. Positions are given
in body terms — how high, what is underfoot, what stands between the watcher and the
watched — before any nautical name is allowed to carry them.

**The license.** This is not a modern concession; it is the memoir's own register.
Erskine writes for landsmen and explains as he goes ("in those days sailors wore
tarpaulin hats"; "The colt is a piece of rope about three feet long and half an inch
thick"). The narrator inherits that habit as period voice, not footnote voice.

**Techniques, in order of preference:**
1. **Teach through consequence** — what the thing does to a body: a belaying-pin is
   "set there to anchor the running ropes, and loose in the hand a club."
2. **Apposition gloss** — the picture rides the term in one clause: "eight bells — the
   strokes that end one watch and set the next."
3. **Body-relative staging** — "he was not aloft; he stood on the planks of the deck
   itself, and the commander sat one deck beneath his feet."
4. **Drop the jargon** when the picture works without it ("the break of the
   quarterdeck" → "he turned and walked forward, up the ship").

**Budgets.** One teach-clause per object, once per book (a term re-taught is a term
distrusted); never an encyclopedia detour (STYLE_GUIDE cut #3 governs); scale always
physical and familiar (a forearm, a blacksmith's hammer, thirty paces).

**Infrastructure.** The deck-geography brief (GAP 3) is hereby also reader-legibility
infrastructure: it must record not only where things stood but the plainest true words
for them.
