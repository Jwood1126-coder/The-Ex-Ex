# ACT II — THE ATLANTIC AND THE FIRST ICE
*(August 1838 – April 1839)*

---

## Chapter 8 — The Floating Forest

The first discovery of the United States Exploring Expedition was a tree.

A few days out, the lookouts raised what appeared to be a ship without masts, dead ahead — a derelict, or the hull of a wreck. It resolved, as they closed, into a cottonwood: a whole tree adrift in the open Atlantic, "one hundred and twenty feet long, fourteen feet in circumference," by Charlie Erskine's estimate, so long at sea that barnacles armored it stem to crown. Around and beneath it moved an entire society — dolphinfish, deep-water sharks, shoals of smaller fish schooling in its shade — a floating forest with its own citizens, riding the currents a thousand miles, Charlie guessed, from the mouth of the Mississippi. The scientifics crowded the rails with the sailors; fish were struck and hauled aboard for Peale's brush and Pickering's knife; and the expedition's first specimens went into the first jars. It was a good omen read honestly: the voyage's business would be exactly this — the unregarded thing, looked at properly for the first time.

The squadron settled into the long rhythm of an Atlantic crossing, and the enterprise's paper soul was born the same month. On September 13, the commander promulgated an order from the Secretary of the Navy: every officer to keep a daily journal of occurrences, submitted weekly for inspection and surrendered to the Navy Department at the cruise's end. Private journals were forbidden. On the *Vincennes*, Passed Midshipman William Reynolds obeyed the order — and disobeyed it. He commenced the official record, and he kept writing the other one, the real one, meant for no eyes "but those few at home, who will be kind enough to excuse inaccuracies of thought or expression, & who will feel an interest in the writing, because of their love for him who writes." Without that act of quiet insubordination, the voyage's inner history — nearly everything in it with a pulse — would not exist. The government got its record. We got Reynolds.

Two days later the sea showed the young squadron what it was signing.

September 15, Reynolds' forenoon watch: a glorious following breeze, the ocean all foaming whitecaps, "so beautiful as to charm the gaze of all the Scientifics." Around noon, making more sail to chase a strange brig, a seaman named George Porter was aloft loosing the topgallant sail when a jammed line suddenly came free. The sail dropped, the slack shot upward with the force of filling canvas — and the rope snapped around Porter's neck and took him off the yard. He gave one sharp cry, an animal sound that froze the deck, and then he was swinging above his shipmates, literally hanged, his body tossed with every roll of the ship, his face darkening. The rope had not broken his neck; it had wrapped his jaw and the back of his skull, and he was strangling in the air in front of three hundred men who could see no way to save him — lower the sail and he fell to his death; haul the line and it finished the noose's work. Then a few quick topmen went up the mast and did the sailor's thing: they gathered him in as though taking in the sail itself, and got him to the deck. Half the ship cried out "He is saved!"; the other half answered "He's dead." The surgeon bent over the blackened face, and slowly, horribly, Porter came back — and his first words, upon rejoining the living, were a worry that he might lose that day's ration.

Reynolds sought him out afterward. Porter remembered nothing — only a shaking, no swinging, no gallows. And Reynolds, twenty-three years old, went below and wrote the kind of sentence that announces a real writer has walked onto the stage of a story: "Though the man's life was preserved, the sight was most appalling... Had he died, his death would have been most easy; he would have gone without pain... I always thought hanging and drowning were easy deaths."

He had a point, and the voyage would test it. There were many deaths ahead, and few of them would be easy.

## Chapter 9 — Madeira and the Glowing Sea

The squadron's first landfall seems, on a flat map, like a wrong turn: bound for Rio de Janeiro, they sailed for islands off northwest Africa. The sea has its own roads. A sailing ship rode the great wheel of the Atlantic — down the coast, across with the trades — and the Madeiras sat conveniently on the on-ramp.

They raised Funchal on September 16 — and here the ocean played its first joke on the age before radio: the *Peacock*, which had crossed the same sea at the same time, anchored the next morning, the two ships having traversed an entire ocean within reach of each other and never once met. To the crews' joy an American brig lay in the roads, homeward bound; letters could go back. "Our hearts were full of gladness," Reynolds wrote, and you can feel in the sentence how long four years was about to become.

Madeira itself was the crews' first taste of the world: part Europe, part Africa, part hothouse — volcanic ridges over clear rocky bays, warm air, the whole island terraced and green. To boys from the gray coasts of New England it was another planet at seventy degrees Fahrenheit. The scientifics were off the ships and up the trails almost before the anchors held, hammering at rocks and pressing plants — and here, in miniature, the expedition met its own permanent civil war. The scientists' work lived ashore; the Navy's work — the charts, the surveys, the government's actual purchase — lived on the water. Every day in port would henceforth be a negotiation between the two, refereed by a commander whose sympathies were, inconveniently for the scientists, with the charts.

The island's other export was already famous. Madeira wine had toasted the Declaration of Independence; the founders' cellars had leaned on it because the thirteen colonies could grow no wine-quality grapes of their own; and the wine's signature — its indestructibility — had been discovered by accident when a shipment came home unsold from a long tropical voyage improved by the heat and the rolling. A drink literally made better by exactly what the squadron was about to endure: the officers took the hint, and Wilkes laid in casks of it for the wardrooms. The Madeira would resurface at odd moments for years — most memorably in a tiny cabin at the bottom of the world, in April, after a storm.

They sailed on the 25th for the Cape Verdes, shoal-hunting as they went — for this leg carried the expedition's first working orders. The Atlantic charts of the day were haunted by *vigias*: reefs and shoals reported once, by someone, somewhere, and copied from chart to chart for decades like rumors — each one a maybe that forced every prudent shipmaster into detours. The squadron's job was to sail straight at the maybes. They ran down the reported positions of one phantom shoal after another and found blue water a mile deep, and the hunt itself turned into sport — eyes straining from every masthead for breakers or discolored water, "a spirit of ardour & liveliness among the crew, and all listlessness forgotten." They were disturbing immense schools of flying fish, "myriads of them, like flights of swallows"; Drayton drew what Pickering dissected. Nothing was there, and proving nothing was there was the work: as the expedition would learn, and as its best chronicler later put it, discovery is as much the finding of what does not exist as of what does. Erase a phantom from the chart and you have made every future voyage shorter and safer. It lacks only the glory.

Then, on the night of October 5, off St. Jago, the sea turned itself on.

It began at the bow and spread: a green-gold light in the water, rising in intensity, until the ships sailed in halos of their own making. "Every drop that was tossed up shone from its own light," Reynolds wrote, "and as it fell again into the ocean, diffused around it rings & circles of the same intense glow, the night being black as Erebus." Fish drew glowing lines under the surface; the wake burned white; even the ship's idlers were lured up from below to hang on the rails and wonder. We can name it now — dinoflagellates, single-celled organisms that fire with light when the water moves them, the churn at bow and wake exciting them brightest — and the naming subtracts nothing. But stand where those men stood. Knowledge in 1838 traveled by ship and by book, and most of these sailors had neither; their sea was still stocked with monsters and mysteries in roughly equal measure, and the wise ones held both possibilities loosely. They were exactly the right audience for a miracle: informed enough to marvel, ignorant enough to be humble. The ocean had begun performing for them, and the performance was a promise. There was more ahead — stranger, colder, and not all of it kind.

## Chapter 10 — Mr. Wilkes

Off the approaches to Rio de Janeiro, the lookouts sighted a great man-of-war flying the flag of home. "We saw our own flag floating to the breeze from the mizzen peak of that magnificent specimen of naval architecture," Charlie wrote — the razee frigate *Independence*, flagship of the Brazil Squadron, wearing the broad pennant of Commodore John B. Nicolson, one of the most senior officers afloat. As the exploring squadron came up, the *Independence*'s band manned the rail and played "Hail Columbia" across the water: the courtesy of the service, a greeting and an honor. Custom called for an answer — a salute of guns from the arriving ships.

Wilkes did not fire.

He had a reason, and it was even a good one: the squadron's chronometers and magnetic instruments, calibrated over painstaking weeks in Virginia, could be thrown out by the concussion of a salute, and the entire scientific mission rode on those calibrations. But Wilkes, being Wilkes, did not trouble to send the reason across with his compliments. What Nicolson saw was a lieutenant snubbing a commodore, and what followed was a correspondence — stiff notes pulling back and forth across a few hundred yards of Brazilian water — in which the commodore's side observed every regulation of address, which meant his letters went to *Lieutenant* Wilkes, commanding the Exploring Expedition. Mister Wilkes. Not Captain, because he was not one; that was the regulation, and no shot fired at the expedition in four years ever went home harder. Wilkes answered with volleys of self-justification, wounded and vigorous, defending an explanation no one had asked him to withhold in the first place, and the wardroom watched its commander spend days of the expedition's time on a quarrel about a courtesy.

It is a small episode, and it is the whole man. The insult existed almost entirely inside Wilkes — Nicolson had used the only address the regulations allowed — but Wilkes lived in the gap between the command he held and the rank he'd been promised, and anything that touched the gap touched the nerve. The squadron was learning what Charlie Erskine already knew, and the telescope confirmed it: for since Madeira, the men had begun to notice their commander on the flagship's deck, watching the other ships — watching *them* — through a spyglass, as if fidelity were a thing you could catch in the act of failing. The friendly commander of the Depot and the shoals was dissolving, sea mile by sea mile, into something warier and colder. Command isolates every captain; it was doing something faster than isolation to this one. He had begun lashing out at officers over trifles — Reynolds' journal marks the squalls — and the crews adjusted as crews do: they charted him, the way you chart any hazard, and gave the reef a wider berth.

## Chapter 11 — Rio

Rio de Janeiro in 1838 announced itself the way it still does — as scenery committing excess. The squadron worked in past the Sugarloaf into a bay "more than one hundred miles in circumference," in Charlie's words, "sprinkled here and there with evergreen islands," ringed by granite peaks with the city pooled at their feet. Reynolds had been here before, as a boy on his first cruise, and the return undid him a little: "'Tis the same as ever: the same enchanting beauties, the same wondrous prospects, the same sublimity of scenery that charmed my young heart some eight years ago are now around me, fresh and unchanged."

The anchorage was a world's fair of navies. Towering over everything lay HMS *Thunderer*, a British ship of the line — ninety guns, a thousand men, more force in one hull than the visiting Americans could assemble in six. Britain's navy was still double the size of America's, and the *Thunderer*'s people knew their history: across the water of an evening came songs celebrating British victories over the young republic — the *Chesapeake* and the *Shannon* — serenades that, Charlie noted dryly, "aggravated the American patriotism." The Americans swallowed it with the compensating thought that their fathers had beaten this navy anyway.

And there was the other traffic. Brazil was the largest importer of enslaved Africans on Earth — millions landed across the centuries of the trade, many times the number ever brought to North America — and the bay held slave ships as routinely as it held merchantmen. Charlie went aboard one, a captured brig under British guard, and was on deck when they opened the hold. "Such a cloud of steam and such horrible smell issued that it staggered everybody on deck," he wrote. "They found only thirty living human beings out of three hundred. This was only one of the many horrors of the African slave-trade." Thirty of three hundred. Charlie — a boy whose own back had been opened by lawful authority — wrote it down plainly and did not look away, which is more than can be said for most of the wardroom; Reynolds, sighting a slaver at sea alongside the *Peacock*, spent his ink that day on the American ship's clumsy appearance. What the century found remarkable, and what it didn't, is its own kind of record, and this voyage keeps both ledgers.

The expedition made its base on Enxados Island in the great bay — observatory ashore, the leaking *Peacock* under repair, the scientifics loosed on a landscape so rich that new species are still being described from those mountains today. The junior officers, meanwhile, discovered the local sport: the peaks.

A party including Charlie climbed the Sugarloaf itself — legend on the waterfront held its summit had been reached only once, by an Englishman who planted his country's flag "and was never seen afterwards." The climbers reached the top after dark and spent the night there, lashed together with lead-line "so that none of us could roll off while asleep," and woke among pitcher plants, purple as morning-glories, each little vessel dripping "drop after drop... from its mouth."

Reynolds chose the other giant. Corcovado — the "hunchback," the sheer granite peak that today carries Christ the Redeemer and then carried nothing at all — rises more than two thousand feet in a single gesture over the city. Reynolds set out on horseback with a companion the journal calls Old Whittle, following the colonial aqueduct into the hills — "the road was smooth and green, and we galloped lightly along, the horses' feet unheard on the soft turf, and the songs of birds and the murmuring of the falling water making sweet music to our ears" — then up switchbacks steep as staircases, until the trees quit and the summit stood revealed in its whole intimidating immensity. They came out on top of the world: a bare uneven platform of rock, every edge but the path falling away toward the sea. They tethered the horses to old iron stanchions and looked, and Reynolds reached for language and admitted defeat as beautifully as anyone ever has: "The ocean, the bay in all its windings, formed a panorama the glorious magnificence of which is not in the power of the mind to conceive or the tongue to tell."

Then he noticed the flag.

Below the main summit, across a sharp chasm some twenty feet deep, lay a lower, lonelier platform — smooth-walled, hand-holdless, guarded by the two-thousand-foot drop — and on it, a staff flying the tricolor of France. Someone had gone down there for no reason on Earth except that it was hard. Reynolds absorbed this and arrived immediately at the correct American response: "It was evident that some Frenchman had crossed over, and why should not we!" He had no flag. He had a handkerchief, and the intention of tying it *above* the French colors.

What he and Whittle also had was horse tack. They stripped the saddles — cruppers, stirrup leathers, girths — and knotted them into a rope, made one end fast to a stanchion, and dropped the other down the chasm wall. Reynolds appraised his own engineering with the honesty that makes him worth following anywhere: "these made a very precarious rope and we had some misgivings as to the result of the adventure. If the leather did not break and we succeeded in the descent, we were not so certain of our ability to get up again, a process which was likely to resemble the crawling of a fly up a wall."

"'Nick or nothing,' here goes" — and he went over the edge. "The instant I found myself fairly hanging by it, my confidence in its strength vanished entirely, and the thought of the 2,000 feet below me was somewhat distressingly intrusive."

The leather held. The two of them stood on the far platform in the last hour of the day and were paid in full: the sun going down into the ocean in "golden glory" while in the east a full moon rose, "whitening the mountains, the city and the bay." Reynolds, drunk on it, added the sentence that tells you he was twenty-three: "If Earth can afford another prospect like this, I would travel for a year to see it — but I would take someone with me, who would not be a man!" They hauled themselves back up "by the exercise of sheer strength of arm and agility," and rode down the mountain through a moonlit landscape he compared to fairyland.

Pause on him there, on the ride down, because this is the thing his whole journal keeps teaching: what such a moment *was* in 1838. There was no photograph to take, no one to show, no way to keep any of it except memory, words, or a sketch — the scene existed once, for those present, and then only as well as they could tell it. Beauty lay in its novelty and its evaporation. That is why these men wrote the way they wrote; the sentence was the camera. It is also, frankly, why this book exists.

The squadron's Rio interlude ended with two footnotes in a minor key. An hour before departure a ship's boy named Henry Hughes went over the side of a dinghy in a bid to desert — and surfaced under the waiting grip of an officer's hand at the moment, of all moments, when he must have believed himself free. Reynolds recorded it with a wince of distant sympathy and no further comment; the Navy's comment may be imagined. And on the night of January 6, 1839, repairs complete, the squadron stood out of the great bay under a farewell of its own devising — rockets, blue lights, signal guns — the sky over Rio briefly full of American fire. They were bound south now, and every man aboard could do the arithmetic of the coastline: south of Rio there was one more errand, and then the Horn.

## Chapter 12 — Río Negro

The errand was a survey — the mouth of the Río Negro, on the desert coast of Patagonia, roughly halfway down the long slide from Rio to the Cape. It should have been routine, and instead it produced the expedition's first call to arms — over an enemy that did not exist.

The approaches were miserable: shoal water, a tide that rose and fell twelve feet and ran like a river, the sea stained brown with stirred riverbed. In a heavy squall off the land, the schooners grounded; boats went to their aid; and in the evening, with a survey party — Ringgold, several officers, the scientifics — aboard the *Sea Gull* inside the river, a signal was misread in the worsening light. The squadron understood: *the natives are attacking.* The ships boiled over. Within minutes five boats full of armed men, cutlasses out, were pulling hard for the shore to rescue a landing party that did not know it needed rescuing — until Wilkes sorted the signals and called them off.

Ashore, meanwhile, the local population was living the same nightmare in reverse. The settlement had taken the strange squadron for *French* warships — France was then blockading Buenos Aires, and a descent on the coast was a live fear — and had reacted as people do: pilots withdrawn from the river, the militia under arms, women and children sent fleeing into the interior. Two groups of armed and frightened human beings had spent an evening preparing to defend themselves from each other's defensive preparations, and only luck and daylight kept the guns cold. When the flags were sorted out, relief was general and the hospitality real — the governor sent gifts of the country, ostriches and armadillos among them (the ostriches died aboard; the armadillos were eaten; Reynolds kept a shell) — and the Americans traded cutlery and compasses for guanaco skins while the scientifics filled their crates.

Where great ships meet small settlements, both sides arrive pre-frightened. Each has heard what the other is capable of; everyone's prudence looks like everyone else's aggression; and once the first retaliation lands, escalation does the rest. At the Río Negro the crossed signals cost nothing but an evening's terror, and everyone got to laugh about it over ostrich and armadillo. The voyage would meet the same arithmetic again in a warmer sea, and no one would be laughing.

Reynolds, surveying the coast, banked one more image against the cold to come — two gaucho horsemen coursing the dark beach, "horse and rider in clear relief against the sky... How I wished for a sketch of the fierce and mobile features and graceful costume of the men, and of the Arab-looking steeds" — and then had his own brush with the coast's temper: caught by the falling tide in a rising sea, his boat nearly trapped between breakers and shore until a gun from the ship called him off, and he came back aboard with the boat half-full of water and a conclusion for the day that any sailor of any century would countersign: "It is the most comfortable feeling in the world, when a man steps from danger into the midst of companions and security."

Wilkes weighed the survey against the calendar — the southern summer was burning down, and the Horn does not wait — cut the work short, and turned the squadron south. The weather grew teeth on cue. Reynolds' journal from those days is a portrait of a ship as a shaken instrument: seas that smashed the crockery wholesale, nights when sleep was impossible in a bedlam of "the tramping and voices of men, the bleating and grunting of the livestock, the workings of the masts and guns, the creaking of the ladders, the howling of the winds," dinners eaten in "agony for fear of broken dishes and lost mouthfuls and of becoming the recipient of your neighbor's plate, contents and all." Storm clothes were served out. Warm gear came up from the holds. Every man aboard knew what the issue of fur meant about the latitude ahead.

## Chapter 13 — The Horn

Cape Horn had been the sailor's synonym for dread for two centuries, and it earned the reputation honestly. At the bottom of South America the Atlantic and Pacific meet with nothing to referee them — two oceans' weather compressed through a gap between the continent's last rocks and the ice, breeding waves and williwaws with a violence found nowhere else on the planet, over a seafloor salted with the ships that had tried before. The land it guards is its accomplice: black, contorted, treeless rock, a coastline that looks less designed than *shattered*. Reynolds, running the Strait of Le Maire at twelve and thirteen knots with wind and tide astern — "a passage dreaded by so many navigators" — described Staten Island to starboard as resembling the coast of Rio "but infinitely more contorted and confused in its features. It seemed as if it had been thrown up by an earthquake but the night before": snow on the summits, dark scrub in the ravines, outlying rocks scattered offshore "like vessels under sail." Even Cook — *Cook* — had pitied anyone drawn to the lands south of here. It is the sort of landscape a modern reader reaches for science fiction to describe — another, colder planet, and the squadron spent those weeks sailing along its coast.

The men braced accordingly — and the Horn, having read their expectations, declined to meet them. Signs of the south came first as wonders, not blows: the old escort of albatrosses joined by new company, penguins and shags ("the former dive, the latter fly much like ducks" — Reynolds, ever the note-taker); daylight stretching until after eight and returning by half past three, "so darkness has a short reign"; the thermometer at forty and fifty degrees in the height of summer. Then, on a morning watch, the thing itself: "Cape Horn was in full view, about twenty-five miles ahead — the weather mild, the sky clear, the sea smooth." Mild. Clear. Smooth. The oldest Cape Horners aboard testified they had never seen its like, and Reynolds logged the miracle with a sailor's precise suspicion: "How long this will continue I know not. Changes occur here like lightning — quick and often unexpected."

For one of them the doubling of the Horn landed as a private anniversary. "This day seven years ago — I made my debut on the ocean," Reynolds wrote on February 17, 1839, "and now I am off Cape Horn. Off Boston the weather was freezing, and here at this time it is mild and summer-like. Seven years!!!!" Four exclamation points, from a man who rationed them. He was twenty-three, a third of his life already given to the sea, rounding the most feared headland on Earth in shirtsleeve weather. The sea does occasionally pay in advance. It was, as he suspected, an advance.

On February 19 the squadron stood into Orange Harbor — a snug, landlocked anchorage on Hoste Island, hard by the continent's absolute tip — and found the plodding *Relief* and both schooners already there, the storeship's people dead asleep in open daylight, "instead of the usual stir and bustle of a man-of-war's deck... the deathlike silence of sleep. All hands were at their rest, that rest which they needed so much." The Vincennes' crew got their own first still night in weeks — "Oh! how sweet was my sleep" — in water smooth as glass, the air summer-warm, birds all around "in fancied security." (The fancy was Reynolds' joke at his own expense: he shot a penguin for the collection, and watched a passing canoe calmly pick it up and carry it off, the finders declining to recognize the shooter's claim.)

The canoe was the other introduction. Its occupants were Fuegians — Yámana people of the channels, the most remote human society any man aboard had encountered: a family entire in a small bark canoe, father, mother, a half-grown boy at the paddles, three infants "coiled away on a bed of grass" amidships, and — the detail that stopped every sailor who saw it — a fire burning aboard, on a bed of mud in the canoe's belly, carried across the freezing water like a household god. They had visited the *Relief* and been given old clothes, which they wore in combinations all their own; they declined to board the flagship, the father signing that he had far to go. Reynolds took them in with his usual double vision — the period's reflexive disgust at their bareness and dirt fighting, sentence by sentence, with plain observation that kept landing on respect: the children bright-eyed and animated, the man's face "intelligent... and rather a pleasant one," his voice musical, his manner without a particle of fear, "evidently an old visitor to ships." Here were people wintering, nearly naked, in a country that had the best-equipped expedition in American history huddled in rubber and fur — and every honest man in the squadron registered the comparison, whatever his vocabulary did with it afterward.

And it was here, at the anchorage, that the voyage's veil finally lifted. "No sooner had we cast anchor," Reynolds wrote, "than the veil of mystery regarding our movements was withdrawn, and all hands went to work as if life and death depended on their exertions."

The squadron was not wintering at the Horn, and it was not turning for the Pacific islands. It was going *south* — now, at the raw end of the season, into the ice that had turned back Cook. All hands understood at once why the work felt like life and death. It was.

## Chapter 14 — First Ice

Wilkes split his force four ways, and the plan had the clean audacity of a man drawing on a chart in a warm cabin. The *Relief* would carry the scientifics on a specimen-hunting cruise toward the Strait of Magellan — safest duty, to the scientists' loudly expressed disgust. The flagship *Vincennes* would hold Orange Harbor as base. And the remaining vessels would make two thrusts at the far south: Wilkes himself taking the *Porpoise*, with the *Sea Gull* under Lieutenant Robert Johnson, southeast toward the Peninsula and the South Shetlands, while the *Peacock* and the *Flying Fish* — the latter under Lieutenant William Walker, twenty-something and about to become briefly immortal — drove southwest for the waters where Captain Cook had set the mark, the farthest south of the age, before a wall of ice turned even him around, declaring he would not envy the man who tried to go beyond it.

Late February, in unstrengthened wooden ships, with the southern summer already dying: it was a token effort and Wilkes knew it — a reconnaissance, a down payment on the real attempt promised for the following season. The ice did not grade on intent.

Both divisions crossed the Antarctic Convergence — that invisible frontier where the water abruptly chills and the whole ecosystem changes managers, new birds, new life, the sea itself announcing a different country — and then came the bergs. The first icebergs stand outside every witness's power of preparation; the men had heard of them all their lives and were staggered anyway. And here the voyage produced one of its genuinely confounding moments: Wilkes — insecure, raging, spyglass-wielding Wilkes — met the most hostile seascape on Earth and *fell in love*. "I have rarely seen a finer sight," he wrote among the bergs off the Peninsula. "The sea was literally studded with these beautiful masses. Some of pure white, others showing all the shades of opal, others emerald green, and occasionally here and there some of a deep black, forming a strong contrast to the pure white." Off a shore where two hundred bergs crowded a single panorama, Ringgold surveyed the scene and pronounced, "This is adventuring with boldness!" — and Wilkes, delighted, named the nearest islands the Adventure Islands. This Wilkes was as real as the one with the spyglass. The tyranny and the rapture were the same appetite — a man starved for significance, gorging wherever he found it: in a title, in a grievance, in a wilderness of opal ice that asked no promotion of the men who marveled at it.

The sea took its first man of the campaign almost immediately. On the *Peacock*'s southern run, William Stewart, captain of the maintop, fell from aloft — glancing off the rigging on the way down — into water cold enough to kill on its own. His sea boots kept him afloat just long enough; a bowline was flung and looped "over his exploring boots and he was drawn on board... but it was a narrow escape, for a boat could not have lived in such a sea." The fall had broken him inside. "Poor William died soon afterward, and his body was committed to a watery grave." The expedition's first death: not ice, not battle, not scurvy — the ordinary sailor's death, gravity and rigging, transplanted to the least ordinary sea on Earth.

The southern probes ran out of season together. Wilkes' division pushed down the Peninsula's flank to about 63°30′ S before gales and closing ice forced him out; storms on the retreat drove them to shelter in Good Success Bay, where a landing party went ashore — and the weather then locked the ship out of her own bay for the better part of a week, the boats unable to live in the surf, the men on the beach in sight of food they could not reach. "For four days the gale did not abate," Reynolds recorded of the episode, "and the food, though in sight, could not be reached! Hungry and wet, exhausted with the cold and exposure... their sufferings were great, almost greater than they could bear." When the sea finally relented and the survivors were fed and recovered, the ships crawled back toward Orange Harbor with the season's plain verdict aboard: the Antarctic could be visited, at this time of year, in these ships, only on the Antarctic's terms.

Except that one vessel had not gotten the message. The little *Flying Fish* — ninety-six tons, the smaller of the two New York pilot boats, a harbor schooner sent to the end of the world — had lost company with the *Peacock*, kept going alone, and on March 22 fought her way to seventy degrees south: a long day's sail short of Cook's immortal 71°10′, in a vessel a fraction the size of Cook's ship, through weather her own log called tremendous. The far coast she nearly reached carries her memory now — Cape Flying Fish, above the Walker Mountains, schooner and skipper fixed to the map of Antarctica. When she finally beat back into Orange Harbor on April 11, she had been given up by some as lost, and she came in carrying the expedition's first genuine feat — a record run by its most laughable asset. The squadron went wild for her. The season's lesson now had a codicil, and it was pure Ex. Ex.: the ice made no exceptions for size, but it apparently *could* be argued with by the sufficiently stubborn — a finding the commander filed away for January, with a continent's worth of consequences.

## Chapter 15 — The Launch

While the ice divisions were away, the expedition's most dangerous assignment of the season belonged to its smallest command. Lieutenant James Alden was given the *Vincennes*' launch — an open boat thirty-five feet long, eight in beam, five in depth — with ten men, a month's provisions, surveying instruments, and orders to chart the drowned labyrinth of coastline around the tip of Tierra del Fuego. Alden chose Reynolds as his second. Even Wilkes, handing down the orders, called the mission "a hazardous one!" and issued a sheaf of precautions — which is roughly the point at which a modern reader checks the arithmetic: an undecked boat, in the waters that had just schooled the squadron's ships, along a coast that was not so much a coast as a debris field of rock and channel and tide, unmapped because everyone before them had possessed the sense not to try. In hindsight the mission looks nearly suicidal. In the event, it made Reynolds the writer this book leans on, because for twenty-five days he was cold, terrified, overworked, and utterly awake, and his journal caught fire.

The country got into him first. Rambling alone along a beach "where most likely white man had never trod," gun in hand, he stopped pursuing birds and simply stood in it: "The birds and I were all that savored of life there. I looked upon the giant and rugged hills, the dashing waterfall, the gloomy woods, the glimpses of ocean in the distance and the placid, lake-like water at my feet; but there was no busy city, no hum of men; only screaming birds, my quiet self, and my noisy gun." And then the location stamp, half thrill and half homesickness: "Now we are in the midst of a rugged scene, some six or eight miles from Cape Horn! — our home! our friends! where were they!"

The people of the country came next, and the launch's encounters were of a different temperature than the squadron's formal contacts. Off Lennox Island and in the channels, canoes came alongside; trades were made (a knife for a dog; red flannel that the recipients "immediately converted into a sort of head gear, neglecting the body entirely"; ship's medals, brass rings, raw pork — accepted eagerly — against otter skins and slings); and at one anchorage a party of young Fuegian men and boys, six to perhaps twenty-five years old, spent a day with the boat's crew that Reynolds recorded with unguarded delight: "They were lively, and we skylarked among the snow together, as if we had been old friends. They were naked and we warmly clad, and I just thought that we presented as wide a contrast of person and habit as could be met with anywhere in the world." They were mimics of genius, repeating English "with singular exactness and joining in the songs and dances of the men with a perfect observance of note and time." He noted their weapons (whalebone-tipped spears, bows, slings), their single cooking method (coals), their fastidious horror at American whiskey and seasoned food — a disgust, he owned, exactly mirroring what the sailors felt toward Fuegian delicacies. And then, warmed by the finest day of cross-cultural play in the whole four years, he sat down and reasoned himself back into his century, concluding that he was thankful to belong to "a more artificial community," and would "waive forever" the notion that those with the fewest wants led the more enviable life. Read the whole passage when you build this scene: the day argues one thing, the summary argues another, and the space between them is the nineteenth century, caught in the act.

(The squadron's own mascot from this coast deserves his paragraph. At Orange Harbor a Fuegian man attached himself to the *Porpoise* with perfect self-possession, accepted the full 1830s makeover — warm-water wash, shave, haircut, sailor's rig — dined with officers and men "behaving with perfect propriety, for he imitated the manners of those around him without an effort," ate prodigiously, was mystified by sails, delighted in the galley fire, answered to the crew's christening of "Jim Orange" — and then one day departed without ceremony or farewell and never returned, having extracted everything of interest from the American navy and rendered nothing about his own world in exchange. The anthropological ledger of the encounter, fairly audited, runs entirely in Jim Orange's favor.)

The survey work itself was a grind of tides, squalls, and sounding lines, and its crowning act was a climb. Back in ship company but unsatisfied with a chart he knew to be "all wrong," Reynolds went up a thirteen-hundred-foot summit with Alden and men from the crew to sketch the archipelago from above — compass and spyglass work, racing a storm visible in the southwest. What he got, in a few minutes of hard drawing, was a better idea of the coast than the expedition had possessed; what he *saw* stayed with him for life. Among summit rocks that stood "like tall pillars twenty or thirty feet in height... rearing their heads as if they were spectators," he wrote: "We sunk to dwarfishness beside them... We were awe-stricken and humbled into fear and wondering, and our thoughts turned to the power that made and created all things." Below and beyond lay the whole theater of the voyage in one look — "that great ocean, whose bounds are not known, the icy barriers of which have never been passed... for once undisturbed... no sail to be seen on the watery waste" — and, starting from his very feet, "the bold and terrible promontory Cape Horn, thrusting its rugged form far into the ocean, the last, lingering point of the great western world."

Then the sea presented the bill. The launch's return leg fell into the path of a full gale, and Reynolds rode it out aboard the *Sea Gull* with the launch towing astern — on the deck of the little schooner, in the worst weather of the season, with his notebook.

What he wrote there is the finest sustained passage in his journal, and among the finest in the literature of small ships. The *Sea Gull* — 110 tons, a New York pilot boat, "decreased to a cock-boat" in seas that ran like moving hills — rode the gale with her helm lashed and her foresheet flat, no hand touching her, and rode it perfectly. "A landsman, could he have woke to such a scene, would have deemed his hour come. He would have put no faith in the wood and iron in such a chaos of the elements." But the schooner did not need faith; she had form. "So perfectly was the Sea Gull modeled, and so well balanced were all her qualities... that with her helm lashed a-lee... without a human being to aid or control her, she rode... It seemed much more natural to think that she had a mind, an instinct, a will of her own, and that guided by it, she defied the threatening dangers of the gale. She seemed endowed with human sagacity." Two figures held the deck through the dark hours — Reynolds and a New Zealand Māori sailor named John Sac — watching mountains of water heave up around them, and, in the glimpses between squalls, watching the launch astern "struggled on top of a foaming sea."

The launch lost her fight. Her lines parted once and were doubled at desperate risk — getting a man aboard her in that sea was "an operation of extreme difficulty and danger" — and then, as the gale ground on, she began taking water faster than her emptiness could shed it. "The launch sheered wildly about as the seas struck her... In fifteen minutes she was more than half full, and rising on the crest of an enormous wave, the poor old boat capsized as it melted away from her, and she was a wreck." All hands came up to watch her go — the boat that had carried twelve of them through twenty-five days of that coast — cut loose at last, dwindling astern, "every second she grew smaller," gone. Reynolds' obituary for her, entered later with the salt still on it, catches the exact mixture of sailor's sentiment and sailor's arithmetic: he would always remember his days in her "with much of interest and pleasure," would volunteer at once for her successor — "but I trust not quite so far as to the bottom of the sea."

The storm blew itself out, and the *Sea Gull* ran for home over a sea turned suddenly theatrical — full moon, silver flood, island after island flying past, the schooner with "all her snowy wings abroad" and "a bone in her mouth," her wake glowing with the same phosphorescence that had enchanted the squadron off Africa, "marking her rapid track over the moonlit sea." In the quiet of Orange Harbor the whales came so close at night that Reynolds feared for the boats at the booms, the harbor resounding with breathing "loud and shrill as the whistling of a tempest," huge black forms heaving out of moonlit water. Even the shore had teeth to show: the fearless foxes of the country that raided the observatory camp had never learned men, and did not run from them — "they gave battle without turning to flee and were shot down without trouble, because they had not the instinct to run away" — a sentence that reads, on this voyage, less like natural history than prophecy.

When the *Sea Gull* stood into the anchorage on April 5, the squadron's glasses found her deck and then swept astern for the launch, and found nothing, and the watching officers spoke the dead men's eulogy aloud — "there is no launch there! the poor fellows are gone!" — until someone made out the launch's crew crowded alive on the schooner's deck, and the eulogy dissolved into a mobbing, the returned "almost torn to pieces by the hearty shakings of the hand and overwhelmed with innumerable questions asked in a breath but not answered so readily." Days later the *Flying Fish* came in from seventy south to a hero's welcome of her own. The squadron was nearly whole; the season's ledger read one man and one boat lost against a coastline charted and a record nearly matched; and the ships turned to preparing for the Pacific — the winter's warm work among the islands — while the far south froze itself shut behind them for the year.

Nearly whole. The *Relief* had never appeared — off nearly wrecking herself among the western channels, as the squadron would learn, in an epic of bad luck that would end with her dismissal from the story — and so, when the squadron sailed on April 17 ("without regret, just two months after we had entered it" — Reynolds, closing out a country he called "sufficiently dreadful"), the little *Sea Gull*, the schooner with a soul, the best sea boat any of them had ever known, was ordered to remain behind with her sister to wait ten days for the storeship, then follow on to Valparaíso.

She was eleven days from the end of her story, and no one aboard the departing ships would ever see her again.
