# ACT V — THE PRICE OF THE CHART
*(May 1840 – June 1842, and after)*

---

## Chapter 25 — Fiji

Every ocean of the age had one archipelago that sailors used to frighten each other, and the Pacific's was Fiji. The reputation ran on two rails. The first was navigational: hundreds of islands netted together by reef systems of murderous intricacy, barely charted, strewn with the bones of ships — the finest bêche-de-mer and sandalwood grounds in the Pacific, reachable only by threading a maze that ate the threaders. The second was human, and the forecastle version of it was simple: the Fijians killed shipwrecked and stranded white men, and ate them. The forecastle version, for once, was not pure invention — cannibalism was real in the islands, embedded in war and rite, and the beach-traders' testimony of it, however embroidered, had a documentary core. Charlie and his shipmates went in carrying two years of accumulated stories, and the stories had teeth.

What the stories left out was that Fiji was also a *civilization* — kingdoms in full political motion, thousands of people, great chiefs playing great games, into which the squadron now sailed as one more piece on somebody's board. And the board already had white pieces on it. Scattered through the group lived the beachcombers — deserters, castaways, and traders' men gone native by choice or shipwreck — and the first among them was a Nantucketer named David Whippy, jumped ship years before, now a man of standing at Levuka: fluent in the language, married into the islands, trusted by chiefs and shipmasters alike, the indispensable hinge between two worlds that could barely see each other. Wilkes had the sense to grab him with both hands. Whippy became the expedition's pilot, interpreter, and walking intelligence file, and the difference between the Fiji survey succeeding and the Fiji survey becoming a four-ship casualty list is owed in no small part to a man whose original contribution to American maritime history was desertion. The Pacific kept producing these figures — men who had fallen off the edge of one world and become load-bearing in another — and the Ex. Ex., official to its buttons, ran on them everywhere it went.

The expedition's first formal business, in May, was diplomatic: at Levuka on Ovalau, Wilkes concluded commercial regulations with Tanoa Visawaqa, the old king of Bau, most powerful chief of the group — terms of trade, protection for American vessels, the standard architecture — with the king's formidable son Seru, whom the world would later know as Cakobau, watching from the rank just behind the throne. A British surveying ship, Belcher's *Starling*, lay in the same waters on the same errand for the same reasons. The Pacific was filling with flags.

Then the squadron dissolved into boats, and for three months Fiji became the hardest surveying campaign of the entire voyage. This was the assignment the whole enterprise had been funded for — *these* reefs, *these* passages, the specific water that was drowning the specific commerce whose owners had lobbied Congress — and Wilkes prosecuted it with total commitment: boat crews out for days and weeks at a time, triangulating island to island, sleeping armed, standing anchor watches with loaded guns, under standing orders that treated every beach as a potential ambush because some of them were. The tension of it ground men down in a way the ice never had. The Antarctic had been honest — it wanted to kill everyone impartially and advertised the fact. Fiji smiled, traded yams across the gunwale at noon, and might or might not come over the gunwale at midnight, and no one could ever stand fully down. Three months of that. The squadron's nerves arrived at July already half-spent, and the voyage's two darkest stories were both waiting in the second half of the summer.

## Chapter 26 — Vendovi

The first was an arrest six years in the making.

In August 1834 — four years before the Ex. Ex. sailed — the American brig *Charles Doggett* had been working bêche-de-mer at Ono, in the south of the group, under an arrangement with a chief named Vendovi, a brother of the king of Rewa: trade goods for fish, and a minor chief lodged aboard as a living guarantee of the shore party's safety, hostage-keeping being ordinary contract law in the islands. The guarantee was gamed. The hostage pleaded illness and was allowed ashore; the next day he returned with Vendovi and his men, asking medicine at the trading house, and when the mate came in with eight men and a boy, the Fijians killed all but one of them. The survivor swam for the ship. The captain ransomed back the bodies of the dead — all but one: a Black American sailor whose body was never returned, and whose fate the traders reported in the one word Fiji's reputation was built on. Ten men, a burned station, and the specific horror that traveled farthest in the telling. The affair had been sworn out in depositions and carried home in owners' complaints, and it was sitting in Wilkes' orders now as a named piece of business: the Fijians were to be taught that American blood had a price, and Vendovi was the invoice.

Hudson served it, in the *Peacock*, at Rewa, with a coolness that the wardroom admired and that reads, at this distance, like the plot of a hostage drama run in reverse. The royal family of Rewa — Vendovi's brothers, among them the king, the Roko Tui Dreketi himself — came aboard to be feasted, as chiefs did; and when they were fed and settled, Hudson informed them, with every courtesy, that they were his guests until Vendovi was his prisoner. No blood, no bombardment: just the trap of hospitality, sprung by the people who had spent six years condemning it as treachery when the *Charles Doggett*'s hostage arrangement was gamed the same way. The brothers took the point — some accounts have them taking it with grim professional appreciation — and Vendovi was produced, and gave himself up with a composure everyone recorded.

What the expedition then had was a problem shaped like a man. There was no court to try him in; no law of 1840 reached a Fijian chief for an act against Americans in Fiji six years gone; so the squadron improvised the sentence the age found natural — Vendovi would be carried to the United States, to see the country whose citizens he had killed and be improved by the sight of it. He was given the run of the ship at irons' distance, sketched by Agate, studied by the scientifics, and gradually absorbed into the strange shipboard category of prisoner-mascot-specimen. The forecastle, which had its own instincts about rank and about captivity, came to know him as a shipmate knows anyone, and mostly liked what it knew: a grave, intelligent, homesick man of standing, picking up the ship's words, keeping the dignity of a chief in a situation his captors had not thought through past the arrest. He was two years from an island he would never see again, aboard a wooden world that had decided his abduction was a mercy, and he crosses the rest of this story the way he crossed its decks — quiet, unresolved, the voyage's conscience walking. His account comes due in New York harbor, timed by fate like a verdict.

## Chapter 27 — Malolo

The second story is the one the expedition never stopped carrying.

On July 24, 1840, two survey boats were working the western islands, short of provisions and bartering for food at Malolo, when the machinery of hostage, misread signal, and hair-trigger — the machinery this book flagged at the Río Negro, where it cost an evening's fright — finally engaged at full force. The Americans held a hostage in one of the boats: a son of the island's chief, taken as surety in the standard manner while the trading party stood on the beach. The young man jumped. Over the side, running through the shallows for home, and the boat crew fired over his head to stop him — and on that beach, in that instant, every ambiguity in the Pacific collapsed into blades. The men in the boats swore afterward that the escape was a prearranged signal, the trigger for an ambush already set. The men who survived the beach believed the gunfire itself had detonated a crowd that might have stayed a crowd. The two accounts have never been reconciled, because they cannot be: it is the Río Negro question — *who moved first?* — asked at the one volume at which no answer survives.

What is not disputed is who lay in the water when the boats fought clear. Lieutenant Joseph Underwood — the officer this narrative has kept meeting at the pressure points, the man who carried the news of the snubbed chief Tuetila to Wilkes' door — and Midshipman Wilkes Henry, the commander's own nephew, his widowed sister's only son, whose place on the expedition Wilkes had personally arranged. Speared and clubbed in the shallows of an island neither had ever seen before that week, aged perhaps thirty and twenty-one.

Wilkes' grief was total and terrifying, and what it unlocked should be told without shelter. The next day some seventy sailors and marines landed on Malolo and made war on it. The island's warriors met them and were broken by volley fire; the two principal villages were stormed and burned to the ground, crops destroyed, canoes destroyed; and by the counts that survive — the expedition's own, and the memory of the islanders, an old man of Malolo later reckoning it at some eighty dead — between seventy-four and a hundred and four Fijians were killed in the day's work. No American died. The following day the island's survivors came to the victors and made the submission their own law prescribed for the utterly conquered: they crawled. On hands and knees, to the men with the guns, suing for peace from the *papalagi*, and Wilkes received the submission and considered the account balanced.

It has to be written both ways, because both are true and each is a lie alone. By the customs of the sea and the standing practice of every navy in the Pacific, a bartering party had been massacred and an example was made; not one government on Earth in 1840 would have faulted the reprisal, and several would have thought it light. And: a United States exploring and *scientific* expedition, sent to chart and to know, answered the death of two of its own by killing something near a hundred people and erasing two towns, on an island whose side of July 24 exists in no archive, because the expedition burned the town and sailed away with the only pens. The charts of Fiji the squadron carried off that August are magnificent — the boats went straight back to surveying, and finished the group — and the names of Underwood and Henry were added, in time, to the officers' monument at Mount Auburn, joining the *Sea Gull*'s fifteen under the same stone. Malolo's dead have no stone. The reader may hold the chart in one hand and the ledger in the other; the book's only demand is that neither hand be allowed to open.

On August 9 the squadron rendezvoused off Macuata, stood out of the group within days, and put Fiji astern forever. No one aboard was the same age they had been in May.

## Chapter 28 — Honolulu and the Mountain

The passage north was almost a convalescence. The squadron named the Phoenix Group as it went, and touched at Palmyra — the first scientific expedition ever to examine that lonely atoll — small deposits in the account of firsts, made in flat calm. By early autumn the ships lay at Honolulu, the crossroads capital of the Pacific: whalers by the score, missionaries in force, and a Hawaiian kingdom governing the collision. The officers were received by King Kamehameha III and Governor Kekūanaōʻa with full honors, and for the men there was that rarest of commodities, a friendly port with time in it.

The friendliness got a test almost at once, because at Honolulu the enlistments came due. The crews had signed for three years; the three years were up; and the voyage was not. Wilkes needed his sailors to sign again, and the negotiation that followed showed the commander at his shrewdest and then at his worst. The shrewdness: pay down, liberty, grog, and the full theater of largesse for the men who re-shipped. The worst: for the core of refusers — men whose contracts were simply *finished*, asking only the discharge the law owed them — Wilkes had the ringleaders, as he saw them, seized to the gratings and flogged, and flogged past the mark. Twelve lashes was the legal ceiling absent a court-martial, written in the statute; men at Honolulu took double and more, by the commander's sole order, for the offense of insisting their signed terms meant what they said. It answered in the short run, as terror does — the squadron kept its manpower, topped off with some fifty Hawaiian recruits, the *kanakas* who would earn every dollar in the year ahead. But mark the scene, because the law eventually would: of everything Charles Wilkes did in four years — the seizures, the paranoia, Malolo itself — the offense a court-martial finally made stick was this one. The dozen-and-more at the gangway. The category of act that opened Charlie Erskine's back in 1838 was the single crime the United States Navy ever proved against him.

Then Wilkes went and spent the winter doing something magnificent, because he was never only one man. The expedition had come to the one archipelago on Earth where the planet does its making in public, and the scientifics went at it like men given the keys to the workshop. At Kilauea the party stood on the crater's rim and looked down into the lake of live lava — the molten sea rising and crusting and tearing open in lines of fire, the ground breathing under their boots — and Dana, the coral man, found his life's other subject boiling in front of him; he would go home to become the century's great volcanologist substantially on the strength of what Hawaiʻi showed him. But the campaign was the mountain itself. The Ex. Ex.'s physics program required gravity measurements from a great elevation, and the greatest elevation available was Mauna Loa — nearly fourteen thousand feet of active volcano. From December 1840 into the new year, Wilkes led an expedition to its summit that was, in everything but temperature, a polar campaign: hundreds of Hawaiian porters carrying the pendulum apparatus, instruments, and stores up a mountain whose upper reaches were bare lava and snow — and up a route of Wilkes' own blazing, the commander characteristically declining the existing trail in favor of his own line, at the usual cost to everyone under him. Men went snow-blind in the glare. The clinker lava — *ʻaʻā*, the Hawaiians' own word for it — shredded boots and the feet inside them; the altitude sickened porters and sailors alike; gales raked the summit camp, a ring of stone-walled shelters Wilkes named Pendulum Peak, where for weeks the party swung its pendulums and read its instruments on the roof of the Pacific while the volcano steamed around them. He had found a way to have Antarctica in the tropics, and the men who froze on a Hawaiian volcano for the shape of the Earth added it, with the ice cruise, to the strange double ledger they kept on him. Down at sea level, meanwhile, the survey machine did to the Hawaiian Islands what it had done to Fiji: harbors, reefs, coastlines, the heights of the great mountains measured for the first time — charts of such quality that mariners were still steering Hawaiian waters by their descendants a century later, when the next war came through.

## Chapter 29 — Two Cruises, 1841

The expedition's final year opened with the squadron split across the hemisphere, running its last two campaigns at once.

Hudson had the tropical one. Through the first half of 1841 the repaired *Peacock* and the *Flying Fish* swept the central Pacific — back through Samoa, then the Ellice Islands, then the Gilberts — closing the survey gaps and squaring old accounts, and the cruise wrote both halves of the expedition's character into the record one last time. At Upolu, where a wanted chief in the killing of an American sailor was not surrendered, Hudson's answer was a bombardment: the ship's guns turned on a Samoan town, the debt-collection theory of naval diplomacy applied without a landing. At Drummond's Island — Tabiteuea, in the Gilberts — the pattern found its full Fijian shape. Rumor of a wrecked ship and murdered crew; a landing party under Lieutenant Walker surveying a wary but peaceful town; and then, at the day's end, a man of the *Peacock*'s crew missing from the count — vanished into the crowd, never seen again, alive or dead. A demand for his return was met with a beach full of armed warriors. The next day the ship bombarded the town of Utiroa and Walker's party landed through the surf against, by the expedition's estimate, some seven hundred fighting men; the volleys broke them as volleys had broken Malolo's; a dozen Gilbertese died by the American count, the town burned, and the missing sailor was never found — not his body, not his fate, nothing. An entire punitive action, a town in ashes, over a disappearance that remains a blank to this day.

And in the same weeks, between the fires, the boats did the work the fires keep upstaging: through April 1841 the *Peacock*'s surveyors charted the Gilbert chain island by island — Tabiteuea, Nonouti, Abemama, Maiana, Butaritari, Makin — and among them a middling atoll called Tarawa, sounded and triangulated and inked like all the rest, filed with two hundred sisters in the expedition's portfolio. Remember the name. Of every line the Ex. Ex. drew, that chart has the longest and heaviest afterlife, and this book will meet it again in its final pages, in November of 1943.

Wilkes had the continental cruise. The *Vincennes* and *Porpoise* left Honolulu on April 5, raised Cape Disappointment on April 28 — and discovered that the grand climax of their orders, the survey of the Columbia, opened with a river that would not let them in: the bar was a smother of breakers from headland to headland, the most dangerous river entrance on the coast, and Wilkes, of all people, looked at it and declined. He took the squadron north instead, into the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the inland sea beyond, and there the expedition spent a golden season doing exactly what it had been invented for. The Hudson's Bay Company received them at Fort Nisqually with the full hospitality of an empire that understood precisely what an American survey of these waters meant and was too well-mannered to say so; the Americans accepted the beef and the horses and surveyed anyway. The boats took Puget Sound apart arm by arm — the *Porpoise* working Admiralty Inlet, the *Vincennes*' boats down Hood Canal and north toward the Fraser — through a country of deep still water and black timber where the leadsmen kept calling depths that belonged on an ocean chart, a drowned mountain range of a harbor, room for every navy on Earth to anchor out of sight of each other. Lieutenant Johnson took a small party over the Cascades on horseback — the expedition's one true land expedition into the interior — to Fort Okanogan and Colvile and the Whitman mission on the Walla Walla, connecting the coastal survey to the fur country by compass and stirrup. Wilkes himself went down to Fort Vancouver to take the measure of the great McLoughlin, the white-maned autocrat of the Columbia, and the two most imperious men on the coast got on famously, each recognizing the type. Out among the Willamette settlers — the ragged first hundred of what was about to become the Oregon Trail's flood — the Americans asked the United States Navy when the United States was coming, and were told, in effect, *soon: wait for the government to throw its mantle over you*. And the expedition planted names on that inland sea like a farmer sowing a field — Eagle Harbor, Port Madison, Elliott Bay, Port Ludlow for a lieutenant dead in the last war — a naming spree so thorough that the modern map of Washington State reads in places like the *Vincennes*' muster roll. On the fifth of July, 1841, at the company farm by Nisqually, the crews held games, a feast, a salute, and a sham battle for an audience of Nisqually people and HBC men — the first American Independence Day ever celebrated west of the Mississippi, on ground the United States did not yet own. Five years before the Oregon Treaty, the Ex. Ex. was the American claim, made in soundings and place-names, and both London and Washington knew it. It is the expedition's least famous campaign and its most consequential: no one died, nothing burned, and the border of a continent tilted.

All that remained was for the two halves of the squadron to meet at the mouth of the Columbia. The *Peacock* sailed from Honolulu on June 21 to keep the appointment.

## Chapter 30 — The Columbia Bar, and Home

She arrived off Cape Disappointment on July 17, 1841, and on the 18th, Hudson took her at the bar.

The bar had moved since the sailing directions were written — the Columbia redraws its own entrance yearly, which was precisely why the survey had come — and the *Peacock* — the ship whose rot Norfolk had painted over, who had survived the Antarctic pack by thirty hours of miracle — struck on the sands and stuck. The breakers did the rest at their leisure. There was no storm and no night to the disaster, just the Pacific arriving on schedule, sea after sea, while the rotten hull worked and opened; and the wreck of the *Peacock* became, in the hands of her people, the best-conducted catastrophe in the Navy's records. Discipline never cracked. The boats went out through the surf again and again, and by the end every man was ashore in Baker's Bay alive — and not only the men: the journals, the survey books, the chronometers, cases of Agate's drawings, the paper soul of three years' work, handed down into the boats ahead of the crew's own sea chests. A one-eyed Chinook pilot named George brought the little *Flying Fish* safely in past the wreck. On the sands where the ship broke up — Peacock Spit, the charts say now, her one permanent estate — her people built a driftwood camp the squadron christened Peacockville, and the Hudson's Bay man at Astoria, James Birnie, and the Methodist mission, and the Clatsop and Chinookan people of the river fed and supplied the castaways of the power that had come to survey them. The expedition that had burned towns for lesser losses was itself wrecked, destitute, and dependent on strangers' kindness for three weeks, and received it. There is no evidence the irony landed on Wilkes. It is at least in the record for the rest of us.

Wilkes bought an American trading brig lying in the river — the *Thomas H. Perkins*, taken into service as the *Oregon* — and the survey absorbed the disaster almost without a stride: the Columbia charted from the bar toward Walla Walla, Grays Harbor sounded, and then the last land campaign of the voyage, a party of thirty-nine under Emmons — Eld and Agate and Dana and Peale among them, with the mountain man Joseph Meek guiding — marched overland from the Columbia south by the Siskiyou Trail, past Shasta, down the Sacramento by Sutter's fort, to meet the *Vincennes* in San Francisco Bay in late October. One more great harbor surveyed — a harbor belonging to Mexico, examined by the United States Navy with a thoroughness that reads, knowing what 1846 knows, like a man measuring a house he intends to buy. On the last day of October 1841, the squadron stood out the Golden Gate and turned for home the long way: Honolulu in November for a last provisioning among the now-familiar islands, then west into the emptiest quarter of the ocean, raising lonely Wake Island in December — one more uninhabited speck fixed for the chart, a name waiting a century for its war. Manila in the new year, where the officers dined with Spanish colonial society under chandeliers and the surveys went on regardless; down through the Sulu Sea, where Wilkes, closing the ledger of his orders item by item, negotiated with the Sultan of Sulu the trade protections written last on the list — the expedition ending as it began, a commercial errand wearing a sword. At Singapore the gallant, expiring *Flying Fish* was surveyed, condemned, and sold out of the service — the record-holder of the far south, the ninety-six-ton harbor boat that had out-sailed Cook's mark and out-lived the *Sea Gull* and the *Peacock* both, ending her days, the crews heard later, as an opium runner; the men who had frozen aboard her took it hard, sailors being sentimental about exactly two things, and the other one is pay. Then the Indian Ocean, the Cape of Good Hope, the long South Atlantic with the trade winds doing the work and the ships full of men counting days for the first time in four years — and on June 10, 1842, New York.

Four years all but two months. Eighty-seven thousand miles. The last all-sail squadron of the United States Navy ever to go around the world came up the harbor — to nothing. No cannon, no crowds, no city fathers at the landing; the newspapers had spent the spring printing the expedition's quarrels ahead of its discoveries, the administration that launched it was two administrations gone, and the Navy's first communication of substance to its returning explorer concerned the irregularity of his pennant. Wilkes hauled down the broad blue fiction himself, in the harbor, unwatched.

And the day after the anchors went down, the voyage supplied its own epitaph. Vendovi — two years a prisoner-passenger, the man carried ten thousand miles to be improved by the sight of America — had sickened on the last passage, and on June 11, 1842, having lived just long enough to raise the country that took him, he died in the naval hospital at New York. The expedition kept his skull. It went into the collections, and the collections went on display at the Patent Office in Washington, where for years the visiting public could file past the assembled wonders of the great national voyage — the corals, the war clubs, the bird skins, and the labeled skull of the last king's brother the *Peacock* ever arrested. Every voyage of discovery comes home as a museum. It is worth one hard look at what, exactly, this one exhibited.

## Chapter 31 — Judgment, and Three Codas

What the United States did with its greatest expedition was put it on trial.

The summer of 1842 dissolved into courts-martial aboard the ship of the line *North Carolina* in New York harbor — a season of reciprocal prosecutions in which Wilkes tried the officers who had defied him and the officers, led in evidence by the surgeon Charles Guillou, tried Wilkes. The charges against the commander gathered up the whole four years: the loss of the *Peacock*; oppression and mistreatment of his officers; illegal punishment of his men; counts touching his conduct and his claims. The verdict, when it came, was a document of almost novelistic precision. On the loss of his ship: acquitted. On the tyrannies that had filled a squadron's journals: acquitted. On everything, acquitted — except one charge. *Illegal punishment*: the floggings beyond the lawful twelve, Honolulu's gratings, the dozen-and-more. For that, and that alone, Charles Wilkes was convicted and sentenced to be publicly reprimanded — a paragraph of official displeasure, read into his record by the Secretary of the Navy.

Stand Charlie Erskine next to that verdict. The United States Navy examined four years and eighty-seven thousand miles of Charles Wilkes and found exactly one provable crime, and it was the category of act that had opened a fifteen-year-old's back at the gangway of the *Porpoise* in 1838 — the act that put the belaying pin in the boy's hand in the dark. The law never heard of Charlie's night at the taffrail. But the only conviction it ever recorded against his captain was, in kind, *his*.

Congress, having tried the man, then had to decide what to do with the treasure, and its answer belongs in the national comedy: an Act of August 26, 1842, ordering the expedition's results published in a style worthy of the great voyages of Europe — in an edition of one hundred copies. One hundred. One per state, a few for the crowned heads and the Library of Congress, one each for the senior commanders; the American people, whose expedition it was, could apply to their legislature for a look. Wilkes was named to superintend the work in 1843, and it consumed him for twenty years — the five-volume *Narrative* in 1844, then the scientific series, Dana's great volumes on corals and geology among them, nineteen tomes published of twenty-eight planned before the money died; Asa Gray's second botany volume, Agassiz's fishes, and Wilkes' own physics still sit unborn in the national ledger. The collections themselves — the sixty thousand specimens, the seeds and living plants that seeded a Botanic Garden, the artifacts, the skull — passed from the Patent Office cases into the keeping of a brand-new institution founded in 1846 on an Englishman's bequest, and became its backbone: the reason there was something for the Smithsonian to *be*. And the charts — some two hundred of them — went to the fleet and the whalemen and simply worked, decade after decade, which is the fate good charts want. One of them worked longest of all. In 1943, planners preparing the amphibious assault on a Gilbert Islands atoll found that the best survey of Tarawa in Allied hands was still, "with only slight modifications," the one the *Peacock*'s boats had drawn in April 1841 — a century-old chart, carried into the landing plans of the Second World War with cautionary notes in its margins. The reef lines held true. What the boats of 1841 had never stayed to measure — the atoll's freakish, faithless tides — was the blank the modern navy filled with a guess, and on the twentieth of November 1943 the guess failed, the tide sat down on the reef, and the Marines of the 2nd Division waded eight hundred yards into machine-gun fire past the grounded landing craft. The Ex. Ex.'s legacy, whole, in a single morning: the chart that was still the best on Earth, and the cost of every sounding it never took.

The whole account, side by side, in plain figures, the way the country never bothered to draw it up: four years, all but two months. Eighty-seven thousand miles under sail. Some two hundred and eighty islands surveyed; eight hundred miles of the Oregon country's rivers and coast; fifteen hundred miles of Antarctic shore; some two hundred charts; sixty thousand specimens; the seed collection of a national garden; the founding cargo of a national museum. Against it: two ships, twenty-eight men by the strict count and nearer forty by the loose one, two burned towns in Fiji and one in the Gilberts, a hundred-odd Fijian dead, a dozen Gilbertese, a chief who died of the passage, and a commander convicted at home of the lash. No nation has ever gotten more chart for its dollar, and the dollar figure is not the whole invoice, and both of those clauses go on the stone.

**Wilkes.** He got his rank at last the slow way — commander in 1843, captain in 1855 — and then, at sixty-three, the Civil War handed him one more chance to be exactly himself. Commanding the steam frigate *San Jacinto* in November 1861, he stopped the British mail packet *Trent* on the high seas with two shots across her bow and took off her, by force, the Confederate commissioners Mason and Slidell — instantly the most famous man in the Union, thanked by Congress for his "brave, adroit and patriotic conduct," toasted from Boston to Chicago, while Great Britain moved troops to Canada and the two nations stood, for some weeks, at the edge of a war that would have remade the world. Lincoln's government released the prisoners and disavowed the act, and the oldest pattern in this book ran once more at national scale: the brilliant, unauthorized stroke; the applause; the disavowal; the file. Made commodore in 1862 and given a West Indies squadron, he feuded with neutrals, with the Navy Department, and with the Secretary himself, leaked his own scalding letter to the newspapers, and in 1864 faced his second court-martial — disobedience, insubordination, disrespect, conduct unbecoming — and this time was convicted on *every* charge, sentenced to reprimand and three years' suspension, which Lincoln, the last in the long line of superiors who couldn't quite bring themselves to finish him, cut to one. Rear admiral on the retired list, 1866. He spent his last decade in Washington on his autobiography, restating his case to history in the second person plural, and died on February 8, 1877. They buried him at Oak Hill; in 1909 the nation moved him to Arlington, where the government stone above him carries the epitaph he would have chosen against all advice, and won in the end: *He discovered the Ant-arctic continent.*

**Reynolds.** His lieutenant's commission, dated September 1841, chased him across the Pacific and caught him near the voyage's end; Rebecca Krug of Lancaster married him on August 16, 1842, nine weeks and a few days after the *Vincennes* dropped anchor — the girl and the commission both faithful through four years, the one form of accounting in this book that closes clean. The sea gave him a middle act nobody would script: his health broke, and the Navy's discard pile turned out to be Honolulu, where the exile of the flogging squadron spent the 1850s as a naval storekeeper in the islands he'd first seen from the *Vincennes*' boats. The Civil War recalled him; command at Port Royal made his name; and then the ladder simply kept going — captain of the *Lackawanna*, the officer who in 1867 formally claimed Midway Atoll for the United States (the man was constitutionally incapable of a Pacific cruise without annexing something for the chart), commodore, chief of a Navy bureau, rear admiral in 1873, commander of the Asiatic Squadron with his wife beside him becoming the first American woman to walk the Great Wall of China. And twice, in 1873 and 1874, the duty officer of the Republic's sea power: *Acting Secretary of the Navy* William Reynolds — the passed midshipman who had filled a secret journal with the wish to see his commander hanged, finishing his career at the top of the department that had never quite known what it possessed in either of them. He retired on his sixty-second birthday and died in Washington on November 5, 1879, and they took him home to Lancaster and buried him beside his younger brother John — Major General John F. Reynolds, killed at Gettysburg on the morning of the first day — two brothers from one Pennsylvania house holding, between them, a corner of the continent's story and a corner of the ocean's. The secret journal stayed in the family's papers for a century and a half, and was published at last in 2004. Every reader since has discovered what Lydia always knew: the best writer on the United States Exploring Expedition ranked, at sailing, four hundred and somethingth in its chain of command.

**Charlie.** He never did stop going to sea — the title he chose says so: not four years before the mast but *twenty* — and somewhere in those years the boy who could not read the muster book that bound him taught himself his letters, one more survey of unknown country conducted without funding or fanfare. In old age he toured the lecture halls with a moving panorama of the great voyage, the flogged ship's boy retelling the national epic to paying crowds, and in 1890 he set his name to the book — published at Philadelphia in 1896, its title paying its tormentor the rank history finally gave him: *the late Admiral Charles Wilkes*. The confession is in its pages, where this book began: the pin, the dark, the sleeping head, the mother's face that stayed the boy's arm — "God forgive me... how many times I have thanked Him." And around the confession, the choices of an old man squaring accounts. For his epigraph he reached past every admiral and author to the words of James Smithson — the stranger whose bequest built the house where the expedition's treasures live: *Every man is a valuable member of society who, by his observations, researches, and experience, procures knowledge for men.* Charlie Erskine, who was never permitted to be a scientific, claiming the scientist's charter for a sailor before the mast — and the claim is just, for his observations came home too, and you are holding some of them. And for his dedication, this, entire: *To the Crew of the Ship Universal.*

The boy at the taffrail had wanted one man dead. The old man at the writing desk signed his book over to everybody alive. The distance between those two positions was eighty-seven thousand miles, and he had the log to prove it.
