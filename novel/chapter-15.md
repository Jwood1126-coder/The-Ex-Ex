## January 24, 1840

He had been aloft three times that week and seen the land on each, and none of it had
gone easily into the book. Two summits on the sixteenth, holding their bearing while the
bergs drifted past them, that Hudson would not enter on the word of two young men at a
masthead. A dark headland on the nineteenth, which he did — position and bearings
attached, the lesson of the sixteenth learned at a cost of three days. Now the coast lay
somewhere off the bow behind the ice, and Reynolds was on deck with the rest of them,
because the deck was where a man was wanted when the pack came to collect.

Hudson had worked the *Peacock* deep into a bay in the ice, standing south after the
land the mastheads kept promising. Reynolds had watched the walls close on either hand
as she stood in, and thought — the way a man thinks a thing and files it and keeps
hauling — that a bay in the ice is a bay only for as long as the ice consents. It
withdrew its consent before noon. The wind backed; the floes on both quarters came
together with the slow certainty of a gate swinging to; and Hudson put his helm over to
wear her clear while a lead still showed.

She struck in the turn. Ice under the counter first — the stern's overhang, a shove that
came up through the planking into his knees before he had it named — then ice on the
quarter, the stern's shoulder, harder, and the shove drew out into a grinding that ran
her whole length and did not leave off. When it left off, the tiller — the bar the
wheel's ropes hauled on — was swinging loose across the deck.

That was the whole of the disaster in one motion, and every officer aboard read it where
he stood. The rudder had been torn off its fittings and the head of it split; the wheel
amidships now turned nothing at all. Six hundred and fifty tons of ship, shut into moving
ice, the weather going gray to windward, and the one thing that steers a ship lying
wrenched from her stern.

For a while she was nobody's ship. With no rudder to bite and no way on her, the set of
the ice took her where it pleased, and it pleased to carry her round and lay her against
a berg — a wall of old ice standing higher than her tops, that she came up on broadside
and ground along, fending as she went, until the same drift that had put her there let
her off it again. A quartermaster stood at the dead wheel through the whole of it, his
hands off the spokes, out of habit, or out of not knowing where else a quartermaster
was meant to stand.

## The thirty hours

They fought her for a day and a night and the better part of the day after, and the men
who lived counted it ever after among the worst hours of their lives and the ones they
were least willing to give up.

There was no steering her, so they steered her by hand and by main strength. Ice anchors
— great single hooks — were carried out ahead by boat and driven into the firmer floes by
hand until they bit; then parties tailed onto the warps made fast to them and hauled her
up to the bite, a boat's length at a time, through leads that opened grudgingly and closed
behind. The lines came inboard wet and went stiff as they came, so that a man near the
end of a long pull was gripping ice that had lately been rope. Where the floes bore down
on her sides the crew met them with spars, with capstan bars, and, when there was nothing
else to hand, with their own braced arms — fending a berg off a wooden hull with about
the purchase of a shoulder set against a wall already coming down.

The cold worked on them the whole time, patient as the ice.
Wet wool froze on the man who wore it. Fingers split at the knuckle on the frozen lines,
were bound up, and were put back on the warp. There was no watch below worth the name; a
man off the haul lay down in his wet clothes where he could and was up at the next alarm,
and the alarms did not keep to the half hours the bell did.

She took her worst at the stern. A floe came in over the quarter on the lift of the
swell and went out again with a length of her — bulwark, the davits the boats hung from, the
spare spars that had been lashed there a breath before — and the noise it made going was one no
man aboard owned a word for; and Reynolds, who made his living in words, did not try to
find one. He gave it up and hauled.

The boats were swung out and stored against the chance that there would be no ship left
to stand on. Every hand that helped store them knew the sum: three hundred miles of
frozen coast under their lee that no keel had ever touched, and the nearest help a whole
season astern. The boats were not a way home. They were only a way to be longer about
the end of it.

Below, through the deck, he could hear the carpenters. The rudder had come up onto the
berth deck to be rebuilt, and they were at it in water to the wrist and then to the
elbow, by the light of lanterns that showed nothing, the armorer's hammer going in a
slow patient stroke under the random gunnery of the bergs somewhere out in the white. He
went down once and did not go again; the sound was worse below, for knowing what it was,
and how little of the hull stood between it and the sea.

And through all of it the ship kept her hours as though the day were an ordinary one. The
bell was struck every half hour as it had been struck since Norfolk. Watches were
relieved on time by men coming up from below into that and going down out of it and
taking their turn at the warps between. The cook got a hot kettle up to them in the
middle of the worst of it, and they ate standing, salt beef in the fist, mittens off for
the minute the eating took and no longer. That, Reynolds thought, was the thing no one at
home would credit — not the ice, which they would believe soon enough, but the beef.

She came clear on the second day. Worked out of the closing bay under a scrap of
after-canvas, steered by the trim of it and by the arms of the men at the warps, she
found the last lead and took it; and when open water stood plain and dark ahead her
company cheered her — the old *Peacock*, whose rot every officer had written up and every
economy ashore had ignored — for the one thing left, that she was still under them.

## North

Hudson brought his crippled command round and steered her north the only way she could
now be steered. The rebuilt rudder hung in its fittings again, but no wheel reached it any
longer; it was worked by relieving tackles — ropes rove to the tiller below the deck, a
gang tailed onto each side of it. She was steered by voice after that, an order called
down from the quarterdeck and a watch below hauling her head over, this side and then
that, the tackles creaking and the men grunting in the dark under the deck they could not
see over — eleven hundred miles of it back to Sydney to be made whole, a thousand further
than the three hundred of frozen coast they had just measured under her lee.

What the season had cost came clear to the officers on that deck, and it gave no comfort.
The coast was real. Reynolds had never doubted it, not since the sixteenth, and there was
no man aboard now who would stand up and call the gray masses that held their bearing week
on week a bank of cloud. What the ice had answered was the part no one had wanted to say
aloud: that the price of being the ship to find the land was a rudder on the carpenter's
bench, a stern boarded over under a patch of sail, and one ship in four beaten out of the
campaign before February — and that the bill was still being added.

He kept his pages as the land drew astern. The two summits of the sixteenth stood a
while off the quarter, holding their shape against the drift exactly as they had when he
first swore to them from the rigging, until the ice took them down; they carry his name
and Eld's on the charts now, on a coast no one believed them about until too late. Then
there was nothing off the quarter but the pack, going gray, and then the pack was under
the horizon too.

She limped in through the heads of Sydney harbor in February — the colony that had asked the
Americans in December whether their wills were made, and got its answer now in the shape
of a ship with her bulwarks gone and her stern boarded over and every soul of her company
standing alive on her deck, her officers already talking of the country they had seen.
The seamen on the wharves read her hull the way Reynolds read a coast, and the tone of
the newspapers, which had been irony, went quiet, and then turned clean over.

She would not go south again. Months of refitting lay ahead of her, and the campaign
would run on to its end without her — the flagship and the brig standing away west along
the barrier into an ice that no man with a journal open was left to watch.

Reynolds closed his on the near side of nothing, with the last of the pack sinking behind
him and the land already gone under it. He had been carried to the edge of the seventh
part of the world and shown it, and he was being carried off again with his rudder in
pieces on the berth deck below. The pen that saw the coast first was sailing out of the
ice a passenger.
