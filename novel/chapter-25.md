## New York, the 10th of June, 1842

For four years the sounding-lead had come up with the far side of the world stuck to it —
the underside of the lead armed with a blob of tallow, pressed into the bottom to bring a
sample of it back up — coral sand, black volcanic grit, the gray ooze of seas no line had
touched before it. On the ninth of June, 1842, in a fog off the American coast so thick a
man in the waist could not see the head of his own ship, the lead came up at eighty
fathoms — six feet to the fathom, near five hundred feet of line run out — with nothing on
it but cold mud, and every man who hauled the line knew the mud for what it was. They fired a gun into the fog to call a pilot off the coast, and out of the fog
a pilot came.

They had come the last width of the Atlantic with the trade winds doing the work and
nothing left to survey, and for the first time in four years the men had been counting
days. A ship with her work behind her sails differently. The discipline holds; the
reason for it wears thin.

By the next morning the fog had lifted enough to show the Highlands of Neversink
standing up behind Sandy Hook — the first American ground four years of eyes had rested
on. There was a short lie at quarantine, the health officer's boat pulling out to
look them over for fever and pulling back satisfied. Then the flagship went up the harbor to the city.

She went up to nothing. No gun answered from the Battery, no crowd stood at the landing,
no city fathers came off in a barge. The newspapers that spring had been printing the
squadron's quarrels faster than its discoveries; the President who signed its orders was
two Presidents gone; and the first word the Navy sent its returning explorer, ahead of
any thanks, had been a note questioning the flag he flew. Eighty-seven thousand miles,
and the country was looking somewhere else.

Charlie took none of it for a slight. He came up the harbor a free man in the land of
freedom — the words are his — and to be let out from under four years of a man-of-war's
discipline was, he said, bliss indeed. The shore came at the rail smelling of cut hay
and chimney-smoke and river mud, land smells, and the body knew them the way it had
known the mud on the lead.

*  *  *

Off the Battery — the old fort at the foot of the island — all hands were called to
muster. The men were packed into the waist; the commodore stood above them at the
quarterdeck rail, Charlie one of the upturned faces below.

He thanked them for the manner in which they had conducted themselves through the cruise,
and said he had no doubt the Government would reward their long and perilous services. Then the salute went — twenty-six guns, one for
every state, the smoke of it standing on the flat water — and at the last gun the broad
blue pennant of the commodore of the squadron came down from the masthead by the
commander's own hand.

It was the flag the Navy List had never granted him. He had flown a commodore's pennant
for four years on a lieutenant's commission, and now he took the halyard himself and
hauled the fiction down, in the harbor, with no one by who could gainsay it.

Then, in the sailor's own account of the day, one sentence: *the commodore then left the
ship and proceeded to Washington.*

The memoir ends its whole long reckoning with him exactly there — at the gangway,
stepping off toward the capital — and does not follow. What waited for Charles Wilkes in
Washington that summer was a court, and then another; the sentence that carries him
ashore stops one word short of the season that would try him for all four years held and
convict him of a single thing. The boy at the rail did not write that part. He did not
yet know it, and when the time came to write his book, he chose not to learn it onto the
page.

*  *  *

The men had passed their own verdict already, and it was not the court's.

Somewhere on the long haul home they had made a song of him. It set Cook and Ross, the
great navigators of the age, as mounds of dross beside their captain's name; it spoke
Columbus and their chief in one breath; its chorus called him *Bold Captain Wilkes.* The
forecastle that sang it held men he had had seized up and flogged, and it sang him a
hero without a blush.

*  *  *

One man aboard had come the whole eighty-seven thousand miles only to arrive, and be
killed by arriving.

Vendovi had come aboard at Rewa two years before, a chief taken for a killing six years
older than that, and the lower deck — which kept its own counsel about rank and about
captivity — had made him a place. His irons had come off after six months. Charlie set
him down, in the end, as a very thoughtful, genial, and pleasant sort of a man. The
scientifics found him something else again: when the expedition's philologist — the
scholar sent to collect the native tongues — wanted the
old Fijian account of the great flood, it was Vendovi who gave it to him, and the
philologist wrote it into his volume and cited the chief for it by name, the way a
scholar cites a source — the man carried across an ocean as a specimen, sitting as an
authority on the history of his own sea, telling how his people had once kept their great
canoes hauled up and ready against the water's return.

He had sickened on the last passage. The day after the anchors went down — the eleventh
of June, the ships not a full day home — he died in the naval hospital across the water — a
Brooklyn ward with the harbor at its windows —
having lived just long enough to raise the country he had been carried ten thousand miles
to be improved by the sight of. *He died soon afterward,* Charlie wrote, and folded the
ocean and the two years into three words, the way the forecastle folds anything it cannot
look at straight.

The expedition kept the rest. In Washington that summer its crates were coming open in
the Great Hall of the Patent Office — corals and pressed plants, war clubs and bird
skins, and Agate's careful pencil drawing of a chief's face — and the flesh was cleaned
from Vendovi's skull, and the skull was labeled and set among them in a glass case — case
thirty-seven of the national collection — while the rest of him went into the ground of a naval cemetery
in Brooklyn. The flood-story kept its place in the philologist's book, under his name.
The man kept none. He had been the message the whole way home, and arrival was the one
thing the message did not survive.

*  *  *

Charlie went looking for his mother, and for a day could not find her either.

The first Sunday home three of them went up into the old streets together — Charlie, and
Isaac Carney, and Bill Roberts, who had first read a letter to Charlie aloud when Charlie
could not — and by noon not one of the three had turned up the people he had crossed the
world to see. They sat down in the old wood-shed behind Mother Paine's and cried like the
boys they had stopped being somewhere south of the Horn.

His mother was not where he had left her. The house on Bridge Street Court had a stranger
in it who said the family had moved off three years back; her name was not in the city
directory; and he went from the old street into newer ones with the paper of the address
wearing soft in his hand, until on Canton Street he found her.

*Such a joyful meeting as it was!* he wrote — an old man by then, with the whole voyage
behind him and words at his command for anything he cared to set down. *I shall not
attempt to describe it.*

She had lived, in part, on him. The hundred dollars he had sent up from Honolulu the
year before — the money folded into the first letter he ever wrote out with his own hand,
over his own name — had come, she told him, a real Godsend, just in the nick of time. The
message had gone on ahead of the boy and kept the house standing until the boy could
reach it.

For his supper, home from around the world, he asked her for the one thing eighty-seven
thousand miles of ocean had not set before him anywhere on its whole length: some of his
mother's hasty pudding and milk.
