## 1 · The Deplorable Expedition

On the twenty-first of May, 1828, the House of Representatives resolved that a ship be sent
to explore the Pacific Ocean, and President John Quincy Adams ordered the Navy to make one
ready.

The Senate declined to pay for her.

The idea was never really about wonder, and its friends knew better than to argue wonder in
Washington. By the 1830s the United States owned the largest whaling fleet on earth, and the
nation's lamps burned what that fleet brought home. The whales were in the Pacific — off
Chile and Peru, off Japan — in seas that appeared on American charts as guesses, rumors, and
blank paper. Whaleships sailed into the blanks, and some of them did not come back, wrecked
on reefs no chart admitted existed. Every loss was a widow's grief and an owner's argument.
Yet the owners would not pay for charts. A chart lowered the risk for every ship that
carried it, a competitor's ship included, and no merchant in New Bedford was going to buy
safety for the man across the street. Only a government could afford to want the Pacific
charted, and in May of 1836, eight years after Adams' ship went nowhere, Congress
authorized a United States Exploring Expedition and voted the money.

The two years that followed nearly killed it at its moorings.

The Secretary of the Navy, Mahlon Dickerson, was a cultivated man of sixty-six who
professed a love of science and handled the expedition like a man waiting for it to die of
natural causes. He dithered over the ships, the scientists, the stores, everything.
President Jackson, impatient, bolted a second authority alongside him — Commodore Thomas ap
Catesby Jones, an old comrade from the Battle of New Orleans, to command the squadron and
pick its officers — and the two authorities ground against each other from the first week.

The officer corps looked the enterprise over and wanted none of it. There was no war in it,
no prize money, no glory of the kind the service kept books on: four years of carrying
scientists to the end of the world and pulling a survey boat around reefs. Chasing pirates
was a career. Surveying was a chore. Senior officers declined the command, or took it and
drifted off. Sailors shipped for the voyage lay at anchor until their enlistments ran out
under them. The newspapers found their name for the whole affair and wore it smooth: the
Deplorable Expedition.

Then 1837 arrived and concentrated minds. The banks failed in the spring. A new president,
Van Buren, inherited the wreckage. And word came over the Atlantic that France had Dumont
d'Urville bound for the far south, with a British expedition under James Clark Ross fitting
out behind him. The bottom of the world was going to carry somebody's name, and it had
begun to look as though the name would not be American. In November, Commodore Jones, his
health gone and his patience gone after it, resigned the command in disgust.

The expedition now had ships, money, instruments, and scientists. It had no commander,
because every officer in the United States Navy senior enough to take it had refused it.

## 2 · The Depot

Charles Wilkes was born in New York City in 1798, a great-nephew of the English radical
John Wilkes. His mother died when he was three. The aunt who took him in, Elizabeth Ann
Seton, would convert to Catholicism, found an order of sisters, and become, long
afterward, the first American-born saint of the Catholic Church; widowed and overwhelmed,
she sent the boy away to boarding school. Columbia College followed, and then, in 1818,
the Navy.

It was a navy that kept its accounts in seniority and broadsides, and the midshipman from
Columbia brought it neither. What he brought was measurement. He learned his mathematics
under Nathaniel Bowditch himself; triangulation under Ferdinand Hassler of the Coast
Survey — the surveyor's geometry, which lays a measured line along a beach, takes angles
from its two ends, and walks a whole coastline onto paper by arithmetic; the earth's
magnetism under James Renwick. In 1833, on the strength of his survey of Narragansett Bay,
the Navy gave the lieutenant its Depot of Charts and Instruments: a modest office in
Washington where the service's charts were kept and corrected, and its chronometers were
rated.

A chronometer is Greenwich time in a box — a spring-driven clock slung in pivots so no roll
of the sea can tilt it. Longitude is nothing more than the difference between the sun
overhead at local noon and the hour inside the box, which makes the box the one instrument
a deep-water ship cannot navigate without. Every chronometer gains or loses a little each
day, at a rate all its own, and the Depot's business was to learn each clock's habit to the
second, so its error could be subtracted at sea. It was tedium of a high order, and Wilkes
did it superbly.

He had been circling the expedition almost from the day it was authorized. Dickerson
recommended him early; Jones distrusted him as green; no appointment came. Wilkes made
himself useful anyway — organizing, corresponding, drawing up lists, assigning midshipmen —
and the Depot, its few rooms of charts and rated clocks off a Washington street, filled up
after dark, one lamp kept burning over the work, with a small crew of young officers who
caught his energy like a fever. One of them was a Pennsylvania midshipman — the Navy's apprentice
grade of officer — named William Reynolds, whose letters home to his sister Lydia held the
lieutenant up as a marvel.

The letters had a year of warmth left in them.

Among the instruments that gathered for the voyage were dip circles — a magnetized needle
hung in an upright brass ring, free to tip its point down toward the pole and read off the
ring's scale the slant of the earth's magnetism. The readings the needles would someday give
were bound for a project nobody in that lamplit office described in large terms: Gauss's observatories
at Göttingen, the British stations, needles to be read on every continent at agreed hours,
the first experiment mankind ever ran on the whole planet at once. At the Depot they called
it the magnetic work, and packed the needles in straw.

## 3 · The crates

In 1836 Dickerson sent Wilkes to Europe to buy the expedition's instruments — five months
in London and Paris, in the workshops of the best instrument-makers alive. He came home
with superb theodolites — the angle-takers of Hassler's measured geometry, ground to read
down to seconds of arc — and with chronometers. He came home, too, with pendulum apparatus
for weighing the shape of the earth itself, since a pendulum swung against a clock ticks out
the local strength of gravity, and gravity, taken station by station around a globe, gives
the globe's true figure. On the astronomical side it was an outfit fit for the Royal Society.

The expedition's naturalists went through their own crates twice. There was not so much as
a microscope.

Their fury cost him. When the civilian corps was organized, Wilkes was offered a place on
the expedition after all — as its astronomer, a berth beneath the very men whose
denunciations of him were barely dry. He refused, and not quietly: he had spent years on
the project, and he would go at the head of work he was fit to lead or he would not go.

Then, instead of sulking, he went to sea. In the spring of 1837 he took the brig
*Porpoise* out over Georges Bank and the Nantucket shoals — the deep fishing grounds off
Cape Cod, east of the Massachusetts coast he would come home to that fall — water the
merchants were desperate to see charted and no officer would volunteer a season for — and he carried his
new European instruments along, testing his own purchases against the fog. The season's
charts made his name with the Cape Cod fishermen, with the merchants, with the politicians
who answered to both; in Boston, old Bowditch praised the work to his face and returned
days later to say it again. The survey was real — dangerous, useful, visible. It was also
addressed. The Navy Department had never once asked Charles Wilkes a question, and here
was the answer anyway, engraved and bound, waiting for the question to arrive.

The last packet of letters to reach the brig at Charlestown that fall came aboard
water-stained. The sailing orders were inside.

## 4 · The interview

Van Buren, finished with Dickerson, handed the wreck to his Secretary of War: Joel
Poinsett, the traveled, capable former minister to Mexico, remembered now, when he is
remembered at all, for the red Christmas flower that carries his name. Poinsett moved
fast. His first choice for the command was Captain Joseph Smith, popular and able, who
named one condition of his own — that Wilkes come along as his surveyor. Wilkes, summoned
to Washington in March 1838 fresh from the shoals, heard the arrangement out and declined
it. He would have had a vessel of his own, but under Smith — and with officers senior to
himself berthed aboard her, free to give an order on the deck of his own ship and have it
obeyed over his head. It was the astronomer's berth again, wearing epaulettes.

He said no.

Some in the service had smelled a deeper game all winter: a man who declines every seat
below the head of the table is bargaining for the head of the table. "I never thought of
such a thing," Wilkes said afterward. "I was too young an officer to aspire and did not
dream of it." Smith's health failed; the arrangement collapsed; and Poinsett was left
holding the Navy list with every name above a certain line already crossed off — each by
its own hand.

What remained on the list was awkward, and could not be argued with. Wilkes was a
lieutenant of forty who had not held a sea command since his twenties. He was also,
verifiably, the best surveyor in the United States Navy, endorsed by Bowditch himself; and
he wanted the expedition, which in the spring of 1838 made him a class of officer with one
member in it.

On an evening that spring, Poinsett had him to his house — a lamp lit, a low fire, two
chairs drawn up to it — and, in everything but name, interviewed him. Wilkes took neither
the second chair nor his ease; he stood to the fire the length of it. No clerk kept the
conversation; what survives is what he laid on the table, which was the whole voyage, drawn
as if he had already sailed it. A young squadron:
junior officers holding acting commands — rank lent for the voyage, not yet confirmed by
the department — men still soft enough to shape, with no seniors aboard to fight him. The force rebuilt: the big ships kept for the open ocean and the long
passages, small craft added that could swim in the Pacific's shoal lagoons, where the
survey would be won or lost. The bill paid out of the scientific corps, cut from the grand
planned twenty-odd civilians down to a working handful — the astronomy, the surveying, and
the physics of the earth gathered into his own hands. Poinsett put the one question ten
years of failure had taught him to ask: could all this truly be organized in months?
Wilkes assured him it could.

Poinsett told him the command was his.

By Wilkes' own account he was struck speechless. He recovered. The appointment came down
in April 1838, and the United States Navy erupted. Protests piled into the War Department
in writing: senior officers who had spent a decade declining the expedition discovered, on
learning who had it, that the honor of the service now required it for themselves.
Poinsett held. The rules of seniority, he answered, were rules for war, and this was not a
war command — which was true, and consoled nobody.

One promise traveled with the appointment. The Navy's highest working rank was captain;
everything above it was courtesy. A lieutenant at the head of a squadron would spend four
years giving orders to men who stood above him on the only list the service believed in,
so Poinsett gave his assurance that Wilkes, and William Hudson, the friend who would sail
as his second, would have captains' appointments — acting ones at the least — before the
ships left the country. Then Poinsett fell gravely ill, and the Navy Department discovered
that the promotions were properly some other office's business. That summer the president
himself came down to Norfolk to review the squadron, and Wilkes took the review at its head,
still a lieutenant: white trousers and blue jackets, guns fired in salute where band music
should have been, and a grand party at which the senior officers of the Navy were
conspicuous by their regrets. The announcement everyone had come expecting was not made. The
guns saluted, the word *captain* went unsaid over a lieutenant's epaulettes, and the
president went back up the bay.

Wilkes kept the command. In twenty years it was the one thing the service had ever handed
him outright, and it came to him on paper: six ships, half the world's blank water, and a
lieutenant's commission to do it on.

He closed his hand around it.
