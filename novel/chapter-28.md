## 1 · New York, the summer of 1842

TRIAL OF LIEUT. WILKES. The words ran across the top of the column, the same line reprinted above every column of testimony, week after week, all that season. He had come up the harbor in June at the head of a squadron; the newspapers gave him, in print, the rank the Navy List — the service's own roster of officers — had always kept for him, and set it in that running head above his own testimony.

The court sat aboard the *North Carolina*, one of the Navy's largest ships, laid up at anchor in the harbor. It was a season of prosecutions that ran both ways at once: Wilkes trying the officers who had crossed him, and the officers, their evidence marshaled by the surgeon Guillou, trying Wilkes.

And he had asked for it. The court had been ordered, the papers reported, crediting the Journal of Commerce, "by the special request of lieut. Wilkes, to investigate the causes of some misunderstanding which arose between lieut. Wilkes and some of his officers." The machinery he had called for would run three months. It would find one thing.

The acquittals came back through the summer over the Secretary's signature, one officer at a time, and each one printed the charge the commander had brought. Lieutenant Johnson — who had taken a party across the Cascade mountains on horseback into the fur country of the Northwest — had been tried on "disobedience of orders and wasting the public property," on charges preferred by lieut. Charles Wilkes, and acquitted. *A. P. Upshur.* That was the size of the thing the commander had carried into court: the survey of a continent, argued down to a clerk's complaint about spoiled stores, and thrown out.

When the Navy did convict one of the officers, it showed the country the instrument it kept for the purpose. A full reprimand ran in the Register in August, addressed to one of the men Wilkes had accused: guilty of "disrespect to your superior in the execution of his office," an offense that "although it involves no moral turpitude… strikes at the foundation of all discipline. A respectful deportment is part of the duty of obedience, and obedience is the first law of military service." The whole liturgy of the service, set in type. The same voice would speak to Wilkes within the month.

## 2 · The paper turns

For four years the expedition had run on documents — sailing orders in a wet hat, sealed instructions, the broad blue pennant that flew at the masthead to mark a commodore, which he had hauled down himself in the harbor in June, a claim written in ink and dared to hold. Now the documents turned around and read their keeper.

The confidential orders came out first: extracts from the private instructions James K. Paulding had issued Wilkes before the ice, in December 1839, entered now as evidence and set in type for anyone with three cents. Then the exhibits — his own Sydney dispatch of the eleventh of March, 1840, read aloud in court by the judge advocate, the court's lawyer; and testimony that walked back over the instruments themselves, a witness allowing that the compass that took the ship's bearings "was so sluggish" its needle would not settle true — the continent Wilkes had bled a squadron to reach, brought into a New York courtroom and made to account for its own longitude.

And in September, four lines easy to miss between the trials: four marines "who allege themselves to have been maltreated by Lieut. Wilkes, on board of the Vincennes, and other vessels," had brought suit against him in the United States court, and "laid their damages to a large amount." Enlisted men going at their commander through the plain civil law that owned everyone alike, as citizens, with a claim. At sea they had been hands, entered by rate on a ship's book; the court called them citizens. No court of the sea had ever given a flogged man that door. These four had gone looking and found another one.

## 3 · The sentence

On the tenth of September the defense was read, the court cleared the room and sat in secret, and the verdict went south to Washington in an envelope. Four years and eighty-seven thousand miles, folded up and mailed to the department that had launched it.

It came back an acquittal with a single exception. On the loss of the *Peacock* at the mouth of the Columbia: not proven against him. On the oppression the officers' journals had recorded for four years: not proven. Count after count, the same finding, until the record read like a man walking clear of everything he had done. And then the one charge that held. Illegal punishment: the lash brought down on a bared back past the lawful twelve at the Honolulu gangway, twice that number of strokes and more, on men whose only offense had been to say their signed time was up. Of all of it, that.

The sentence was itself a letter, dated the twenty-second of September, and the Register printed it whole:

> Navy department, 22d September, 1842. — Sir: You have been duly tried by a court martial… and sentenced to [be publicly reprimanded by] the secretary of the navy, at such time and place as he may deem proper. This sentence is approved. The country which honored you with a command far above the just claims of your rank in the navy, had a right to expect that you would, at least, pay a scrupulous respect to her laws. The rebuke, which, by the judgment and advice of your associates in the service, she now gives you, for having violated those laws in an important particular, involving the rights of others of her citizens, will be regarded by all as the mildest form in which she could express her displeasure. I am respectfully your obedient servant, A. P. UPSHUR. — Lieut. Charles Wilkes.

Every clause found a nerve. *A command far above the just claims of your rank*: the government put in one line what the whole voyage had turned on — the commodore who was a lieutenant, the wound now pressed by the same hand that had cut it. *The rights of others of her citizens*: the men. The four marines with their writ, the sailors at the gratings, and behind them, unnamed and unreached, every back a captain's temper had ever opened. The government's word for them was *citizens*, and it was the word the sea never used.

## 4 · Below decks

The Register set the sentence in its columns and went on with the day. The act to publish the expedition's discoveries had passed that same season — "under the command of lieutenant Wilkes," the rank following him even into the law — on a page it shared with an act to regulate the pay of pursers. The reprimand ran among notices of about that weight. The country that had sent four ships around the world took the reckoning in stride, filed it between smaller business, and turned the leaf.

Below decks there is nothing to turn to.

Charlie Erskine was twenty that year, a coxswain paid off with the rest, and the whole trial ran in a room he was never in, in the one thing he had spent these very years teaching himself at last: the written word. The memoir he would set down in Boston fifty years on — the book that kept the pin, the mother's face at the door, the colt across his knuckles at the age of fifteen — gives the courts-martial not a line. He had followed Wilkes down through a skylight in the dark once, stood over him with the pin, and had not brought it down. He never followed him to Washington.

The one crime the United States ever proved against Charles Wilkes was the kind that had bent a boy over the breech of a gun in 1838, and the body's book does not say so, because the man who wrote it never learned the verdict was its own. That reckoning lives in only one place — where the two papers can be laid side by side, the printed sentence and the page the old man left blank.

The sentence survives where it was always going to survive: on paper, in the Register, under a running head that in all those columns, all that summer, never once let him be the commodore. TRIAL OF LIEUT. WILKES. And the page turned to the pay of pursers.
