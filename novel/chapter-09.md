## 1 · At sea, 14 July 1839

Two days out of Callao, with the coast of South America gone under the eastern rim and
nothing ahead but the ocean the whole enterprise had been built to cross, the commander
had his order read to the ships' companies from the quarter-deck — the officers' ground at
the after end of the deck, back toward the stern, where a man stood to be obeyed.

It was a good order. Reynolds stood in the ranks with the rest, hats off, the sun already
white on the deck planks, and heard it out in the reading officer's flat quarter-deck voice.
The squadron was to show the islanders courtesy and kindness; it was to avoid every act of
force; its aim, the order said, would be "peace and good-will." A man could set his name
under each word of it.
Then, near the end, the same order promised "most exemplary punishment" to any of them
who wronged a native, and promised it in the same even voice, so that the mercy and the
threat came off the one page in the one breath.

The drum, the watch, the ocean. The order went into the order-book and into no one's
conduct that anyone could afterward point to. Reynolds copied a fair sense of it into his
own pages and turned to the work ahead, which was to make paper out of the blank third of
the world.

## 2 · The sorting

Long before an island showed, the men knew which ones were bad.

The knowledge came off the whaleships and the trading brigs, handed down the forecastles of
two oceans: this atoll would swap pearl shell for a fishhook and thank you, that one would
fight, this shore had taken a boat's crew in some year no one
could quite fix, and the man who told it had it from a man who had it from another. The charts
carried the same freight in their margins, where they carried anything at all — a name, a
doubt, a warning in a dead man's hand. The squadron stood into the Dangerous Archipelago with
its islands already dealt into two heaps, the hostile and the hoped-for, and not a single one
of them yet met.

Reynolds felt the sorting in himself and did not much question it. Later, well into the
archipelago, with hostile beaches already behind him, the boats would be waved off the reef
at one island before they could land — the people ranged along the strand with a plainness
that wanted no interpreter — and he would come away sorry, and set the sorriness down. He
"regretted exceedingly that we could not land," he wrote, "particularly because the other
Islanders had been so hostile." Hostility was still, at twenty-three, a thing he could be
curious about. It had not yet
occurred to him to ask what the islanders had been sorting the ships into, or on what
evidence, or how much older and better founded their heap might be.

## 3 · Clermont-Tonnerre, 13 August 1839

They raised the first atoll at dawn: a smudge of palms that seemed to grow straight up out
of open water, then a line of white where the sea broke on a reef that ran down under the
boats like the face of a cliff. The charts called the place Clermont-Tonnerre. The people
living on it called it Reao, and had not been asked.

Boats down, the falls paying out through the blocks, the crews dropping hand over hand into
them. Reynolds wedged himself onto a thwart, the boat's hard cross-bench, with his board and
his instruments, and the lead went over and came up and went over, the sun already hard on the
back of his neck, the reef water so clear it lied about its depth. The big vessels stood off
and on beyond the break, working back and forth under sail to hold their ground, while the
boats did the near work, and the near work was all angles: ship to boat, boat to shore, the
same stretch of reef caught from three places at once, the pencil striking each bearing down
against the board until a line stood there that no chart had ever held. The survey wanted the shore. The
shore did not want the survey. Along the beach the men of Reao stood with spears and with clubs,
and a shout went out over the water, one word pitched to carry across the reef, that the
Tahitian interpreter caught only the shape of, though its meaning carried without him: *go*.

Somebody had taught them that word for ships. Whalers, traders, whatever hulls had come
before over whatever years — the record does not say, and the men of Reao were not writing
it down; but the welcome they gave was the settled welcome of people who had met the sort
before and kept the account.

Somewhere among these first atolls — the record does not agree on which one, or whether it
was this one — the squadron sent its own voice ashore. John Sac went into the water.

He was a New Zealander, one of the crew, a chief in his own country and an excellent sailor in
this one, and the nearest thing the squadron owned to a tongue these islands might answer. He
went over the side and swam in through the surf, and the surf took him and set him down in the
shallows, and he stood there streaming and hailed the beach in a tongue that was not his own
either, a Pacific man carrying a republic's compliments to a people who had already declined
them. The answer came back, and Erskine had it by heart more than fifty years after and set it
down word for word: *Go to your own island. This belongs to us.*

The boats tried the rest of the catalogue — patience, then a fishhook and a length of cloth
held up in the air and shaken, then the whole dumb-show of goodwill a survey carries in place
of a language. Reao was not buying. And the commander would not enter his first island onto
the chart as unexamined.

The order was bird-shot. Small stuff, the kind that stings and scatters and does not, when
the gunner means it not to, kill. Along the boats the guns came up to the shoulder, and the
flat reports rolled out over the water one on another, and the shot went in low across the
shallows and peppered the sand at the islanders' feet. The men of Reao flinched, wavered, and
broke, the whole line of them off the open sand in the time it takes to say it. And the boats
pulled in over the ground that had just been emptied for them, and the lead went over, and the
soundings came up one by one, and the reef grew on Reynolds' board a length at a time even as
the last of the islanders went back into the trees.
When it was done no body lay on the sand. By the arithmetic of the age it was a clean
morning's work, nothing lost that a man aboard would think to enter in a column.

The first shots the United States Exploring Expedition ever fired, it fired here — at the
people it had crossed an ocean to set down on paper, over a landing they had told it plainly
they refused.

## 4 · A Sunday

Some days on, or some islands on — the journal keeps a truer count of latitude than of the
week — they coasted a lagoon island on a Sunday afternoon, and the ocean turned and showed
him the other face it had been keeping, the one the forecastle stories had all been about.

He had earned the sight, if soreness earns anything. The archipelago had been weeks of this
by now, the boats down at dawn and up at dark, the thwart printing its edge into him, the salt
drying white in the seams of his coat, his own bones teaching him a lesson he would not have
believed a rowing boat had it in her to teach. And then, on the odd afternoon, this: a
Sunday, and a lagoon, and nothing asked of him but to look.

From the boat, and better from aloft, the whole ring lay open under the eye: the water inside
it a blue that nothing in Lancaster or on any sea since had made him ready for, a reef set
round it for a green frame, the palms standing along the strand, "as a screen, a drop curtain,"
as he would write of them.
Several hundred of the islanders moved along the shore beneath the trees, showing and hiding
as the boats drew down the coast, there and gone and there again. He watched them the way a
man watches a play.

And he took up his pen and wrote the lagoon down whole — the transparent blue, the rich green
frame, the water lying beneath the eye as though it had been "created for no impurer use than
to yield up the pearls that abound in its depths."

Several hundred people were moving through the trees while he wrote it.

He had counted them himself.
