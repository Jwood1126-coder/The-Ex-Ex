## Rio de Janeiro, November 1838

The bay took Reynolds by the throat, the way it had when he was a boy on his first cruise.
He had come in past the Sugarloaf once before and never got over it — the bare granite peak
that stands sentry at the harbor mouth — and here it was again, unchanged, waiting on him.
"'Tis the same as ever," he wrote that night — "the same enchanting beauties, the same
wondrous prospects… that charmed my young heart some eight years ago."

The anchorage was a world's fair of navies, and the ranking American in it was not the
exploring squadron. At her moorings lay the *Independence*, flagship of the Brazil
Squadron, wearing the broad pennant that marks a commodore — an officer set over many
ships at once. As the squadron worked in past her, her band manned the rail and played
"Hail Columbia" across the water: the courtesy of the service, a welcome and an honor.
The custom called for an answer. The arriving ships were to fire a salute of guns.

Wilkes did not fire.

The reason was sound, and the wardroom — the officers' own mess, where word like this
always got to first — knew it. The squadron's chronometers and magnetic
instruments — the delicate clocks and needles the whole scientific mission rode on — had
been tuned over painstaking weeks in Virginia, and the concussion of a salute could knock
them false in an afternoon. A commander guarding his instruments had every right to keep
his powder in the lockers. But Wilkes did not trouble to send that reason across the water
with his compliments, and so what the commodore saw from the *Independence*'s quarterdeck
was a lieutenant, newly come in, declining to salute his senior.

Then the paper started.

Notes went back and forth over a few hundred yards of Brazilian water, a boat pulling each
one across and pulling the answer back — oars tossed and dripping alongside the
*Independence*, a midshipman reaching a folded sheet up to the flagship's rail, the ink on
it scarcely dry — and the commodore's side observed every letter of the regulations. Which
meant that every note off the flagship was addressed, correctly, to
*Lieutenant Wilkes*. Mister Wilkes. To a lieutenant the service says Mister; the title of a
rank it keeps for the man who holds the rank. Wilkes commanded a squadron and answered to
Mister, on paper, in a fair clerk's hand, and the whole wardroom read the notes as they
came, because notes like those are always read.

Reynolds had been watching the commander since Madeira: the figure on the flagship's deck
with a spyglass to his eye, turned not out to the sea but across the water at his own ships,
as though loyalty were a thing a man could catch in the act of failing. Now he watched him
spend the days of a good port on a quarrel about a courtesy no one had asked him to
withhold, and he marked the squalls of it in his journal like weather.

No shot fired at the expedition in four years ever went home harder than the one that was
never fired at all.

## The hold

Brazil landed more enslaved Africans than any country on earth, and the great bay carried
slave ships as ordinarily as it carried grocers and fruit-boats. One of them lay at anchor
under British guard — a brig taken at sea by the anti-slaving patrol, waiting on a
prize-court, the court ashore that would decide who owned her now and free whoever was
still alive below — and Charlie went out to her with a boat's crew and was standing on her deck
in the sun when they knocked off the hatch.

"Such a cloud of steam and such horrible smell issued that it staggered everybody on
deck," he wrote. Grown men, sailors who had smelled a foul hold before, put a hand across
the face and stepped back from the square hole in the planking as from the mouth of an
oven. What came up was warm. It had been three hundred people breathing in the dark under
a battened deck, in the heat off the Line.

"They found only thirty living human beings out of three hundred."

He set the number down plainly, the way he set down everything, and gave it one sentence
more and no less: "This was only one of the many horrors of the African slave-trade."

That same day, aboard the squadron, the official journals were kept as the order required.
The day's run, the soundings, the state of the rigging, the noon weather — a fair hand in a
ruled book, submitted weekly for the commander's eye. Not one of them held a column for
what came up out of that hold. The boy who owned no book, and could not have written a line
in one, was the one who kept it.

## The songs

Over everything in the anchorage rode HMS *Thunderer*, a British ship of the line — ninety
guns stacked in her tiers and a thousand men to work them, more force in one hull than the
Americans could put together out of six. Her people knew their history. Of an evening the
songs came across the dark water: the old victories, the *Chesapeake* and the *Shannon* —
the frigate the British had taken off Boston inside living memory — sung on purpose, at the
new arrivals, night after night. They "aggravated the American patriotism," Charlie wrote,
which was the object. The men took it standing at the rail in the dark and swallowed it
with the one comfort a young navy had to hand — that their fathers had beaten this same
navy anyway, songs or no songs.

Wilkes let it run a while. Then he answered it. He had the fifty best voices in the
squadron mustered on deck, and he turned them loose. "Yes, my man," he told the man who
brought him the matter — "give it to them in thunderous tones, with plenty of Yankee
lightning." Fifty Americans sang the war back over the water, and when they were done the
*Thunderer* gave them a cheer for it — three cheers, and three more, and three more again,
a thousand English throats saluting the noise the small ships had made.

It was the same man who would not fire one gun for a commodore, and would not be out-sung
by a thousand Englishmen.

## Mother

Bill Roberts was a Boston boy, before the mast like Charlie, and at Rio the letter-bag held
something for him. He read his two aloud on the berth deck — the low, crowded space below
where the crew slung their hammocks — with the other boys drawn in around him. It made
Charlie feel badly to hear them. Nothing in the bag was his. He could not have read it if
it had been.

He got up and went further down, to the yeoman's storeroom — the yeoman being the man who
kept the ship's small stores, his storeroom a locker deep in her, close and dark and
smelling of tar and slop-cloth and tallow — and there, by one lamp, he asked to be taught
to write.

He started with a single word. He had the yeoman set it down for him first, a true copy to
work from, and then he went at it with a will — the letters coming out wrong, and coming
out wrong again, and slowly coming out right, sheet after sheet of the storeroom's paper
gone under one word repeated. The word was *mother*. He "covered many fathoms of paper with
that precious name."

Two years back he had signed his name to the Navy's articles with a mark, because a mark
was the only shape he knew how to make. Now, four thousand miles from her, in the dark at
the bottom of a ship, he sat teaching his hand the letters of the one word he had said all
his life — building himself, a stroke at a time, a way to carry across the water.

The way was one word long. The letters that counted still came to the other boys and were
read to him by the other boys; at Valparaíso, months on, it was still Roberts who "got his
usual number, and read them to me." But the word was made, and it was hers, and it was
first.

Fifty-two years later an old man in Boston would fill three hundred pages in that same
hand. He had learned his letters to send
one word home. He never did stop setting it down.
