## 1 · Levuka, May 1840

The squadron came into Fiji carrying two years of stories. Every crew in the Pacific kept a version of the group, and the versions agreed: hundreds of islands netted together by reefs no one had honestly charted, the best bêche-de-mer grounds in the ocean — a sea slug, gathered off the coral and dried and smoked into a trade cargo — and the bones of the ships that had gone in after it. That was the water. Ashore, the stories said, lived a people who killed the shipwrecked and ate them. The eating was not invention, or not entirely: cannibalism was real in the islands, a thing of their wars and their rites, and the beach traders who carried the tales had seen part of what they improved on.

What the stories left out was everything else. Fiji was kingdoms — great chiefs running long games of war and tribute and marriage, thousands of people living under law — and the squadron came in as a new piece in games that were old before its anchors found bottom.

On the eighth of May, 1840, the ships anchored at Levuka, on the island of Ovalau, and the first king to come aboard brought his own American with him. Tanoa, the old king of Bau, the most powerful chief in the group, arrived to conclude commercial regulations with the United States — terms of trade, protection for American vessels — and the words went both ways through a Nantucket man named David Whippy. Half a step behind the throne, his son watched: the one the world would come to know as Cakobau.

Whippy had jumped ship eighteen years before and stayed. He was a man of standing at Levuka now — married into the islands, fluent in the language, trusted by the chiefs and by the shipmasters, two sets of men who otherwise could barely see each other across a beach — and translation was his living: pilot, interpreter, and a walking file on every reef and ruler in the group. Wilkes engaged him for all three.

He was not the only one of his class. On the hill above the beach the squadron raised its observatory — portable houses carried out from New York, tents, a village of instruments a quarter-mile up from the water — and among its callers was Paddy Connell, a red-headed runaway Irish convict, forty years in the islands, five wives, forty-eight children, and a forty-ninth born while the ships lay at anchor. His one prayer was to live to see the fiftieth. Between Whippy at the king's elbow and men like Connell on the beach, the expedition could ask a question in one world and understand the answer from the other. In Fiji that was worth more than a fifth ship.

## 2 · The boats

Then the squadron dissolved into boats — seventeen of them before the season was done. They bore menagerie names, Greyhound and Fox and Turtle and Leopard and Elephant, and mustered to the call of the boatswain, whose shout could put a boat's crew over the side in a minute: "Away, there, you Leopards, away!" Two officers and five men to a boat.

An island became a chart by triangles: a signal planted on this height and on that one, a man in the boat sighting the angle between the two through his instrument and reading it off the scale, the lead swung all day in the passes — the gaps in the reef a ship could thread. Then the next island, and the next, until the group lay on paper.

Charlie was once sent out alone in the dinky — the smallest boat the ship carried — with a lead line and a compass, to drive a signal-staff into the sand of a beach by himself.

Under the boats the coral went down in shrubbery, pink and yellow and white and orange, and every living thing on it vanished at once when a shark came over the reef.

The written orders every boat officer carried were Wilkes' own. Boarding-nettings rigged — netting laced above the boat's rail, so no rush of men could come over it. All trade passed across the stern. Arms ready; no landing on inhabited ground; the boats never to separate at night. Of the people whose islands were being measured, the standing orders said: "They are in no case to be trusted."

So the boats bartered at noon and watched all night. Crews were out fifteen and eighteen days at a stretch without ever leaving them, salt drying stiff in their clothes, the ache of the thwart in every back, sleeping on their arms over black water. This was what Congress had bought the whole voyage for, and the boats delivered it to the last sounding; it cost a season in which no man in them ever fully stood down.

## 3 · Rewa

The oldest piece of American business in Fiji was six years old, and it had a name on it.

In August 1834 the brig *Charles Doggett*, of Salem, had been working bêche-de-mer at Ono, in the south of the group, under contract with a chief named Vendovi, a brother of the king of Rewa. The arrangement ran on the islands' ordinary contract law: trade goods for the catch, and a minor chief lodged aboard the brig as a living guarantee of the shore party's safety. The guarantee was played. The hostage pleaded illness and was set ashore; the next day he came back with Vendovi and his men, asking medicine at the trading house; and when the mate came in with his party, the Fijians killed them — all but one man, who swam for the ship. The captain ransomed back the bodies of his dead. Save one: a Black American sailor whose body never came back at all, and whose fate the traders reported in one word. The affair went home in depositions and owners' complaints, and it lay in Wilkes' orders now as named business: American blood was to be shown to have a price, and the price was Vendovi.

The *Peacock*, under Hudson, was surveying at Rewa — another kingdom in the group, a separate coast from Levuka — when the chance came. There was no bombardment and no landing party. The royal family of Rewa — Vendovi's brothers, the king among them — came aboard to a feast, as chiefs did; and when the eating was done, Hudson told them, with all the manners of the occasion, that they would remain his guests until Vendovi became his prisoner. It was the trap of hospitality, sprung by the visitors who had spent six years condemning it.

The king's summons went ashore in the hands of Ngaraningiou — of all the chiefs at Rewa, Vendovi's known enemy and rival. He went straight to Vendovi's house, went in, took a seat beside him, laid a hand on his arm, and told him he was wanted: the king had sent for him, to go on board the man-of-war. Vendovi agreed at once and began to make ready. "Not till to-morrow," Ngaraningiou said. The two men passed the evening and the night together, and in the morning embarked.

His brothers were aboard the *Peacock*, and would stay aboard until he came. He came. In the ship's cabin, before the king, the chiefs, and the officers, he was examined, and acknowledged the killings at Ono as his own causing, and admitted that he had held the mate by the arms while his people clubbed him. Hudson said that if Vendovi had not been taken, he would have thought it his duty to burn Rewa. The prisoner was put in irons.

When Wilkes came to write the capture up, he drew a lesson from it: the affair, he said, "shows the force of the customs to which all ranks of this people give implicit obedience."

It does.

## 4 · The leave-taking

The royal party was now free to go. What the *Peacock*'s deck saw before it went was this.

Kania, the king, seated himself at Vendovi's right side and took hold of his arm. Navumialu placed himself at the left. Phillips walked up and down in front of them. "All shed tears," the *Narrative* records, "and sobbed aloud while conversing in broken sentences with their brother." The Rewa people on deck wept with them. Of the whole company, none but Ngaraningiou remained unmoved.

Phillips was the most dejected of them all — Phillips, whose own brother, Seru, Vendovi had helped to kill. He explained it: as long as Seru had lived, he could be saucy; after his death he was all alone, "just like a stick." Wilkes set the sentence down as inconsistency.

Then the king kissed the prisoner's forehead, touched noses with him, and turned away. The lesser chiefs came and kissed his hands. The common people of Rewa crawled up the sun-hot planking to him and kissed his feet.

A young man of Vendovi's household was the last to leave him. He asked to stay aboard, and was refused; he embraced Vendovi's knees and kissed his hands and his feet, and Vendovi placed both his manacled hands on the young man's head and blessed him. The young man went backward away from him toward the ladder, sighing and sobbing, as though his heart would break.

The last thing the king asked of Hudson was for his brother's barber. He asked that Vendovi keep the man — Oahu Sam, a Sandwich Islander, an old whaleman with some English. Sam was received aboard as Vendovi's barber, and the squadron gained, without remarking on it, one more man who could carry words in both directions.

## 5 · The flagship

The islands did not treat the matter as closed. Back at Levuka, word reached the officers that warriors of Vendovi's district meant to fall on the observatory by night and take the commander himself — to secure him, as the report ran, "and by that means compel an exchange of prisoners." A hostage for a hostage. Wilkes slept aboard. The *Vincennes* was hauled around on springs — heavy lines run from her stern to her anchor cable, so her guns could be pointed where they were wanted — with battle-lanterns lit between the guns and the guns loaded with canister, tins packed with musket balls. Six war-canoes lay anchored behind a point.

Charlie stood his watch looking at the dark they lay in. What the night finally produced was one report, from inside the ship's own lines: the sentry John Van Deck's musket, which, the deck agreed, "went off itself."

On the fifth of July the *Peacock* stood into Mbua Bay, on the group's northern coast, and anchored an hour ahead of the flagship. The naturalists were ordered back to the *Vincennes*, and the prisoner was transferred with them. A boat came alongside; a man in irons came up through the gangway onto Charlie's deck, into a wooden country of two hundred strangers.

The men of that country called the officer on its quarterdeck, the raised deck aft where a ship is commanded, commodore; the Navy List called him lieutenant. The man in irons had sat between a weeping king and a weeping chief while the common people of a kingdom crawled to his feet. Rank aboard the *Vincennes* was claimed, disputed, worn on sufferance. Her prisoner was the only man aboard who had never needed to claim his.

Fifty years on, the boy on that deck — an old man in Boston by then, with accounts of his own to settle — would set down his finding on the shipmate the Navy had given him: "a very thoughtful, genial, and pleasant sort of a man."

The *Narrative* spent one clause on the day. The prisoner was transferred to the *Vincennes*, "and remained on board of her until the expiration of the cruise."

The cruise had two years left to run.
