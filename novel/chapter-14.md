## 26 December 1839 · Port Jackson

The colony had made up its mind about them. All through December the Sydney men had come
down to the water to look at the four American ships fitting for the ice, and had gone
away agreeing among themselves that the ships were too frail for it — that the Americans
were young and foolhardy and reckless, that they would not come back, and that this was a
pity, because ashore they were pleasant enough company. One official said as much to
Reynolds' own face and did not trouble to soften it: their vessels were not built to go
among icebergs. Reynolds set it down that night. There was nothing to argue. The orders
had been given, and the ships would obey them, which was the whole of the colony's
misjudgment — they had taken obedience for confidence.

They worked through Christmas Day and stood out of the Heads on the twenty-sixth. A ship
called the *Druid* gave them three cheers as they passed. The cheer went out flat over the
water and was gone.

The sailing order spread the squadron into a line — the *Vincennes*, the *Peacock*, the
*Porpoise*, and the little *Flying Fish* strung abreast across the sea with miles of open
water between each hull, so that four ships could comb the widest band of latitude the far
south allowed, and miss the least of it. It was the tidiest idea on paper the whole
campaign owned. It held for three days. Then a gale came up out of the south and took the
line apart, and after it each ship was alone — which the orders had allowed for anyway, the
next appointed meeting being a bare rock called Macquarie, and after that the ice, where
every vessel would be alone in any case.

Reynolds found he did not mind the solitude. He had come south in the *Peacock*, under
Hudson, off the flagship at last — off the weather side of the quarterdeck, the raised
after-deck where a commander keeps his command and his temper both, and where for a year
and a half a man before the mast, or a passed midshipman near it, had learned to read the
set of one back and know from it how the whole day would go. Hudson's ship was a plainer
place. A man could take a full breath on her.

## New Year's Day, 1840 · at sea

The sea told them where they were before the chart could. A thermometer towed astern
stepped down day by day, a degree and then a degree, as the ships crossed out of the warm
Australian water into the cold circumpolar stream — a border a surveyor could log without
ever seeing it. Then the escort changed. The albatrosses were joined by petrels, and the
petrels by small hard birds that meant ice was making weather somewhere below the round of
the world. Whales blew along the horizon in numbers no man aboard had counted before. New
Year's passed at sea with the glass — the barometer — falling and every ship's company privately doing the
same sum: eight or nine weeks a man could work down here, and the passage down had already
spent two of them.

Cook had come this way sixty-five years before and been stopped everywhere by the pack,
and had written the region its epitaph — that whatever lay farther south, the world would
get no good of it, and he did not envy the man who went looking. Since Cook, the chart of
the quarter now under the squadron's bows was not merely blank. It was an open question
about the planet: a polar continent, or a frozen scatter of islands, or an ice-capped open
sea, and serious men held all three at once. The Ex. Ex. was sailing to find out which of
them the earth was made of.

## 6 January · 53° south

They dressed the ships for it. Little houses of rough board went up over the
companionways — the ladderways down off the deck — to keep the weather from pouring below;
drying stoves were lit between decks for clothes that would not otherwise dry again this
season; the topgallant masts, the slender upper lengths that carry a ship's highest sail,
were struck down and short stumps sent up in their place, so the ice could not load them
and bring them down. Reynolds watched it done and understood the ship was being cut down on
purpose, made small to meet what was coming. A cask was lashed at the masthead, high as a church steeple over the
deck, and a lookout could stand up in it out of the worst of the wind and watch the sea for
what would kill the ship. The men called it the crow's nest.

It was cold in a new way now — not the clean cold of a winter ashore but a wet cold that
got into the wool and stayed. Clothes froze on the man who wore them. The rigging grew a
grey skin of ice that came away in plates when the yards were hauled round, and the plates
came down on the deck like slate off a roof. Reynolds learned to take a bearing with his
mittens off in the time before his fingers stopped answering, and to get them on again
before that time ran out.

## 10 January · 61° south

The first berg came out of the fog like a headland. A wall of ice a mile long and higher
than the mastheads, flat along the top, with the swell breaking white and slow along its
foot. Then a second, and then more than a lookout could number — tabular bergs, flat as
tables and some of them miles from end to end, standing up out of the murk all day as the
ships felt a way among them. The men learned to use them. A boat pulled in under a berg's
lee, out of the wind, and a party quarried the blue ice off its side with picks, and the
coppers below — the ship's great cooking kettles — were filled with it; and the water they drank that week was the sweetest any
of them had tasted at sea, and they said so to one another, and meant it.

## 12 January

Then the loose ice gave way to the thing itself. The barrier: the pack frozen into one
mass, a coast the sea had made out of itself, running east and west past both ends of
sight. The ships crept along it looking for a bay, or a lead of open water that ran south,
and where a bay opened it closed again in ice a few miles in.

That night the wind died altogether. There was no true dark to it — this far south in high
summer the sun only dipped below the sea and came up again a little along the rim, so that
the nights were a blue dusk a man could read a watch by — and in that half-light the
*Peacock* lay off the barrier in a silence Reynolds had not once met in two years at sea.
No wind in the rigging. No sea running. Only, out along the ice, the swash and swash of the
long swell working at the foot of the barrier, unhurried, the way a thing breathes in its
sleep. The men on deck stopped their talking and stood and listened to it. And to the
south, over the ice, there was every appearance of land.

## 16 January

The weather cleared on the sixteenth. Reynolds went up with Henry Eld — Passed Midshipman
Eld, a man who had stood his lieutenant's examination years back and was still waiting on
the vacancy a peacetime navy would not open — the two of them at the masthead, the topmost
place on the ship, where the deck lies far below like a narrow plank and the sea runs
out unbroken to the round edge of the world on every side.

They saw it at the same moment. Beyond the ice field, to the south, two summits — high,
dark against all that white, keeping their shape and keeping their bearing while the bergs
slid past below them. Land holds still. Bergs drift. These held still.

Both men knew to the bone how the far south lies to a masthead. Cold dense air over cold
water bends the light and lofts a real coast into view from far beyond where it has any
right to show, and out of the same trick it raises whole coasts that are not there at all.
Between them Eld and Reynolds had spent the better part of ten years learning the
difference. So they did not come down with it. They stayed aloft and tried it, the way a
man tests a thing he wants too much to believe: watched it against the drift of the bergs,
took its bearing — the compass line from the ship to the thing — and stayed aloft to take
the bearing again an hour on. An hour of the masthead swinging its slow arc across the sky,
of breath clouding and freezing on the wool, of fingers going from cold to numb to useless
in the time it took; and through all of it they put each other's eye to it and asked what
the other saw. It did not slide. It did not fade. It held.

Reynolds could not know, up there, whether he was the first man in the whole history of the
species to look at the thing in front of him or only the latest fool the ice had gulled. He
had the surveyor's answer, which is the bearings and nothing warmer. The bearings were
true. What stood beyond that ice was the coast of a continent no human eye had ever found,
and one of the two summits he was straining his own eyes at carries his name down the
length of the George V Coast today, on every chart printed since — Reynolds Peak, and Eld
Peak alongside it — set there by men who came long after and measured what these two called
first from a rolling masthead and were not believed.

For they were not believed. They came down glowing and reported two mountains to the south,
and Hudson — a fine seaman, a careful recorder, careful most of all with his own name in
his own log — would not write the discovery of a continent into the record on the strength
of two young men's eyes. The sighting went down where it went down at all, with no position
fixed to the hour, with no weight. Reynolds kept the bearings he had. He did not yet know
he would want them one day in a courtroom, with his country's honor and another navy's
sneer both riding on the loose paper of an afternoon. He knew only that he had seen it, and
that Eld had seen it beside him, and that the two of them were right.

Away to the west, over the ice and out of sight, the other ships were feeling along the
same coast — the squadron strung out now for a hundred miles and more against the edge of
the barrier, each hull alone, each sending back word of the same signs: bottom where the
sounding-lead should have found none, birds with the continent's grit in their gullets, the ice
stained brown with its earth. Under all the fog was either a seventh part of the world's
dry land or nothing whatever. Two passed midshipmen in the *Peacock*'s rigging had just
seen which. It would take the century a while to notice.
