## 1 · Honolulu, the enlistments come due

The flagship came over the bar at Honolulu — the shoal across the harbor mouth — on the
strength of the Hawaiians. Several hundred
of them stood in the water to their chests and their chins and walked her in by her lines,
hand over hand, hauling a moored man-of-war across the shoal while her own crew stood along
the rail with nothing to do but watch other men do it well. It was quick work, and clean, and
the *Vincennes* was inside and moored before the afternoon was old.

Then the enlistments came due.

The men had signed for three years. The three years were behind them, and the voyage was not.
After dinner the crew was called aft, and the commodore put the thing to them without
ornament: another eighteen months, and for it three months' pay counted down into a man's own
hand, two weeks' liberty ashore, and every rating's wages lifted by a quarter.

Some had settled it the other way before he opened his mouth. Their time was up; they said so;
they meant to be put ashore as free as the paper made them. Two of the marines would do no
further duty at all, holding that they had served exactly what they had signed and owed the
ship not another hour.

So the quarrel that filled the anchorage the last week of October was arithmetic — a date on an
old paper against a voyage that would not agree to end, and men who stood at muster and would
not turn to.

## 2 · The General Order

On the twenty-eighth a court-martial sat, and what it decided came down to the crew as a
General Order in the commodore's own hand, read at the mast. Three men were named — Michael
Ward, John Riley, and Peter Sweeny — sentenced, the order said, "and the sentences having been
approved by me." Ward was to take a dozen lashes on the bare back with the cat, Riley sixteen,
Sweeny eight; and each man's count was to be laid on not once but, in the order's words,
"alongside each of the three vessels of the squadron now in port." Three ships. Three floggings
to a man.

The cat was not the colt. The colt was a rope's-end a boatswain's mate carried in his hat for
any small thing, and Charlie knew the colt from the breech of a gun at Charlestown. The
cat-o'-nine-tails was the service's own instrument — nine knotted cords on a stock, kept in a
bag until it was wanted and taken out only under a sentence.

The order set down how it would go. "The launch of the *Vincennes* will be rigged with a
platform and gallows" — the launch being the largest of a ship's boats, decked over now for
the purpose and framed at one end with a gibbet — "and a guard of marines, with a boatswain
and three mates, to guard the prisoners and inflict the punishment." It fixed the hour, and it
fixed who would look on: "The crews of the vessels will be dressed, and their rigging and sides
manned, at 10 A.M. on Friday, at which time the punishment will be inflicted." At the foot of
it was the form he always used — *Charles Wilkes, Commanding Exploring Expedition.*

## 3 · Punishment Friday

Friday came fine.

At ten the whole squadron was turned up and dressed — every man mustered in his place, in his
best — and the men of the *Vincennes* went into her shrouds — the tarred rope ladders that run
from her sides up to the mastheads — and lay out along her yards — the crossbars slung from the
masts to spread her sails — and manned her rigging as the order said, because the watching was
meant to do
as much of the work as the lash. Charlie went up with them. No one asked whether he wished to.
A man mustered where he was told.

The launch came alongside under tow, low and heavy with its platform and its little gallows,
the first prisoner already standing in it. He was made fast to the frame: his wrists lashed up
with spun-yarn — the loose rope-yarn a sailor keeps by him for small seizings — and his ankles
seized down to a grating, one of the heavy wooden hatch-lattices laid flat for him to stand on.
Between his feet they set the shot-box, a case of iron round-shot, so that he stood spread, and
could not draw himself closed against the first stroke. One of the gun crew lifted the man's
shirt off his back with his thumb and finger, and the man held still under it and let it be
done. Then the order came up from the boat: *Boatswain's mate, do your duty.*

The count was called aloud, stroke on stroke, the crack of the cat carrying flat across the
water in the forenoon heat, under the manned rigging of the *Vincennes*; and
when it was paid the launch was towed on to the next ship in the line, and the man had it again
there, and again at the third. Three ships to a man. Then the next man was stood against the
frame, and then the last.

Sweeny was an Englishman, shipped at the Bay of Islands, whom the crew themselves had asked the
commodore to be rid of. He was flogged like the others, and after it his jacket was stripped
off him and the Navy's eagle buttons cut from it one at a time, and he was put into the little
dinky, a one-man skiff, and towed ashore stern-foremost — backward, all the way in — to the
tune the fifes kept for the purpose, the Rogue's March.

The port watched every stroke of it. Nine whale-ships lay in the roads with their rigging full;
the fort was full; the housetops and the beach were covered with a mass of human beings — the
whole crossroads of the Pacific turned out to see the American squadron flog three of its own
around the fleet.

Charlie kept his place in the rigging with the rest. His own back had its memory of the cat
from Charlestown, and it woke at every count, so that he did not have to look to know.

Fifty years afterward, writing it down, Erskine copied out the phrase the commodore's own
*Narrative* had used for the practice — *Flogging through the fleet!* — and set his own words
under it, and let the two lie together on the page: "Where is our Christianity? Where is our
civilization?"

## 4 · The table

The articles for the new term lay on a table on deck, with a pen and an inkpot beside them, in
plain sight of the launch. Every man who came aft to be paid came within sight of it — the
platform, the gallows still standing in the boat — and put his name down, or turned and did
not.

Charlie walked to the table.

He was heartily sick of the navy by then, and said so all his life; he had learned more of the
sea in one merchant voyage, he reckoned, than in all his years of it under the flag. He did not
sign for the commodore. He signed because a ship two years out could not spare hands, and he
would not be the man to leave her short in a foreign port.

He had put his first navy articles down with a mark, three years before, because a mark was the
only thing his hand knew how to make. Somewhere in the three years between — no schoolroom, no
lesson anyone thought to record, the letters getting into him by use the way the whole working
of a ship had gone into him through his palms — he had learned to make them. At the table on
the *Vincennes*' deck he wrote his name out in full, every letter of it standing where it
belonged, the first time in his life he had ever done such a thing; and he felt, he said, "as
big and grand as though I were an officer."

For it he was paid three months' wages down and twelve dollars of grog money, and when the ink
had dried he was still a sailor before the mast on the ship of the man who had opened his back
at Charlestown.

## 5 · My first letter

Some of the money he kept back for the liberty ashore. A hundred dollars of it he sent home.

He wrote the covering line himself, the first letter he had ever written. He set down the
ship's name as it
sounded in his ear — the *Vincens* — and told his mother the squadron was to *fair away a
cruseing*, and the spelling fell where a self-taught hand let it fall; and the printer of his
book, half a century on, kept every letter of it exactly as it stood. The hundred dollars went
with it, to be paid over through a Mr. Quincy on Commercial Street in Boston — the same short
run of streets by the water where she had blessed him once and told him to obey his captain and
say his prayers.

She would tell him, when he finally stood in her rooms again two years later, that the money
had come just in the nick of time. That was all still ahead of him.

The launch lay moored astern with its work done. No one had struck the gallows out of it, and
there was no saying it would not be wanted again.
