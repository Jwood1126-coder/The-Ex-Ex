## 1 · Boston, the fall of 1837

In the fall of 1837 the brig *Porpoise* came home to the navy yard at Charlestown,
across the water from the North End of Boston. A brig is one of the small things a navy
floats: two masts where a frigate carries three, a deck a man could walk end to end in
thirty paces. She had spent the season on the coast survey, feeling her way over
Georges Bank and the shallows off Nantucket behind a sounding-lead — a lump of lead on
a marked line, dropped and hauled and dropped again, one depth at a time, until the
bottom of the sea came up on paper as a chart. Her commander was a lieutenant named
Charles Wilkes. The Navy had abler sea-officers than it could employ, and it kept him
for his instruments.

The boy who carried his charts across to the city was Charlie Erskine, of Boston,
fifteen years old, a sailor before the mast — the Navy's phrase for the men who worked
the ship and owned none of her. He had been sent to school and had very seldom gone;
the wharves had taught him instead. His father had gone off when he was small and
stayed gone, and with Charlie it was always mother, mother, mother — he said so
himself, all his life. She had raised her children on nothing, in rooms near the water,
and when he first went to sea she had laid her blessing on him in a back room on
Commercial Street: *Be a good boy, obey your captain, and never forget to say your
prayers.*

He had obeyed his captains from the Grand Banks to the West Indies since. He could
hand, reef, and steer — furl a sail, take it in against a rising wind, hold a course.
The whole working of a ship had gone into him through his palms, and he could not read
a word. When the Navy first signed him, the captain ordered him to put his name to the
ship's articles, and he made his mark where the name should go, because a mark was all
he had.

The day after the brig came in, the captain sent him over the bridge to the city with
the survey's charts, telling him to hurry and be quick. The morning smelled of tar and
low tide.

The charts went to the office of Mr. Nathaniel Bowditch on State Street. Every
deep-water ship on earth carried Bowditch's book — the *Practical Navigator*, the
arithmetic that brought sailors home — and the man himself was old now, and kind. He
looked over the survey's work and said that these were the neatest charts he had ever
seen. Then he gave the boy some advice in a fatherly way, and the boy walked out onto
State Street resolved to disappoint every one, and be somebody.

From State Street he went to the navy agent's office, where he was handed a packet of
letters for the brig. He put them in his hat. A sailor's hat in those days was
tarpaulin — canvas painted black and hard as a board — and it did duty as a satchel. He
had obeyed orders in good shape so far. But the way back ran within sight of his
mother's street, and he had passed his home once already that day without stopping. He
could not do it a second time.

She was very glad to see him. He would remember the embrace, and the *God bless you, my
darling boy!* at the door, all his life. Then he ran for the bridge.

The bridge had a draw in it — a span that swung open to let masted vessels through —
and a schooner was going through the draw. In the press of people waiting at the edge,
Charlie's hat was knocked from his head and went into the harbor, letters and all. He
went after it the quickest way there was: over the rail and down into the schooner's
boat towing alongside, a drop the height of a room, and fished his hat out of the
water. The letters were saved.

The letters were also wet.

## 2 · The gunner's daughter

Back inside the yard, near the dry dock — the great stone basin where a ship is floated
in and the water pumped out from under her — he met the captain, and told him how the
letters had got wet: the schooner, the draw, the harbor. Wilkes said nothing that the
record kept. His face went dark, and he ordered the boy aboard.

He went straight to the landing — the yard's stone steps down to the water, where the
brig's boat lay — and the boat's crew told him the captain was mad as a hornet. They
pulled him out to her anyway; there was nowhere else to go.

As he came up her side, in through the gangway — the gap in a ship's rail where she is
boarded — Lieutenant Boyle ordered the boatswain to introduce him to the gunner's
daughter.

The gunner's daughter was a cannon.

They bent him over the breech of a gun — the heavy sealed end of it, waist-high to a
boy — and held him there, and the boatswain took out his colt. The colt was a piece of
rope about three feet long and half an inch thick, and the boatswain and his mates
carried one coiled in their hats, for immediate use.

The boatswain laid on. The rope came down flat and loud. Charlie worked his right hand
behind him to cover what he could, and the rope cut him across the knuckles, and he
screamed. No one counted the strokes for the record, and no one wrote down the charge,
because there was no charge. There was a captain's temper and a boy's back. Around
them the yard went on with its work.

All this while, the brig lay not a quarter of a mile, in a straight line, from where
his mother lived. If she had been standing at an open window, she could have heard him.

## 3 · The orders

He could not sit down with any comfort for several weeks.

Among the letters, dried out and opened, were the sailing orders the captain had been
expecting.

The brig sailed the next day for the south, and Charlie sailed with her, because a boy
who ships before the mast goes where the ship goes.

Fifty-three years later, an old man in Boston set down what the day had cost, in the
plainest arithmetic he owned: *My ambition was gone, yes, whipped out of me, — and for
nothing.* And then, because he had spent a lifetime among men with backs like his:
*This has been the case with many a sailor.*

Wilkes did not yet have the expedition, and the oath — sworn somewhere in these months,
he never said where — did not yet have its man in reach. The next year supplied both.
