## 1 · Off Point Grenville, April 1841

The fog came down on the Northwest coast in the last days of April, 1841, and the *Vincennes* felt her way north through it toward the Strait of Juan de Fuca with the lead going. This whole coast ran nearly north and south — the Strait of Juan de Fuca opening east into an inland sea at the top of it, the mouth of the Columbia far down the same shore to the south, and a straight run of rock and breaking water between. A man at the rail swung the sounding-lead over the side and sang the depths up as they came — thirteen fathoms, then nine — the bottom rising under her faster than a stranger's coast should rise. The fog stood on the wool of a man's jacket in beads and would not be shaken off. A watch could see the length of the deck and no farther, and the ship went into the white blind, on trust, at the pace of a lead line.

Then the masthead, somewhere overhead where the lookout could see no more than the deck could: *Breakers on the lee bow!*

She was brought up into the wind and held in five fathoms — thirty feet of water under a ship that drew half of it — while the fog thinned enough to show what had called out. Half a mile under her lee stood a head of black rock with the sea breaking white all round its foot: Point Grenville, off the island the old charts had already named Destruction. Fifteen minutes more on the course she had been steering, and she would have gone onto it and to pieces on it, in water no one had sounded.

Charlie had come clear of worse and knew it. He had doubled the Horn in his shirt-sleeves and hugged a pea-jacket off the ice; he had been washed out one port-hole and back through another in a Hatteras blow; he had watched two icebergs close on the ship in the Antarctic dark and felt the whole company give itself up for dead. A fogbank off a nameless head of rock had come nearer to finishing the *Vincennes* than any of it.

This was the coast they had crossed the world to draw. In one cast of the lead it had shown them why it needed the charting.

## 2 · The Bar, July 1841

July the eighteenth, and eighty miles down the same coast the *Peacock* lay off the mouth of the Columbia, waiting to go in.

Where the great river of the country ran out to meet the Pacific, the two of them fought over a ridge of sand thrown up between — a *bar*, the sailors' word for the shoal a river builds across its own doorsill — and the ocean swell, running in over the shallows, tripped there and stood up and broke in a line of white from headland to headland. The ridge shifted with every season, besides; the river was forever unmaking and remaking the one door it owned — the worst river mouth on the coast. That was the whole reason the survey had come so far — to fix in ink a thing that would not hold still to be fixed.

The *Peacock* went at it under Hudson, and struck. She was the rotten one: the ship whose bad timber Norfolk had painted over before she sailed, the ship that two winters back had come clear of the Antarctic pack with thirty hours to spare and a rudder rebuilt by lantern-light in a flooded hold. There was no gale to blame now and no dark to hide it. The Pacific simply arrived on schedule, sea after sea, and worked the old hull open at its leisure, and the roar of the bar carried down the wind for miles.

The order that went out on the sands has few equals in the Navy's books. Save the paper first, it ran, and it was kept to the letter: down into the boats, handed man to man ahead of any sea chest, went the journals and the survey books, the chronometers in the swinging cradles that kept them level, Agate's drawings — three years of soundings and painted birds, the paper soul of the whole cruise, ferried out through the surf before the men who had made it. Reynolds went down into a boat with the record he had kept since Norfolk under his arm. The boats ran the surf again and again. Some were caught wrong and turned end over end, and the men in them went into the cold and were taken up by the next boats coming in, dragged over the gunwales heavy with the water in their wool. When the *Peacock* went finally to pieces, every soul of her stood ashore in Baker's Bay, Hudson the last man off her. Not one lost with the ship.

The little *Flying Fish* was brought in over that same bar, past the wreck, by a Chinook pilot the crews knew as George — one-eyed George, who read that water off the deck of a ninety-six-ton schooner as no printed sailing direction ever could.

The sands where she broke up carry her name yet. Peacock Spit: the one estate the ship ever came to own, on the one coast she had crossed an ocean to survey.

## 3 · Peacockville

They built a camp on the beach out of what the sea handed back — driftwood and wreckage and salvaged canvas — and they named it Peacockville, the way men will christen the place that has wrecked them. Everything the ship had carried was on the bottom or in splinters: three years' provisions, the stores, the tools of the trade. The castaways of the United States Exploring Expedition sat destitute on the sand at the edge of the continent, and were fed.

The Clatsop and the Chinookan people of the river brought fish and supply to them across the days. James Birnie, the Hudson's Bay man over the water at Astoria, sent what a company post could spare. The Methodist mission gave what it had. For three weeks the wrecked squadron lived on the river and on the people who held it.

Reynolds had spent four years on the taking side of every meeting the expedition made — the survey party landing armed, the son of a chief held in the boat as surety, the yams handed across a gunwale at noon under the eye of loaded guns. He had watched the squadron answer the loss of two of its own at Malolo with fire, and burn a village in the Gilberts over a single sailor who walked into a crowd and never walked out. The meeting that reached him now, on the sand at Baker's Bay, asked nothing of him and was owed nothing. People with a country came to men who had lost everything but their lives, and put food into their hands.

The survey books that had cost the ship lay open on the driftwood to dry, in a camp the river was keeping alive.

## 4 · Neah Harbor, and Cape Disappointment

Charlie was nowhere near the bar. Back in April, out of the same fog that had nearly finished her off Point Grenville, the *Vincennes* had gone the other way — up into the strait and the drowned mountain-country of still water beyond it — and by the height of summer she lay at Neah Harbor under Cape Flattery, at the corner where the whole coast turns east.

A canoe put off from the shore there and came out to the ship through the tide-rip, the broken jabble of water where the current sets against the swell and stands it on end. The man in it laid the canoe alongside under the *Vincennes*' rail with an ease the ship's own boats never found in that harbor, a hand on the paddle holding her steady in the rip. Halibut came up over the side, and oil; down went ship's biscuit, and a hoop of iron worth more ashore than the fish were worth afloat. There was a word across the rail, and a look, and the trade was made and done. Charlie had crossed the whole world and twice come within a quarter of an hour of dying on this coast, and here the ship bought her dinner from a man who read that water as plainly as Charlie read the wooden mess-kid his mess drew its ration in.

On the third of August a runner came in overland from Nisqually with the news: the *Peacock* was lost, on the bar of the Columbia. The ship the ice had failed to take had been taken by the very thing she came to chart. Charlie heard it as the whole lower deck did — secondhand, off a man who had walked it up the coast on his own two legs — while a hundred miles south the people who had lived it sat in a driftwood town, fed off the river day by day.

Three days after that the *Vincennes* stood south and made Cape Disappointment at daylight, and fired her guns into the mouth of the river to say she had come. Out of the river, over the bar that had opened the *Peacock*, came the little *Flying Fish*; and about nine in the morning Hudson climbed the *Vincennes*' side and went aft and told Wilkes that the ship was gone and every man of her saved.

It had lost a ship, been kept alive three weeks by strangers, and gone back to its soundings. Whether that ever carried aft of the mainmast — whether it reached the quarterdeck, or the cabin under it — the record does not say.
