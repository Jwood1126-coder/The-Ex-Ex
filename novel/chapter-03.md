## 1 · Hampton Roads, the summer of 1838

The expedition that had been ten years in the arguing gathered itself in one summer, at
Hampton Roads — the sheet of sheltered water inside the Virginia capes where the rivers
come down to meet the sea. By August six vessels swung there at their anchors: the
flagship *Vincennes*; her consort the *Peacock*; the storeship *Relief*, built to carry
the squadron's provisions; the brig *Porpoise*, up from the survey work; and two little
schooners, the *Sea Gull* and the *Flying Fish*, New York pilot boats bought into the
Navy for the shoal lagoons of the Pacific, where the big ships would not be able to
follow.

All summer the work of fitting out went on. Stores swung down the hatches. New canvas
came aboard in bales; the boats were overhauled; clothing was stowed against every
climate on earth, fur suits and India-rubber jackets in the same hold as thin duck for
the tropics. Some of the men had shipped for the voyage two years before, when Congress
first found the money, and had been parked a year in the frigate *Macedonian* to take
off the rough while the expedition went on failing to sail. In the last days they were
given liberty ashore, and took all of it; for Jack, as the forecastle held it, is a
hand before the mast, but he is a fellow-man, with rights and feelings. The brig's own
people painted ship and rove new rigging until she looked, Charlie thought, more rakish
than ever.

Charlie Erskine was sixteen that summer, before the mast in the *Porpoise*, and he was
where he wanted to be. He liked his captain very much — Ringgold, who *treated the crew
like men*: Charlie's own measure of an officer, and the highest he had. And the
commodore lay half a mile off across the anchorage, aboard the flagship. A squadron is
six ships. A boy could go around the world in one corner of it and never once stand in
the commander's shadow. That was the arithmetic Charlie had shipped on.

## 2 · The muster

Near the middle of August the commodore inspected his squadron, ship by ship. Aboard the
*Porpoise* all hands were mustered — called up from below and ranged in ranks on deck,
toes to a seam of the planking, every man in his best, to be looked at. The ranks stood
between the guns, and one of the guns Charlie knew by the breech.

Wilkes came over the side of the brig he had lately commanded and went down the ranks at
his own pace, missing nothing. The August heat stood on the deck; the ranks sweated in
their best, and there was no sound but the small creak of the brig working in the dead
air as he came. Charlie knew the voice before he ever saw the man; he had served a season
under it. It was the better part of a year since that voice had ordered him to the
gunner's daughter, and he had not stood this near its owner since.

Old Adam is the meeting-house name for the first man's nature still alive in a person —
the fallen part, the part that keeps its own watch. Fifty years on, Charlie gave the
moment one sentence and no more: *As he passed me at muster, 'old Adam' came up, and I
could not raise my eyes from the deck.*

Wilkes passed. He spoke no word to the boy, and nothing in his face said the boy was
anything he had ever seen before. The mark he had put on Charlie was under Charlie's
shirt, where inspections do not go. He finished with the brig and went over the side to
the next of his six ships.

The day after the inspection, the President of the United States came down to see the
squadron — Mr. Van Buren, with his cabinet about him. For the president the crews manned
the yards: men sent aloft to stand out in rows along the spars, the rigging of six ships
dressed with sailors, and the guns firing a national salute, the smoke walking off over
the anchorage. Ten years the country had haggled over these ships. Now it rowed out and
looked at them.

## 3 · The seventeenth of August

On the morning of the seventeenth of August, 1838, a gun was fired aboard the flagship:
one gun, blank, the signal for the squadron to get under way at last. Aboard the
*Porpoise* the word was still going round the deck when a boat came down the anchorage,
oars flashing together, and rounded to alongside with a thump against the brig's side. It was the commodore's gig — the light, narrow boat a
commander keeps for his own person, pulled by his own picked crew — and it brought
orders for one hand of the brig's company: Charlie Erskine, to shift to the flagship,
and to come as sailors come, with his bag and his hammock, which are between them
everything a sailor owns.

Charlie went to his captain and begged to be kept. He was sixteen, with the anchor
coming up under him, and he stood on the deck of the brig he loved and begged. Ringgold
heard him out. He said that he wanted him to stay — and that he must obey orders.

There was no more appeal in the Navy than that, and no time for one; across the water the flagship had
already loosed her topsails, canvas tumbling loose from her upper yards. The bag went down into the gig, then the hammock, then the
boy. The gig's crew shoved off and pulled through the middle of a squadron coming
alive — capstans turning, anchors breaking free of the bottom, sail falling loose from the yards of
six ships at once — with their one item of freight sitting silent in the stern. The
second gun was fired while he was on the water between the two ships.

The *Vincennes* stood up over the boat as they came alongside: the first full-rigged
ship Charlie had ever belonged to, three masts where the brig carried two, three decks,
two hundred men. He went up her side and in through the gangway with his bag on his
shoulder, one more hand reporting aboard, and nobody on her crowded deck took any
particular notice of him. *I felt more like jumping overboard,* he wrote, *than sailing
with my worst enemy, and one on whom I had sworn to be revenged.*

The oath had found its ship.

## 4 · Two hundred men

The squadron sailed down the Roads that evening and anchored at five o'clock at a bend of
shoal water called the Horseshoe, inside the capes. On the nineteenth, at four in the afternoon, Cape Henry
went astern, and the ships were at sea.

That night Charlie slung his hammock on the berth deck — below the gun deck, the hammocks
swung so close an elbow found the next man's — in the dark, among two hundred strangers.

The flagship had to be learned like a new town. Her people were told off into two
watches, starboard and larboard, turn and turn about, and every man given his station —
some sent aloft among the topmen, the rest ranged along the deck, bow to stern. Charlie went to the mizzen-top — the aftermost of the three masts — among the men
the watch bill numbered in the forties and the rest of the ship called the never-sweats,
the mizzen being the lightest duty aloft. The crew ate in sixteen messes of
twelve, with a mess apart for the petty officers, and a mess was a family with its own gear: a canvas cloth spread on the deck for
a table, a tin pail and a tin pan, two small wooden tubs bound with brass hoops — kids,
the ship called them — for carrying the cooked rations from the galley. From the purser,
the ship's storekeeper, who sold to a sailor against wages he had not yet earned, each
man drew his small stores: tea, sugar, pepper, soap, tobacco, a needle and thread and a
lump of beeswax to stiffen the thread.

At night the officer of the deck called through his speaking-trumpet — the tin horn that threw a
voice the length of the ship — "Bright lookout fore and aft!" and the answer came back from every lookout station in turn, bow to
quarter, the ship talking herself around her own dark decks.

On the first Sunday at sea, all hands were called to divine service, and Chaplain
Elliott preached the squadron its departure sermon. He spoke of the land they had
watched sink from view astern; of the dangers of such a cruise as theirs; of deaths. And
he gave them a sentence Charlie would keep: that *his all-seeing eye was ever upon us,
whether at sea or on dry land.*

Afterward each mess drew a Bible and a Prayer Book from the ship's stores. Charlie could
not read a line of either book. The sermon he could carry without letters: an eye that
did not close, at sea or on dry land — and his worst enemy one deck beneath his watch.

Six ships stood east in loose company. The weather held fine for days.
