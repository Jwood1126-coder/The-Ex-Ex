## 1 · Orange Harbor, 19 February 1839

The squadron came into Orange Harbor — the anchorage at the very tip of South America,
where the Atlantic and Pacific meet — and found it asleep. The storeship — the vessel that
carried the squadron's spare food and gear — and the two schooners lay already anchored,
and their people lay where the work had dropped them, in open daylight — "instead of the usual stir and bustle of a man-of-war's deck," Reynolds
wrote, "the deathlike silence of sleep."

The *Vincennes* had not had a still night in weeks. That night she got one, in water flat
as a pond under hills that still carried snow, the air gone summer-warm at the very foot
of the continent. "Oh! how sweet was my sleep," Reynolds put down, and meant the whole of
the sentence.

He woke to birds. They had never been shot at in this harbor and had not learned to leave
a man alone; he set them down, in a phrase he would have cause to remember, as living in
fancied security.

Then the veil came off. "No sooner had we cast anchor," Reynolds wrote, "than the veil of
mystery regarding our movements was withdrawn, and all hands went to work as if life and
death depended on their exertions." The squadron was not wintering here, and it was not
turning north for the warm islands. It was going south — now, at the raw close of the
season, into the ice that had turned Cook around. Every man on deck understood at once why
the work felt like life and death.

The holds came open and the cold-weather gear came up: fur, oiled coats, india-rubber,
sea boots to the thigh — the outfit of the best-found expedition the republic had ever
sent anywhere. And across water cold enough to stop the breath came a bark canoe with a
fire burning in it.

## 2 · The carried fire

A family entire rode in the canoe: a man, a woman, a half-grown boy working the paddle,
three infants coiled asleep on a bed of grass in the canoe's middle — and in the belly of the boat, on
a pad of wet mud, the fire. They kept it lit on the water. They carried their coals from
camp to camp rather than strike fresh flame each time, and the mud held the heat off the
bark. They had already been to the storeship and come away in her castoff clothes, worn in
arrangements of their own devising, and when the flagship signed for them to come aboard,
the man signed back that he had far to go, and went.

Here were people wintering all but naked in a country that had the Americans reaching for
fur, and every honest man in the squadron did the sum, whatever his mouth made of it after.
Reynolds looked at the father a long time and wrote down what he saw against the grain of
everything he had been taught to see: a face "intelligent… and rather a pleasant one," a
voice with music in it, a manner without a particle of fear — "evidently an old visitor to
ships."

Forward, the crew made their own contact, which ran on trade and food. Somebody passed a
pan of hot bean soup out through a port-hole to a canoe alongside; the people in it tried
to cool the soup in the sea, found the sea had salted it past eating, poured it out, and
kept the pan. Charlie reckoned the loss in the only ledger the forecastle kept: he was a
quarter out of pocket, he wrote, besides his regular beans. Red flannel was the prize of
the anchorage, torn into strips and wound round the head with the body left to the wind.
The women never came aboard; they held off in the canoes and tended their fires on the
stones. When the fifer struck up "The Bonnets of Blue," the visitors danced. There was one
word Charlie carried away and spelled as it fell on his ear — *Yammurscunar* — and never
learned the meaning of.

## 3 · The channels

In March Reynolds went out with Lieutenant Alden in an open boat to chart the drowned
country west of the Horn — the tangle of islands and channels at the very foot of the
continent: weeks of tide and sounding-line and wet blankets among channels
no man had yet put on paper. At one anchorage a party of young Fuegian men and boys came
down to the boat's crew, and the day that followed is the one Reynolds could never make
come out even.

They skylarked. There was snow on the ground, and they threw it, sailors and boys
together, up and down the beach; and Reynolds — cold, filthy, weeks from a dry stitch of
clothing — set the afternoon down that night exactly as it had felt. "We skylarked among
the snow together, as if we had been old friends. They were naked and we warmly clad, and
I just thought that we presented as wide a contrast of person and habit as could be met
with anywhere in the world." The boys were mimics of a high order. They gave back English
"with singular exactness," and took up the men's songs and dances "with a perfect
observance of note and time." It was the best day the boat's crew had along that whole
coast, and Reynolds knew it while it was happening.

Then he turned the page and reasoned it away. In the same entry, warmed by the finest
afternoon of his winter, he owned himself thankful to belong to "a more artificial
community," and waived forever the notion that the men with the fewest wants might have the
better of the bargain.

## 4 · Jim Orange, and the foxes

One of the country's men had already audited the whole American navy and delivered his
verdict by leaving. At Orange Harbor a Fuegian had come aboard the *Porpoise* and stayed,
with a composure that unsettled everyone who tried to condescend to him. He took the full
treatment — a wash in warm water, a shave, a haircut, a sailor's rig — and sat to dinner
among the officers "behaving with perfect propriety," Reynolds allowed, "for he imitated
the manners of those around him without an effort." He ate enormously. He was baffled by
the sails and delighted by the galley fire. The crew named him Jim Orange, and he answered
to it. Then one day he went over the side and did not come back, having taken from the ship
everything that interested him and rendered nothing of his own in exchange. The account of
the visit, honestly kept, closes in his favor.

The foxes had less of his sense. They came into the shore camp after the meat — where the
instruments stood in their tents and the observations were kept — and they had never
learned that a man was a thing to fear. They stood their ground when a sailor walked up,
and were shot where they stood. Reynolds entered it as a naturalist enters a fact: "they
gave battle without turning to flee and were shot down without trouble, because they had
not the instinct to run away." It went into the journal between a tide table and a sketch.

## 5 · The Sea Gull

The boat survey ended in the worst weather of the season. Coming back, the party fell into
the path of a full gale, and Reynolds rode it out on the deck of the *Sea Gull* — one of
the two little New York pilot schooners, a hundred and ten tons, and the best-loved sea
boat any of them had handled — with the open survey boat towing behind her in the dark.

The schooner carried herself through it. Her tiller was tied over and made fast, her sails
trimmed to hold her, and with no hand upon her she rode the seas as though she had decided
to. Reynolds sat on her streaming deck through the dark hours, the seas heaving up around
him like moving hills and cold spray sheeting off the rail, and wrote her the finest love
letter a sailor ever gave a ship. A landsman waking to such a night, he said, "would have deemed his hour come;
he would have put no faith in the wood and iron in such a chaos of the elements" — but the
schooner needed no faith; she had form. "It seemed much more natural to think that she had
a mind, an instinct, a will of her own… She seemed endowed with human sagacity." When the
weather broke she ran for the anchorage under a full moon with white water
breaking off her bow and all her snowy wings abroad.

The sky after the gale turned strange. A lookout raised a sail that no one could find again
once they closed on it, a ship there and then not there, and the men gave her the old
name and called her the Flying Dutchman. For half an hour three suns of equal brightness
stood in the sky at once. Low in the south hung the two pale smudges the forecastle read
like a weather-glass: three burnt holes in the sky, Charlie had them, and when they ran
together, look out for squalls — two of them galaxies in their own right, though no man
aboard would live to hear the word.

The squadron sailed north for the Pacific on the seventeenth of April. The *Sea Gull* did
not go. She was ordered to lie behind with her sister schooner, wait ten days for the
missing storeship, then follow on to Valparaíso at the tail of the
season. She never reached it. Somewhere off the Horn the sea she had ridden so beautifully
took her down with every soul aboard, and no wreck, no boat, no man of her was seen again.
Fifteen, by the usual count. Charlie, remembering, made it twenty — twenty as noble lads as ever
trod a ship's deck, he wrote, gone to a watery grave — and the miscount is the kind grief
makes, rounding up.

The ledger the voyage would keep for four years opened here, and it opened without a stone.
The schooner Reynolds had called almost alive had no grave to mark, and the men in her had
none. The Yámana fire, carried on its bed of mud from one camp to the next, went on burning
at the foot of the continent. The best boat the expedition owned was at the bottom of the
sea.
