## 1 · Noon and midnight

At noon, on almost any beach in the group — Fiji, hundreds of islands netted together
by reef — a boat lay just off the sand with her anchor down and her bow to seaward.
Trade came over the gunwale — the boat's top rail — yams inboard, taro, green coconuts, a pig with its
feet tied, iron and cloth going the other way. The crew kept their stations while
they bartered. And in the stern, among the strangers, sat a young man of the island
with nothing whatever to do: a chief's son, or a chief's brother's son, aboard as the
pledge that the men from the boat, ashore among his people, would come back to her
whole.

The pledge was not the visitors' invention. Surety was the islands' own contract law,
old and exact, and the squadron had adopted it as it had adopted the country's
pilots. While the pledge sat in the boat, the beach was a market. What the beach was
otherwise, no one cared to establish.

The muskets lay loaded along the thwarts — the boat's cross-benches — under canvas
against the spray, and at sundown the boats did not go ashore; they anchored inside
the reef and set an anchor watch, one man awake in each boat while the rest slept
where they sat. None of this was secret, and none of it gave offense. The islanders
knew to a nicety what a musket was. They had been buying them off the trading
ships for years, and had built a doctrine on them that no drill-book carried: a
Fijian loading a musket measured the powder to the man he meant to kill, a heavier
charge for a big man, and a warrior caught in the open watched the muzzle and dodged
at the flash. Two peoples who understood each other's tools to the last grain traded
across a gunwale every day of that season, each with a hand on the machinery, the way
careful men work a mill that has taken hands.

## 2 · The pledges

It ran in both directions, and it did not always run. In the third week of June, at
Muthuata on the north side of Vanua Levu, the old king wished to see the *Peacock*
and would not trust himself aboard her on words alone; so pledges were landed for
him — Passed Midshipman Reynolds, examined for lieutenant and waiting on a vacancy, and
Midshipman Hudson, the captain's own son, sent into the town to sit as the squadron's
sureties under Fijian eyes. The king weighed
the offer, two young officers against one old king, and was, the record says, "not
inclined to venture." Nobody in the group could have taught him anything about
hostages. He stayed ashore.

Two days later Reynolds went out to the work that would hold him the rest of the
season. Two divisions of boats pulled away from Kie Island, off Muthuata — Lieutenant
Walker's to the eastward, Lieutenant Budd's to the westward, and in Budd's boats
Reynolds and young Hudson — to carry the survey along the coast of Vanua Levu. The
work was the lead, the compass, the crossed bearing: from the boat, from an islet,
from a hilltop climbed at a sweat with the instruments on a man's back, the sun on the
neck and the brass slick under the fingers, the bearing of every point in sight was
read against every other, triangle locking to triangle down the coast, until the coast
came up on paper.
It was the plainest work of the whole enterprise, and the reason for all the rest of
it.

The boats were open — no deck, no galley, no dry stitch after the first squall.
Reynolds had learned at Tutuila what five days of that cost a man's bones. This was
weeks, with the muskets loaded. Budd's division came back alongside on the third of
July and went out again the next morning, to carry the survey down to Mbua Bay.

## 3 · The imprudence

The tender had been cruising the southern islands under Lieutenant Carr — the tender
being the squadron's little schooner, sent wherever the ships would not fit. At Moala,
wanting hogs and yams for her, Lieutenant Underwood took the boats in to trade, under
Carr's express order not to land.

He landed.

The market drew a crowd, and the crowd thickened. Underwood
stood on the sand with his back to the water, "unprepared to meet an attack" — the
words are Wilkes' — and the man who read the beach first was not an officer at all
but a white man called Charley, a beachcomber, one of the men the islands had
swallowed and kept. "You had better stand to your arms, gentlemen," he said; "they
are after mischief." The arms came up out of the boats, and the crowd was gone — took
to their heels, the record says, and the record sounds relieved. While the boats got
off, Charley mentioned that these same islanders had lately seized a trading vessel's
boat crew and sold the men back at a price: seizure and ransom, a settled commerce,
with usages and a tariff. The boats pulled out to the tender whole.

There was a second evening, at Moturiki, another island in the tender's track — the
account is Passed Midshipman May's — when Underwood's party, benighted, "imprudently
landed" and carried their gear up into the mbure, the town's great house. Then, in the
dark, the sailors caught natives passing war-clubs from hand to hand behind a log.
Underwood turned them out, took his
men back down to the water, and finished the night anchored in the boats, which is
where the night should have begun.

Nothing came of either evening. What the evenings were worth was settled years
afterward, in print, when Wilkes wrote the voyage into books and gathered these pages
under a heading of his own choosing: the imprudence of Lieutenant Underwood, who "was
very near involving them in difficulties." Imprudence is a filing word. It does not
argue; it shelves.

Underwood was more than his file. He was perhaps thirty, and the voyage kept finding
him at its pressure points; it was Underwood who had carried the old chief Tuetila's
arrival to the cabin door in Samoa and watched the name refused. Where the water
shoaled and the language ran out, the squadron's business fell to whoever would step
over a gunwale and open a market with his empty hands, and Underwood stepped first.
He trusted beaches. Almost nobody else in the group did by July, and the difference
made him useful, and made him watched.

One appointment in the squadron no one begrudged. The expedition's whole economy was
preferment — rank sought or withheld, fought over in every wardroom of the four
ships — and in the middle of it stood Midshipman Wilkes Henry,
the commander's sister's son, a widow's only son, whose berth was his uncle's own
arranging: one place on the muster with nothing behind it but a sister asking and a
brother saying yes. The boy was twenty-one. He did a midshipman's work
in the boats, under the eye that watched everything, and answered to a first name
that was also the commander's last.

## 4 · The sixteenth of July

On the sixteenth of July, the ships lying together in Mbua Bay and the tender and
boats being ready, Wilkes made up an expedition for the western islands and named it
in his own order: Assistant-Surgeon Fox, Acting-Master Sinclair, Passed Midshipman
Eld, and Mr. Agate with himself in the tender; "Lieutenant Alden and Midshipman Henry in the first, and Lieutenant Underwood
in the second cutter of the Vincennes; Lieutenant Emmons and Midshipman Clark in the
first cutter of the Peacock." A cutter was one of a ship's working boats, big enough
for a crew, their arms, and a fortnight's bread. Orders were left for Captain Hudson:
resurvey Mbua Bay, then take the *Peacock* round to Muthuata and come back for the
*Vincennes*. Ordinary orders. The season had issued a hundred like them.

Reynolds was on the water when the tender stood west — out with Budd's division along
the north shore of Vanua Levu, carrying the chart forward a triangle at a time. At
sundown the boats came in through a break in the coral and anchored where the water
lay still. Supper was cold and short. The muskets went under the canvas against the
dew; the day's angles went into the notebook under the stern seat; the anchor watch
was set, one man awake in each boat, and the rest slept where they sat, rolled in
their cloaks on the thwarts. Outside, the ocean came on in its long ranks and broke
on the coral, and came on, and broke, and came on.
