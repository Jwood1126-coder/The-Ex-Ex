## February 12, 1840

The word came aft along the deck the way all words came, station to station, a man
turning to say it to the man behind him and then facing forward again into his own
work: they had found a continent.

Charlie took it at the rail and passed it on, and did not, at first, know what he was
meant to do with it. A continent was a thing in Mr. Bowditch's book, a shape the size
of the page. You could not haul on it. It did not shorten the watch.

But some of it had come up in his own hands. Two days before, the naturalists had put
over the dredge — a weighted bag on a long line, dragged along the bottom to bring up
whatever lived and lay down there — and the hands had tailed onto the line and walked it
back in, wet fathom over wet fathom, until the bag broke the surface heavy with mud and
a few stones. Nobody forward had thought much of it. It was the officers who stood over
the stones the way men stand over a letter. They were rock off the floor of the far
south — the underfooting of the last unfound country on the planet, brought up on a rope
to a sailor's cold fist, and the word for exactly what they proved was still being fought
over in courtrooms when the last man who had hauled that line was an old man in Boston.
Charlie had turned one over a moment and set it down. There was nowhere on the ship for
it to go.

The coast itself had been standing off the beam for weeks, when the fog let it, and no
one on that deck any longer called the gray masses a bank of cloud. They held their
bearings while the bergs drifted past them; they kept their shape from one freezing watch
to the next; they ran on, mountains and ice-walls, west and west, longer than a man could
sail in a week. The ship had traced better than eight hundred miles of it, and the
officers had run the numbers and knew the trend did not close. Not an island. Not a field
of grounded bergs got up to look like land. A thing wider than the whole of Australia,
and it went on.

On the twelfth the commander set his pen to the open log and wrote it down under its own
name.

He had held the course west with the sick list lengthening under him and his officers
advising north, and held it, and the men who paid for that course in their fingers and
their chests had no vote in it and knew they had none. Now, one deck down — in the cabin
where the boy had once stood over his sleeping commander with a pin in his fist — he put
the conclusion into the record: the Antarctic Continent, discovered. It was the truest thing he ever set his name
to. It had cost a crew, and the crew was still aboard.

Forward, the word went mouth to mouth and then went quiet again, because eight bells —
the watch-change — was coming whether or not the bottom of the world had a floor.

*  *  *

The flagship never saw the French. That belongs to the wide shot, to the part of the
season Charlie only heard of long after, and second-hand, and never from a man who had
been there.

Two weeks before the word came aft, far off over the ice-horizon — the squadron strung
out along hundreds of miles of the same ice edge — the brig *Porpoise* had been working
west along that wall of pack when
her lookout raised two strange sail standing in the same white waste. They were the
*Astrolabe* and the *Zélée*, three years out of Toulon under Dumont d'Urville, their
companies thinned yellow by scurvy — the one other fleet on Earth that had come this far
and found the same coast. The French had already been ashore on a bare islet under the
cliffs and run up their flag. They had drunk a bottle to their king in the coldest wine
service in the history of the world, and named the country for a wife at home.

The two squadrons that had discovered the last continent stood within sight of each
other, on the one afternoon it would ever happen. The brig bore up to speak them.

Then it went the way pride goes. The French, to American eyes, made sail and stood off;
the brig's own commander, Ringgold, reading a snub in it, hauled his wind rather than be
seen to chase; and the
two of them drew apart again across the ice without one word passing, each ship's people
certain ever after that the other had turned its back first. History had arranged the
single meeting of the only two fleets that ever found the bottom of the world, and both
of them spent it declining to be snubbed. The sails sank away from each other into the
same fog, and the fog did not tell them apart.

## Termination Land

February took back what its first half had given.

The new season's ice was already making. A bay that stood open in the forenoon would be
skinned over by the middle watch, then floored hard, and the ship that had gone in after
the land had to be out before the door froze shut behind her. The running rigging
stiffened until the ropes would not run through the blocks — the wooden pulleys aloft
that a rope is rove through to be hauled — so that trimming a single yard meant men
beating the ice out of the sheaves before the line would so much as start. The coast the
ship had read for six weeks began to wall itself away behind pack her thin sides could no
longer be hazarded against.

The men were spent in the way that does not show at a muster — every man still answering
his name, still standing upright. A man could still lay onto
a rope; he could not, afterward, get the warmth back into himself, and the whole account
of the cruise lay in the space between those two facts. The ship was scarred along both
sides from the ice and the gales; half her canvas was patched; her people were working
her now on something nearer to memory than to seamanship, doing by feel what they were
too worn to do fresh.

On the twenty-first the commander gave the last headland he could reach its name, and the
name was Termination Land — the chart telling the truth that once, because that was where
it ended, where he ended it. Then the *Vincennes* was put about and stood north, and left
astern of her fifteen hundred miles of coast that no chart on Earth had carried the week
before, traced from a bare, unstrengthened wooden ship with no ice in her bones, in
sixty-odd days below the sixtieth parallel.

Behind her the bays she had not entered went on freezing shut, one after another, the
whole length of the country she had found — the door closing on it season by season,
without reference to the men who had come so far to knock.

## The run north

She carried her prize home rolled up in the cabin.

That was where the greatest month of the whole four years lived: fifteen hundred miles of
new coast in a young draughtsman's ink, worth every mile the century would say it was
worth. And forward of it, and below it, in the same hull, in the dark of the berth deck below,
where the hammocks hung a hand's-breadth apart, lay the men the month had used up — the
swelling sick list, the frost-eaten hands, the chests that had taken the cold in and kept
it. One ship carried the chart and the men who were the price of the chart, in the same
water, at the same time, because there was only ever the one ship.

The old man in Boston, half a century on and the last of them living, set the crew's part
of it down without any softening, because he had been one of them and it was how they had
felt it: they cursed the commander with everything they had, and they were proud of that
cruise until the day they died.

And this, which the wide shot confirms and the old man knew: not one of them ever set his
boot on the thing they had found. The French had scrambled up onto their islet and had
their wine and their flag. The Americans had run fifteen hundred miles along the front of
a continent and touched none of it — no landing, no footprint, not one man of the whole
company ashore on the country his own hands had helped drag up out of the fog. It was a
boon the season had no use for. It went into the cabin in its oilcloth, and the men went
into their hammocks, and the *Vincennes* stood north for a warmer sea, carrying the map
of a country not one soul aboard would ever be let stand on — and a boy at her rail
worked his cold fingers open, one at a time, waiting for the feeling to come back into
them.
