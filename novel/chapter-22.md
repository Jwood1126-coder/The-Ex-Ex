At Drummond's Island, in the Gilberts, a man of the *Peacock*'s crew did not answer the
evening count.

He had spent the day ashore with Lieutenant Walker's surveying party at a town called
Utiroa, while the boats felt out the reef and the lagoon behind it. The town had its own
traffic on the water — outrigger canoes working the shallows, sails cut from pandanus
matting, a people as much at home on that reef as the surveyors were on their own
quarterdeck. The people ashore were wary and close-watching. A story had run ahead of the
ship along the trade routes — that these islanders had cut off a vessel some years back
and killed the men in her — and the survey went forward under the weight of it, every hand
told to expect the worst of a beach that had so far offered nothing but wariness. No blow
was struck all day. Then the party mustered at the water at dusk to pull back to the ship,
and the count came up one short, and no one could say when the man had last been seen. He
had gone off into the crowd on some errand, and the crowd had closed, and that was the
whole of what anyone would ever know.

This was April of 1841. Hudson had the ship. Wilkes was half a world to the north with the
other cruise, and Utiroa was Hudson's to answer, and there was no grief in the thing
anywhere. No commander's nephew lay dead in the shallows; no widowed mother had put her
only son aboard into anyone's keeping. The missing man carried a name on the ship's books
and it has come down worth nothing, because nothing was ever fastened to it — no body, no
grave, no last sight of him upright. He was ashore at noon and gone by dark, and the ship
wanted him back, and could not have him.

The demand went in with the next morning: return our man. The answer was the beach. It
filled with warriors, formed up and armed and unwilling — a town
refusing to be searched by the strangers who had come asking, over a man it may or may not
have taken. So the *Peacock* stood in and opened on Utiroa with her guns, and under the
cover of the bombardment Walker put his party through the surf, and the town came down to
the water to meet it, by the Americans' own reckoning seven hundred fighting men.

They came on, and the muskets stopped them — the party firing by ranks from the wet sand,
loading and firing again into the press. Massed fire is a thing an island learns only
once. Utiroa learned it in its front rank, which folded back into the ranks behind. The
volleys walked forward and opened lanes through the crowd. A dozen of the islanders were
counted dead by the men who fired. How many more were carried off, or died in the burning, or went down
uncounted in the shallows, stands in no book, because the only people keeping books that
day were the people holding the guns. Then the town was fired. Thatch takes flame fast and
whole, and inside an hour Utiroa was ashes.

The man was never found. Not that morning, not that year, not since — not his bones, not a
word of what became of him, whether he was killed in the first hour ashore or lived out a
long life on some far island under some other name. A ship's company had come up one short
at a sundown muster. By the following sundown a town had been shelled and stormed and
burned off the face of the water, a dozen of its people were dead by the low count and the
rest unreckoned, and the sailor the whole of it was for had simply stopped being anywhere.
Then the boats went back over the side to their sounding-lines, and the chart of the
Gilberts gained another clean-drawn island, and the cruise stood on north as if nothing
had cost it a thing.

*  *  *

The squadron was in two halves that spring, running its last two campaigns at once. While
Hudson's ships worked the equator, the flagship had the far northern coast — up among the
still, fir-walled channels and islands behind the strait, where the surveys ran with the
open sea shut out behind them, and then out onto the long homeward road that would not
fetch up until New York, the better part of a year of blue water. And somewhere aboard her
the whole way she carried Vendovi, in irons — his wrists chained — and never let past the
reach of them.

The ship's books called him Vendovi; that was the expedition's spelling of a man. In his
own country he was Ro Veidovi, a brother of the king of Rewa, taken in the summer of 1840
off the *Peacock*'s deck for a killing six years gone, and carried now toward the nation
whose citizens he had helped to put to death, there to be improved — so the reasoning ran
— by the sight of it. His brothers had wept at the parting and the king had kissed his
forehead; the ship had noted the scene and weighed her anchor. Aboard, he was allowed the
run of the deck within bounds the master-at-arms — the ship's own constable — kept clear
in his own head, and he kept his own counsel. He was grave, and homesick in a way that
needed no common language to be read without a word, and he carried himself with the plain
authority of a man who had stood high among his people — aboard a ship that had filed his
rank in its catalogue somewhere between the corals and the war clubs.

They studied him. The gentlemen of science came at him with the same appetite they brought
to a coral head or a bird skin — laid their calipers along his skull and wrote the figures
in their notebooks while he held still under their hands. Agate had drawn his face back in
Fiji, for the plates of a book the chief would never see. And Vendovi studied them in his
turn. He watched the men who watched him, steadily enough to put a few
of them off their ease, and he learned the ship's words, and he gave the ship very little
of his own.

Once he gave it something that would outlast every man who sailed in her. The expedition's
philologist — the young man set to take down the speech of every people the ships fell in
with — sat with the prisoner, paper and ink between them, and wrote out in Vendovi's own
telling the ancient account of the flood that had once risen over Fiji, and how the people
had kept their great canoes laid up ever after against the water's return. The chief told
it slowly, the pen going after him line by line. When the volume was printed, years later,
the account stood in it with Vendovi's name set beside it, credited exactly the way a
scholar credits an authority he has leaned on. For one page of that printed work, the man
in irons was its source.

On the long passages there was little for anyone to do but keep the watches and count the
days, and the ship settled into the plain monotony of blue water — the trades doing the
work, the same faces at the same mess, the miles falling astern. Vendovi kept the deck
when he was let up into it, and stood at the rail, and watched the sea run past the side.
Every mile of it carried him further from the only country he had ever known and nearer
the one that had decided, on his behalf, that the carrying was a kindness.

Charlie Erskine was aboard the same ship. He had come out of Norfolk under Wilkes' own eye
— the commander had picked the flogged boy out at a muster and had him shifted to the
flagship, the marked boy brought back under his own eye — so the two of them rode
the one hull the better part of two years: the boy whose back the ship's authority had
opened with a rope, and the chief whom that same authority held in irons, and not a word
between them that the two of them could both have understood.

They passed sometimes in the waist, or at the head of the ladder, in the close traffic of
a crowded ship. Charlie knew who he was; the whole forecastle did, and mostly liked the
little of him it was given to know — a quiet man of standing who had kept his
dignity in a corner of the world that had not thought his capture through past the arrest.
And Charlie knew, too, without ever finding the words for it or once trying to, the single
thing he held in common with the chief that no other man aboard shared: each
of them was on this ship because one command had put him there and meant to keep him.
Charlie had felt that hand close on him. He could still, on a cold morning, find the place
where it had cut.

He never spoke to Vendovi. There was no language for it, and it would have been a ship's
boy's presumption to suppose the chief owed him so much as a glance. But he looked, when
they passed, and once or twice the chief looked back — not at a friend, nor a fellow
anything, only at one more of the ship's six hundred, a boy among the hands who worked the
wooden country he was being carried through against his will. Then the look would go by.
The chief went aft to whatever length of deck the master-at-arms let him have, and Charlie
went forward to his station, and the ship carried the pair of them on together toward the
one harbor at the end of all that water.

It was Charlie's home. It was not his.
