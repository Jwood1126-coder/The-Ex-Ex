## 1 · The Cargo

What came home in the holds outlasted almost every hand that packed it. Sixty thousand
specimens came off the ships in casks and crates and went into the cases of the Patent
Office in Washington — corals and war clubs, bird skins and pressed plants, and a freight
of seeds that a gardener coaxed into growth until they were the beginning of a national
garden. For a few years anyone could file past the whole of it, the assembled wonders of
the great voyage laid out under glass, boots loud on the board floor, and read the labels,
and go home.

Then, in 1846, Congress chartered a new institution on the bequest of an English chemist
who had never crossed the ocean his money would open, and the expedition's cargo became
its backbone — the reason there was something for the Smithsonian to be. The Englishman
was James Smithson, and he had left, with the money, a single sentence, and it became the
charter of the house his money built: *Every man is a valuable member of society who, by
his observations, researches, and experience, procures knowledge for men.* Half a century
on, a sailor who had shipped before the mast and been flogged at a gangway for wetting the
ship's mail would reach past every admiral and author he might have quoted and set
that sentence at the head of his own book.

The words the voyage brought back proved harder to keep than the seeds. Congress had
ordered the results published in a style worthy of the great voyages of Europe, and then
ordered them published in an edition of one hundred copies — one to a state, a few to the
crowned heads, the American people invited to apply to their legislature for a look. Wilkes
superintended the work for twenty years and got out nineteen volumes of the twenty-eight
that were planned; the rest — Agassiz's fishes, a second botany, the commander's own
physics — sit unwritten in the national ledger still. The collection outran the country's
patience for describing it. That account has never been closed.

## 2 · The Retired List

Wilkes got his rank the slow way the peace afforded — commander in 1843, captain in 1855 —
and then, past sixty, the war handed him one more chance to be precisely himself. In
command of the *San Jacinto* — a steam frigate longer than any ship of the old squadron,
driving into the wind on coal smoke where they had driven on sail — in November 1861 he
stopped the British mail packet *Trent* on the open sea with two shots across her bow and
took off her, by force, two Confederate commissioners bound for Europe. For a few weeks he
was the most famous man
in the Union, thanked by Congress by name and toasted from Boston to Chicago. Meanwhile
Great Britain moved troops toward Canada, and the two nations stood close enough to a war
to frighten both governments out of it. Lincoln's cabinet let the commissioners go and
disavowed the act, and the pattern that had run through Wilkes' whole life ran once more,
now at the size of nations: the brilliant unordered stroke, the roar of approval, the quiet
disavowal, the file closed behind him.

He was made commodore — several warships now under his own broad pennant, a squadron in
the West Indies — and fell to feuding, with neutrals, with the Navy Department, with the
Secretary himself, whose rebuke Wilkes answered
in a letter he saw fit to leak to the newspapers. In 1864 they court-martialed him a second
time. Where the court of 1842 had gone through four years and eighty-seven thousand miles
and found in all of it a single provable crime, this court found the whole indictment
proved — disobedience, insubordination, disrespect, conduct unbecoming, every charge — and
sentenced him to reprimand and three years' suspension, which Lincoln, the last of the long
line of superiors who could never quite bring themselves to finish him, cut to one. Rear
admiral in 1866, and on the retired list with it — the flag rank granted and no deck to
fly it from, an admiral ashore for good. He spent his last decade in Washington restating
his case, page by page, to a history that had not yet ruled on it, and died on the eighth
of February, 1877. The continent he had claimed for his country, and asserted to the end
of his life against every doubter, was at his death still in dispute — the discovery his
whole name rested on, and it went into the ground with him unproven.

## 3 · Acting Secretary of the Navy

The pen rose.

Reynolds' lieutenant's commission, dated September 1841, chased him across the Pacific and
caught him near the end of the cruise. Then his health broke, and the Navy set him on its
discard pile, which turned out to be Honolulu: through the 1850s the man who had filled a
forbidden journal against his commander kept naval stores in the islands he had first seen
from the *Vincennes*' boats. The war recalled him. Command of a storeship at Port Royal made
his name, and after that the ladder simply kept going. Captain in 1866. In 1867, commanding
the *Lackawanna*, he stood on a spit of coral called Brooks' Island, ran a flag up over
it, and took formal possession for the United States — Midway, the first ground the
country ever claimed beyond its own shores, annexed by the pen that had recorded the
expedition, in the expedition's own habit of fixing a speck to a chart and writing a flag
onto it. Commodore in
1870. Rear admiral in 1873. And twice, in 1873 and 1874, the same man who had once
confided to a secret journal his wish to see his captain hanged sat at the head of the whole
department, as Acting Secretary of the Navy.

He retired on his sixty-second birthday and died in Washington on the fifth of November,
1879. They carried him home to Lancaster and buried him beside his younger brother John —
Major General John F. Reynolds, killed on the first morning at Gettysburg — two brothers out
of one Pennsylvania house holding a corner of the ocean's story and a corner of the
continent's between them. The journal stayed shut in the family's papers for a century and
a half and was published, at last, in 2004. Every reader since has learned what
his wife always knew: the finest writer the expedition ever carried had ranked, on the day
it sailed, somewhere near four hundredth in its chain of command.

## 4 · Tarawa

Some two hundred charts went out from the voyage into the working world and simply worked —
sold to the fleet and the whalemen, corrected and recopied and steered by, decade after decade.
Most of their makers were dead before the paper wore out. One chart worked longer than any
man who drew it could have imagined a use for.

In April of 1841 the *Peacock*'s boats had spent a few days sounding a middling atoll in the
Gilberts — a chain astride the equator, a lagoon ringed by reef, a name taken down as
Tarawa — and had inked it and filed it with two hundred sisters, another speck fixed and
forgotten. A hundred and two years later, in the autumn of 1943, the officers preparing to
land on that atoll spread their charts on a table and found that the best survey of Tarawa
in Allied hands was still, "with only slight modifications," the one the expedition's boats
had drawn. The reef lines held true. Across a century of ocean the old chart brought a
fleet to the exact water it described.

What the boats of 1841 had never stayed to measure was the tide. The lagoon at Tarawa keeps
a dodging, unreliable low water that the modern age can name and predict to the hour — a
tide that on certain days simply declines to come in, a fall a man can compute now to the
inch. The men of 1841 had left the tide a blank. The navy of 1943 filled the blank with a
guess.

On the twentieth of November 1943 the guess failed. The tide lay down on the reef and would
not lift; the landing craft grounded on the coral eight hundred yards out — near half a mile
of open water short of the beach — and could go no farther. The Marines of the 2nd Division
went over the sides into chest-deep water and waded that half mile through machine-gun fire,
past their own stranded boats. Many did not reach the sand. The chart had carried them to
the exact water it described, and stopped where the survey had stopped.

So the signal the *Peacock*'s crews had inked into that lagoon reached its readers a century
late. The chart lay on the table, corrected and current, the best survey of that water in
the world. Every hand that made it was gone. The chart was not.
