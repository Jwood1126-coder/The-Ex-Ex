## 1 · Matavai Bay

The flagship let go her anchor in Matavai Bay on the tenth of September, in the lee
of a low spit of black sand the charts marked Point Venus. The scientific gentlemen
went ashore first, with the instruments, and set their tents on the very ground where
Captain Cook, seventy years gone, had sat out a clear night to watch the planet Venus
cross the face of the sun. They stood the long glass — the astronomers' telescope — and the pendulum clocks among
the palms, posted a marine over them, and settled in for a month; and for that month the
squadron did the work it had crossed an ocean to do — sounded the harbor and the reef
that fenced it, filled the water casks, and paid its calls on the young queen's
government ashore.

The Tahiti the crew had shipped out to find was not the Tahiti they came to anchor in. This one went to chapel and kept the Sabbath and wore covered cloth, ruled
over by a girl-queen and her English advisers, its famous ease put under printed
hymnals and a written law. The old hands who had swapped stories about the place since
they were boys looked at the reef and the green valleys and the mission bells and could
not make the one match the other.

The island stood up behind all of it in a green pyramid a thousand feet into the
cloud, the landfall every man aboard had grown up hearing lied about, and the first
lesson of the place was that the lying ran both ways. The order came down that only big
chiefs might cross the gangway. It did not last the week. Every man who paddled out was
a big chief, by his own account and his cousin's, and there was no arguing a Tahitian
out of his rank; so the order fell, as such orders did, and the canoes lay four and
five deep along the side from breakfast to dark, oranges and breadfruit and live pigs
going up the ship's side against fishhooks and cloth, and coming the other way the
whole practiced patience of a people who had been pricing white men off that beach for
sixty years.

## 2 · The night singing

The women came at night, and by water.

About three bells in the evening — half-past nine, deep into the first watch — the
singing would lift off the beach, and then the swimmers were out around the ship in the
dark, their voices coming up the side wet and clear. They sang what the missionaries had
taught them and what the whalers had taught them, in that order and with no seam between
the two. "Old Hundred" first — the doxology every Sabbath-keeping house in New England
carried by heart — and then "Coronation"; and then, the hymns used up, "Black-eyed
Susan" and "The Bay of Biscay, O," which were the sailors' own songs, fetched out to
that water in some earlier ship and left behind like everything else the whaling men
left there. They held it up till two and three in the morning.

They were not let aboard. That order held where the other had failed. So they sang the
ship to her rail and were refused it, and when they had sung themselves quiet they
turned in the black water and swam back to the beach. The next night they came again,
and sang the same songs in the same order, and were refused again, and swam back again.
A man standing his trick on deck could follow the sound of them a long way in toward
the surf before it went under the noise of the reef.

## 3 · The capstan

Two of the crew ran for the hills. There was an old Tahiti still up in the valleys
behind the chapels — or near enough to one that a man might desert for it — and two men
slipped over the side and went inland to find out whether the stories had left anything
standing. They had a warm island in front of them and a hard ship behind, and they made
the trade that men had been deserting to make since the first ship touched there.

Thirty dollars a head was posted for their return, and it was the Tahitians who earned
it. The two ran into a country where every valley belonged to somebody, and the people
of it knew the ground the way the survey boats would never learn it. They ran the pair
down somewhere inland, lashed them
wrists-to-ankles along tree limbs the way a hunting party slings a pig to carry it out
of the bush, and brought them down out of the mountains under the poles.

The reward was counted out on the capstan — the drum-headed winch amidships, waist-high,
that a whole watch tramped round in a ring, bars shipped like the spokes of a wheel, to
walk the anchor up off the bottom. The two men were stood to it. Each took his own
thirty dollars off the capstan-head with his own hands and laid the coins in the hands
of the islanders who had caught him. Then the money was gone and the men were still
there, and each in his turn was lashed wrists-up to the capstan bars, and the
boatswain's mate unrolled the cat and shook its tails loose — nine tails where the colt
had one, and every tail knotted. They bared each back and gave it thirty-six.

Charlie stood in the mustered ranks and watched, and his own back kept the count without
being asked. Fifty years after, an old man in Boston could still fetch up the sound of
it — flat and loud, the same sound the colt had made across his own knuckles a
quarter-mile from his mother's window — and he set it down at last in a book, because
by then there was no one left in the world for his keeping still to shield.

## 4 · The tea-party

What finally turned the men was not the cat. It was tea.

The purser — the ship's shopkeeper, who doled a man his tea and tobacco and thread out
of the small stores and chalked the price against his pay — put the cost of tea up to
double, and the berth deck, the crew's own deck where they slung their hammocks and ate
their mess, held a council of war over the mess-cloth — the canvas spread for their meals. The man
who spoke first was Jack Kennison, whose father had gone down to a Boston wharf sixty-odd years
before and helped tip a whole cargo of it into the harbor, and who held, it appeared, an
inherited opinion on the price of tea.

The finding of the council was plain. They would sign the purser's book for their soap
and their tobacco and their thread, as they always had; and they would put their names
to no more tea and no more sugar — and let this be called mutiny, a tea-party, or
whatever they choose to name it.

Then they waited for the drum. A ship that means to answer her people beats to quarters:
the drum calls every hand up out of the dark below to stand in his place and watch a man
seized to the rigging and the cat brought out, and every one who had signed the short
list knew that was the sound waiting at the wrong end of the thing. They had just watched
it paid out on two backs at the capstan, coin and cat both, and every man of them had a
back of his own. So they ate their breakfast and stood their watches and passed the word
along quietly at each change of the watch, and waited for the drum, and it did not beat.
It did not beat the next morning either, nor the one after that.

The commodore's face, which was a hard face on its kindest morning, that morning
"looked as genial as if he had discovered a new planet." He said nothing of the tea.
Nothing more was heard of it aboard that ship until Sydney, and Sydney was another
country and another quarrel.

They took their tea after that in a scouse of boiled yams and taro and salt junk, and
called it cheap at the price. In October the squadron watered and stowed and stood out
to the westward with the reef dropping away astern, and the women swam off after the ships to
sing them gone — "Old Hundred" first, as it always was — and were not let aboard, and
turned in the dark water, and swam back to the beach they had come from.
