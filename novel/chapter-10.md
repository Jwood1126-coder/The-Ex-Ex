## 1 · San Lorenzo, June 1839

San Lorenzo was a bare brown island a few miles off Callao, the port of Lima, and the
squadron came to anchor under its lee on the sixth of June and lay there the better part
of two months. There was an observatory to set ashore, and a fleet to overhaul before it
turned west into open ocean. Caulkers' mallets went along the waterlines all day; the
boats pulled out from the mainland loaded with water and stores; tar and fresh paint
stood on the wind. A pleasant coast, and good weather, and nothing over the men but work.

The *Relief* had left something in her hold. She was the slowest ship in the United States
Navy, a hundred days to Rio when the others had done it in a third of that, and the
commodore — Wilkes — emptied her of her provisions and sent her home on the twenty-first,
the sick and the worn-out with her, the first of the men whose faces no longer fit. All
hands wanted to go in her. But when her stores came across to the other ships, whisky came
with the salt beef, and it found them.

At sunrise the gratings went up at the gangway.

A grating is a hatch cover of crossed wooden bars, made to let air down into the ship;
lashed upright against the rail it made a frame a man could be spread on. The deck had
been holystoned white at dawn — scoured pale with soft sandstone blocks worked back and
forth on the wet planks — and was still wet with it. The men came up from below and fell in
along the bulwarks in the grey light, quiet, none of them looking at the man being led out.
All hands were called to witness, because witness was the whole of it. A man was seized up
by the wrists, stripped to the waist, and a boatswain's mate laid on with
the cat — nine tails of knotted cord on a rope handle, a heavier tool than the colt, and a
lawful one. The surgeon stood by to say when a man had had enough to be going on with. Another
man, a master's mate, called the count aloud into the cold morning, one to twelve, and then
began again at the next back.

Every man who had been drunk took a dozen, which was the ceiling the law set on a
captain's own hand. It did not hold for the three who had run and been fetched back aboard
the *Falmouth*, another of the squadron's ships; they were seized up after the rest, when
the deck had already learned the sound. One took thirty-six. The other two took forty-one.

Charlie stood the muster with the ship's company and counted under his breath with the man
calling it, because a body that has been over the gun — bent across the breech and whipped — counts
whether it means to or not. He was two years and an ocean from the navy yard at
Charlestown, and his back still knew the arithmetic exactly.

That same fortnight, on the same stretch of water, two Yankee whalers lying off Callao ran
a red flannel shirt up into their fore rigging — Jack's signal that things aboard had got
past bearing. The commodore was pulled across to them himself. He went up the side of one
and then the other, and he stood the two captains in front of him and made them promise
that they would feed their men better and use them better. Then he was pulled back to his
own ship.

## 2 · The burying valley

Up a valley behind the anchorage was the ground where the ships buried their dead —
whatever nation they came off, whoever died on that coast. Charlie went up among the
graves on a liberty day.

The markers were mostly boards, sawn and lettered by shipmates and driven in at the head
of each mound, and the sun and the salt air had been working at them a long time — some of
the older ones so faded a man had to kneel and read them sideways to catch the letters at
all. Whalers lay there, and merchantmen, and the odd man-of-war's man; the sea trades had
been leaving their people in that valley for years, and would go on.

He had lately learned to read and to write. A Boston boy aboard had read him his own letters
first, at Rio, and shamed him into the yeoman's storeroom, where he had covered fathoms of
paper making one word over and over until it came right.

So he took out a pencil and went board to board, copying down the names and the verses off
them, a dozen and more, the wood sun-hot under his hand.

They were nearly all of them boys.

One board carried William Pearce, a sailor boy, aged sixteen years, dead the twenty-fifth
of September, 1838. He had been dead before this squadron so much as raised the Horn. Under
the name a verse, cut small to fit the board:

*A mother's eye will look, but look in vain…*

Charlie was sixteen himself, or near it. He copied the verse out entire and stood over it
a while.

A few mounds on, another board gave another boy of sixteen, Thomas Hendrick. The valley
was full of them, sixteen and seventeen and twenty, the ones the sea trades used up and
the fevers carried off — every one of them lying a long way, in a straight line, from
wherever his mother lived.

Somewhere in that ground the wish took him that he wrote down half a century later, still
wanting it: that when it came, he might be laid in his last hammock in some peaceful vale
like this one. A sailor who dies at sea goes over the side sewn into his own hammock, with
a round-shot at the feet to carry him down; the hammock is the last thing that is his. The
boy in the valley wanted the hammock and not the sea — the held ground, and an eye that
might come and look.

One mound was newer than the rest. The earth on it was still dark, and no board had been
lettered for it yet. A marine off the *Peacock*, Benjamin Olden, had been carried up and
buried there on the eighth of July. He was the squadron's own — the first of the six
hundred to be left in that valley. He would not be the last the year asked for.

At night the town watchmen called the hours across the water from the mainland. *Midnight
is past,* went the cry, thin over the anchorage, *the cross begins to bend* — for they told
the time by the Southern Cross, which stands upright at the middle of the night and then
leans slowly over as the dark runs out. It hangs there still, and does the same work.

## 3 · The Fourth, and the standing out

The Fourth of July they kept at anchor. There was flag bunting up and down the rigging and
a national salute rolling off the ships and coming back flat off the island, and an extra
tot of grog served round — rum and water, the day's ration doubled; splicing the
mainbrace, the sailors called it. Then the salute was done and the grog was drunk and the
island was still there. There was nowhere to keep the Fourth but the deck they
stood on and the brown island watching.

Callao had its own welcome for anyone who went ashore. A man could pull on clean white duck
trousers to go over to the mainland, and inside half an hour they were black to the knee —
fleas, in a moving coat, and where the fleas had fed the white was spotted all over with
his own blood.

On the twelfth of July the squadron weighed and stood out past San Lorenzo, and the island
and its valley dropped astern into the haze. Charlie had the copied verses folded away in
his hat, where the letters had gone the day he was flogged, and William Pearce went with
them, aged sixteen, and so did the wish for a green place to lie in. Ahead of the ships lay
the ocean the whole voyage had been built to cross, unmarked and mostly empty on the paper,
a year of work in it — and its own way of filling ground.
