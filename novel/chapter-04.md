## 1 · The Gulf Stream

The evening the ship entered the Gulf Stream, the sun went down on one side of her
while the moon came up on the other, and for a little while the two lights hung
level across the water. It was the twenty-second of August. The stream ran warm
under her keel, and the weather was fine and stayed fine, day after day of it,
sailing that was mostly sky.

The *Vincennes* was the first full-rigged ship Charlie had ever served in, and
after the brig she was a world: three decks, two hundred men, sixteen messes of
twelve. A mess ate off a canvas cloth and drew its dinner down from the galley in
two little brass-hooped tubs called kids. A man's small stores — tea, sugar,
tobacco, a needle and thread — he drew from the purser against his pay, and the
whole of it together would not have filled his hat. Charlie had come into her on sailing day, fetched across by
the commodore's own gig with his bag and hammock, his begging already spent:
Captain Ringgold had wanted to keep him in the brig, and had said so, and had
obeyed his orders. Ringgold treated the crew like men. That was the man he had
lost.

Two watches cut the crew in halves, starboard and larboard, four hours on deck and
four below, turn and turn about, and every man had his place in her, on deck or
aloft. Charlie's was the mizzen-top, the platform partway up the aftermost of the
three masts, reached by the rigging, where the sails ran smallest and the work
lightest. The ship's slang called the mizzen-topmen the never-sweats.

At dinner the cloth was spread on the berth deck and twelve men sat down to it,
and the kid came down full from the galley and the tin pans went round. Four
hours later half of them were on deck in the dark, and four hours after that the
other half, and so on around the clock to the world's far side and back.

At night the officer of the deck put his speaking-trumpet to his lips and sang out
— *Bright lookout fore and aft!* — and the answer came back from every lookout
station in turn, voice to voice around the dark ship, full
circle, and no man asleep to break it. A lookout's whole work was to see: an hour of standing
still with the night in his face, the hail, the answer, the sea.

The chaplain preached, early in the passage, to the ship's company on what they
had shipped for: on dangers, on deaths, on the land sinking from view astern.
God's all-seeing eye, he told them, was ever upon them, whether at sea or on dry
land. Each mess was issued a Bible and a Prayer Book, to be kept with the pail and
the kids.

The course was set for Madeira on the twenty-fifth. For several days the weather
was fine, and the ship ran south with her decks dry and her people learning her.

## 2 · Eight bells

At midnight on the twenty-ninth Charlie came up with the mid watch — the watch
that holds the deck from midnight to four. His turn at lookout that night was on
deck, not aloft: he went aft to relieve the man on the lee quarter, the sheltered
rear corner of the ship. The station came to him with its news: the ship had
crossed the Tropic of Cancer with the change of the watch. The man he relieved
went below.
After that the deck was quiet — the slow, slight roll of her, the small working of
her gear, and nothing else at all.

Light stood up out of the cabin skylight — a low frame of glass propped open to
the heat — and lay in a pale square on the planks by his feet.

He looked down it. At the table below, under the lamp, the commodore sat tracing
out a chart — awake at midnight while two hundred men slept around him and over
him, alone with the paper. The whole squadron sailed on what came off that table.
The hand moved and kept moving; the head stayed bent. Charlie stood on the dark side of the glass and
watched the man at whose command he had been flogged.

The oath came up in him whole. The stripes were not a year healed, and the sting
of the colt came up with it, fresh as the hour it was laid on.

The devil took possession of him.

Forward, the officer of the deck turned his back and started up the ship, and
every step he took emptied the after end of the deck a little more toward him.

He did not remember choosing. The iron pin was out of the rack and in his fist,
and then it was out over the open skylight, over the lamp and the chart and the
bent neck, and he was waiting.

Not for nerve. Nothing aboard a ship was done against her roll; a man hauled with
it, walked with it, ate and slept by it, and everything the sea had ever taught
Charlie it had taught him in that rhythm. He was waiting for the weather roll —
for the sea to lean the ship to leeward under him, to tip the deck that way and
carry his arm and the iron down with it, so the pin would fall not by a boy's
strength but with the ship's whole shoulder behind it. The officer's steps were
far forward now, faint on the planks. His mouth had gone dry, and his heart was
loud in him. The night's heat came up through the skylight with the lamplight, and
the light ran along the iron in his fist, and under his bare feet he felt her begin
to lift.

The roll began.

What came to him inside it he kept from every living soul for fifty-two years,
and then set down in one sentence, an old man alone with a page in Boston: *I
saw, or fancied I saw, the upturned face of my mother.*

"My God! what does this mean?" It came out of him in a gasp, under his breath.

His arm came down. Not the blow — the arm, with the iron still in it, back over
the rack. The pin went into its place, and his hand stayed shut on it. He had
struck nothing, and his body did not believe it: the fingers had set around the
iron in a death grip, and it was some time before he could take his hand from it.

Below, nothing had changed. The head was still bent, the hand still moving over
the chart. And wherever deeds are kept, he felt, the thing was done all the same.

No one had seen. The officer was far forward; the after end of the deck was his
alone. Nothing had watched him but the eye the chaplain had preached about — the
one that was ever upon them, at sea or on dry land.

He stood out the hours of his watch at the rail, a criminal in thought, if not in
deed. Twice more before morning the trumpet sent its *Bright lookout fore and
aft!* down the dark ship, and twice more the answer went round from station to
station and came to him, and his own voice gave it back from the lee quarter,
steady as any man's. Wherever he put his eyes she was
there: down over the side, in the black water running along the hull; up past the
sails, in the sky between the yards.

At eight bells — four in the morning — the watch was relieved, and he went below
and lay in his hammock with his eyes open.

He did not sleep.

## 3 · Sunrise

At sunrise the hail came down from the fore-topsail yard, high on the foremast,
where the ship kept her farthest-seeing lookout: a wreck in sight.

"Where away?"

"Two points on the weather bow" — just off her course, on the side the wind came
from.

The word went through the ship faster than any order ever moved. Charlie came up
with the rest of them, sleepless, into the early light, and the watch crowded the
rail to look.

The distance closed as the light came on, and the wreck began to come apart in
their eyes — no hull, no spars, no boats, no men. It was a tree. A great
cottonwood, whole, adrift on the open Atlantic, its roots washed bare — a piece of
some far shore loose on blue water, going up and down, unhurried.

Charlie stood at the rail in the sunrise and looked at it a long time. It was the
first thing since midnight that was not her face.
