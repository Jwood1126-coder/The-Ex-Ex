## 1 · Port Jackson, the night of 29 November 1839

The squadron came up on Sydney Heads at dusk, and there was no pilot. There should have
been one; a harbor like this kept pilots the way a great house keeps servants, and a
stranger was expected to lie off till morning and wait to be led in. Wilkes did not lie
off. He put the *Vincennes* at the gap between the two black headlands and took her
through in the dark himself, the other three following her stern-light, and the only
chart aboard was the printed one any bookseller in the world could sell.

Reynolds had the deck — was the officer standing the watch — for part of it, and wrote it
down that night while his hand still remembered the cold of the rail. The leadsman swung
the lead in the fore-chains, the little platform slung out over the ship's side, and
called the depth up into the dark, over and over, the one human voice in a silence that
had the size of a country in it. The headlands closed astern. The harbor opened — bay
after black bay of still water, and here and there along its edge the yellow of a lamp
in a window, a town asleep and keeping no watch. They ran up into the middle of it and
let the anchors go a little before eleven: four men-of-war of a foreign navy, and not a
soul ashore knew they were there.

In the morning Sydney woke and found them.

The newspapers worked the arithmetic out in print by the end of the week, and it did not
flatter the colony. Four hostile ships could have done exactly what these four had done —
come in past the Heads in the dark, past every battery and every regulation, fired the
shipping at their leisure, and been at sea again before the town had finished its
breakfast. Her Majesty's fortress at the bottom of the world had been boarded in its
sleep, by a friend, this time.

## 2 · The Cove

The forecastle went ashore in its watches. The crew's quarters were forward, under the
bows, and the men who lived there were the last aboard to be told anything and the first
to be turned out for it; they came to know a harbor from the waterline up. Charlie came
to know Sydney from the Cove.

The Cove was the sailors' quarter — the run of grog-shops and boarding-houses along the
water where a ship's boat could land a crew and collect it again in worse repair. Charlie
had known Ann Street in Boston and had seen the Five Points in New York, and he
set the Cove down beside them the way a man matches soundings: the same trade, the same
women, the same watered rum, in the same want of daylight. Many of the rum-shops, the
story ran round the boats, were owned by the police. One of them was called the Dew Drop Inn,
and a man had to say it aloud twice before the joke of it arrived.

At the Jolly Sailors' Inn one night the boats' crews of four navies were drinking in the
same low room, close and smoke-thick — Russians, Englishmen, Frenchmen, and the
Americans — and it came round to singing. Each nation stood up in its turn and sang its own, the rest listening and then
roaring at the chorus in whatever words they had, and when the songs gave out they all
spliced the mainbrace together, which is the sailor's word for a drink served round to
every hand at once. For an hour the four flags that watched one another across every
anchorage on earth were one crew in a rum-shop, and no man owned anything but the song in
his mouth.

The daylight streets gave the other face of it. Charlie passed the chain-gangs at their
work, convicts fifty and a hundred to a gang, ironed together at the ankle and stepping
to the time of it, mending the colony's roads under the muskets. A man he had drunk beside in the Cove might be at the end of a
chain in the morning, and there was no telling the two conditions apart by looking. In
Sydney the difference between a free man and a felon was a paper, and it was dangerous in
the street to ask to see it.

## 3 · The mailbag

The mail found them at Sydney, and it was nine months old.

It had gone out from the States in the spring and chased the squadron down one ocean and
up another, round the Horn and across the whole width of the Pacific, always a port
behind, and it came aboard at last in a canvas bag the whole ship counted before it was
opened. Nine months. Whatever it said had stopped being true a long while back, and no
man aboard could do a thing about any of it now but read it and carry it.

Willie Roberts read for the men who could not. He was a Boston boy who got his usual
number of letters every mail and read them out to Charlie the way another man might share
his tobacco; and when the bag came in he sat on a chest under a lantern and read other
men's letters too, quietly, to whoever set one in his hands and could not open it alone.
One of them, that night, was edged in black. The man it belonged to had lost somebody at
home in the spring and had gone the whole nine months not knowing — laughing at the
mess-table, standing his watches, grieving nothing, because the grief had not reached him
yet — and the letter was how he learned it, in November, in another hemisphere, in a
stranger's reading voice.

Charlie had no letter. He never did; there was no one in the back rooms off Commercial
Street who could have set one down. What he had instead was the yeoman's storeroom and
his lessons, and after a year of them he could make one word on paper in a hand a stranger
could read, and the word was *mother*. He knew a few others. He did not yet have the words
for what a man wants to send home from the far side of the world, and he was old enough
now to feel the whole size of what he could not yet write.

## 4 · December

Reynolds went at the colony with his collector's eye and found it the strangest society he
had ever walked through: an English town, church-going and horse-racing and speaking his
own language, standing on a foundation no one in it would name aloud. He set it down in the
tone of a man reporting a natural curiosity.

"There are a few, a very few, honest people there," he wrote, "but you must be cautious in
your mode of speech. It is dangerous to whisper *transportation* in the street... I saw a
fellow dashing by, driving tandem. He was wealthy, but his term had just been out three
weeks. One man built a palace on the ground where the gallows had been erected for him, &
from which he had been reprieved when the rope was around his neck! (3 times, as the story
goes.)"

A country of second chances, kept up by silence. The Americans found it half admirable
and wholly strange, and the colony, starved for new faces, feted them through it —
dinners, balls, the whole hospitality of a place at the far edge of the world that did not
often get the chance to show off.

By the code of the thing the officers owed a return, and the natural return was a ball
aboard the flagship. They proposed it. The commander refused. He would not spend the
government's money on an entertainment, he said, and offered instead a set of half-measures
no gentleman could put his name to; and when the officers pressed him he let them
understand, in substance, that he was glad of the quarrel and did not depend upon them. A
man could read it two ways, and the officers read it the way that cost them nothing
to believe.

Reynolds went below and wrote the entry that is the sound of something finishing.

"He was unreasonable & proposed measures that no one could think of adopting... insulted
the whole body of his Officers, & made himself despised & contemptible... The foul scamp —
he should be hung. I have not patience to speak of him with decency, neither does he
deserve I should. Mercy, how he is hated. Four sycophants cling to him—"

Fifteen months before, the same pen had called the same man a wonder, and had meant it.
There is no measuring the distance between the two entries by anything a chart will hold.

## 5 · The ice, waiting

Under the balls and the quarrels ran the real business, and this time no one pretended it
was anything else. Through December the ships stored and refitted for the south, and Sydney
was a town full of men who had watched whalers fit out for high latitudes all their lives.
They came down to the water, looked the American ships over, and asked the questions any
whaling master asks. Where was the doubled planking? Where were the reinforced bows, the
sheathing, the extra timber a hull wanted before a sane man pointed it at the pack? The
Americans had no answer, because there was none. The hulls were as Norfolk had built them,
and Norfolk had built them thin.

The colonists put it plainer than the officers cared for. The ships were too frail to
cruise among icebergs, they said; but the Americans were young and foolhardy and reckless
and would go and be drowned. Some of them asked, not altogether as a joke, whether the
crews had made their wills. The men had been ordered to go, and they obeyed orders, and
they had learned by now that a ship's condition was a thing set down on paper and altered
by nothing.

Charlie kept his Christmas in Sydney harbor and did not think to count it, though it was
the last one some of the men around him would ever keep. On the twenty-sixth of December
the four ships stood out through the same Heads they had ghosted past in the dark a month
before — the flagship *Vincennes*, the *Peacock* and *Porpoise* behind her, and the little
schooner *Flying Fish* — and turned south, and the *Druid*, lying in the roads, cheered
them as they went.

Three men went with them who were on no list. Somewhere out on the cold water they would
give themselves up at the mainmast in sailors' rig, having stowed away at Sydney to get
clear of it, and asked to be entered for their rations and nothing more. They had chosen
the ice. Every man aboard knew now exactly where the ice was, and how thin the wood was
that stood between it and them.
