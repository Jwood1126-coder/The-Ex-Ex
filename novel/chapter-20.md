## 1 · The record

The expedition for the western islands had been out from Mbua Bay a week and more,
and by the twenty-fourth of July the boats at Malolo were short of food. Lieutenant
Underwood took a party ashore to trade for provisions, the interpreters with him —
Oahu Sam, the prisoner Vendovi's barber, and John Sac the New Zealander. Seven of his
ten rifles had gone back aboard the brig *Porpoise* days before, to lighten his boat,
and the islanders who had helped drag that boat over a reef had seen exactly what was
left. In Lieutenant Alden's cutter, off the beach, sat the surety: a son of the
island's chief, kept in the standard manner while the market ran. Every part of the
season's machinery was present that day, and every part of it was in ordinary use.

The pledge went over the side and made for home, swimming, then running
the shallows, looking back over his shoulder as he went — a man of a country where
they watched the muzzle and dodged at the flash. Alden leveled his musket and held
his fire; Midshipman Clark was directed to put a shot over the swimmer's head, and
did. On the beach the old chief "immediately cried out that his son was killed, and
ordered the natives to make fight."

The trade party was standing among them at the water's edge, holding pigs and yams.

Underwood got his men together and brought them off backward, faces to the crowd. He
knocked one attacker down with a pistol-butt, took a spear-wound, fired, and went
down under a club. Midshipman Wilkes Henry, called to help cover the retreat,
answered that "he
had just received a blow from the club of a native, and would first have a crack at
him," and had it, and at the surf-line, just as he fired, was struck on the back of
the head and went face-down into the water. When the boats fought their way back to
the beach, the record gives what they found in a single sentence: "On the beach lay
Lieutenant Underwood, partly stripped, and Midshipman Henry, quite naked, with a
native close by the latter, badly wounded, who was at once despatched."

The record is the commander's own, printed five years afterward, and it opens the
account by declaring its wound: "One of the victims was my own near relation,
confided to my care by a widowed mother." Three pages later it delivers the ruling:
the attack had been "entirely unprovoked," and punishment would be taken "not by the
burning of the towns alone, but in the blood of the plotters and actors in the
massacre." Between the wound and the ruling stand the record's own earlier sentences:
the surety kept in the boat, the leveled musket, the warning shot, the father's cry.
The same hand wrote them all, and let them all stand.

The punishment itself fills five pages of the record. The rest of the rulings come
after it. When, at low water, the island put a young woman alone out on the bare
reef with the dead officers' clothes arranged beside her and a white cock in her
arms — the emblem of peace in those islands — the commander took the clothes and
declined the bird: there would be no peace "until it should be sued for after their
own fashion." Two boys, about fifteen and seventeen, were sent up the island
carrying two reeds and a message: all the people of Malolo were to come by the time
the sun stood overhead, "to beg pardon and sue for mercy," and if they did not come,
"they must expect to be exterminated." And when the conquered brought two of their
dead chiefs' daughters as a present for the great chief, the great chief refused
them, in a sentence the record preserves entire: "we never fought against women or
children, and never received any gifts or presents."

## 2 · The pen

On Friday, the thirty-first of July, the *Vincennes* lay at anchor off Vanua Levu
with the *Peacock* beside her, a hundred and fifty miles of reef and channel from
Malolo, and Reynolds sat at a table with the season's work in front of him, drafting
a chart of Mbua Bay. William May brought him the news. It was days old by then, and
had crossed all that water by boat and by mouth to reach him: Underwood and Henry
were dead, killed at an island in the west while trading for food.

Reynolds had been two years keeping the other ledger. The journal under his hand had
counted floggings and dated rages, arraigned the commander nightly in a court of one;
no witness in the squadron watched Wilkes harder or forgave him less. What he wrote
of Malolo in that journal, when he wrote it, was a judgment, and the judgment was
approval: the punishment just, the reprisal endorsed whole and without reservation.

Then the chart of Mbua Bay wanted finishing, and he finished it.

## 3 · The body

Charlie was with the boats off the island. He had not been at the beach; he was in
neither cutter's crew; the fight reached him as words, most of them from the men who
had brought the bodies off — Joseph Clark among them, who had stood in it, and whose
upper lip, torn hanging to his chin, the surgeon sewed back.

The schooner *Flying Fish* carried the dead. The boats followed her ten miles —
fifty years on, an old man in Boston would write that they went *on our melancholy
errand* — to a bare sand island with a stand of great fig trees at its middle. The
two went ashore in the commodore's gig, his own
light boat, side by side, wrapped in the flag. The grave was dug deep in the white
coral sand; twenty sailors, all in white, stood around it; Mr. Agate read the
service; three volleys went out over the water. Then the burial party unmade its own
morning — the sand smoothed level, false diggings made at other spots, dead leaves
scattered over the true one — so that the grave could never be found and opened. The
island itself was made the monument instead. Henry's Island, the chart says, in a
small cluster called Underwood's Group.

On the morning of the twenty-sixth the boats ringed Malolo so that no canoe could
leave it, and — the words are Charlie's own — *at nine o'clock we landed well
armed*. Seventy men, in three divisions, with rockets in the gear. The orders were
to move on Sualib, the town of the men who had done the killing, destroying the
plantations as they went, "sparing none except women and children."

The first town they reached stood empty, and burned. Charlie walked with his
division, a musket heavy on his shoulder, and the smoke of that town went with them
down the island.

Sualib did not stand empty. Sualib was a fortress, and not a rude one: a ditch
twelve feet wide, full of mud and water; behind it a palisade of coconut trunks with
living trees growing in the line; behind that a wicker fence ten feet high that no
eye could see through, loopholed for muskets; inside that, a fresh-dug ditch and a
parapet of earth. No Fijian force had ever taken it. The people inside — and the
women were inside, fighting; the bows, mostly, were in the women's hands — called
"lako-mai" over the fence at the divisions: come on.

For a quarter of an hour the town held its ditch. Then a rocket — the war kind, a
tool for throwing fire farther than a man can — lodged burning in a thatched roof;
"a native sprung up to tear it off," the record says, and "that moment was his
last." After that the fire had the town, and what that was like is in the record in
one sentence: "The shouts of men were intermingled with the cries and shrieks of the
women and children, the roaring of the fire, the bursting of the bamboos, and an
occasional volley of musketry." Within the hour Sualib was ashes. Four bodies were
found in them, and one of the four was a child.

In the ditch where the fight had opened, among dropped war-clubs and muskets, a man
picked up *the cap of Lieutenant Underwood*.

On the water it went the same way. Canoes running for the neighboring islands were
overtaken and destroyed. When Emmons's crew, "so much excited," kept firing into two
canoes they had intercepted, it was Wilkes who came up in his gig and ordered the
firing stopped and quarter given, and Wilkes who put a woman, a girl, and an infant
free over the side, "telling them we did not war against women and children." Later
in the day five canoes turned together on Emmons's single boat crew — some eight
warriors to a canoe, with breastworks rigged against musketry — and four of the five
were destroyed with every warrior in them. By five o'clock, by Charlie's account,
every town on Malolo was destroyed. The American cost of the day was a jacket torn
by a ball, a handful of arrow wounds, and one quarter-gunner, Samuel Stretch, hurt
badly enough that his division came home the long way round the beach, on the level
sand, for his sake.

The island took a day to answer the message of the reeds. Then, up the eastern hill
where the commander waited, the conquered came: some forty men, "crouching on their
hands and knees," stopping as they climbed to moan, crawling to within thirty feet
of the strangers before an old man raised his voice, his head still at the ground.
They acknowledged themselves conquered, he said. The island belonged to the victors,
and its people were their slaves. Their provisions were destroyed and their houses
burned, and their head chiefs were dead, and most of the chiefs' wives with them.
The officers on the beach had done them no harm, he said — had been killed "without
the slightest cause" — and the men who did it had answered for it in the fire.

The crawl was not the strangers' invention. It was the islands' own form, the
submission their law prescribed for the utterly conquered, as exact as their law of
surety, and they performed it exactly. Wilkes had refused the white bird and named
the price: peace would be sued for after their own fashion. Of all the usages of
that country the squadron had handled in a season — the pledge over the gunwale, the
tariff of ransom, the emblem on the reef — this was the one the commander read
fluently, and this was the use he found for it.

"They acknowledged a loss of fifty-seven killed." The count is the record's, taken
on the hill, and the record itself leaned toward counting the dead of the canoe
fight inside it, and could not be sure. The reckonings made since run from
seventy-four to a hundred and four; an old man of Malolo, asked years afterward,
made it about eighty. The numbers do not close.

There is no count in that range at which it was not a massacre.

The terms of the peace were the islands' own terms for the conquered: come unarmed,
restore what was taken, bring provisions, carry water for the victors. Through the
next day some seventy of the people of Malolo filled the squadron's casks — three
thousand gallons, carried until nearly sundown — and told the interpreters, more
than once, that "it would have been as well for them to have been killed in battle
as to die of hard work." Among the things they gave back were the silver watch of
Lieutenant Underwood, "almost entirely melted up," and a piece of the eye-glass of
Midshipman Henry. The watch had been in Sualib when Sualib burned.
