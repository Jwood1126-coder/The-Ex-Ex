## 1 · The Golden Gate, 1841

On the last day of October the *Vincennes* stood out through the gate of San Francisco Bay
and put her head east for home the long way, and at her masthead the broad pennant streamed
ahead of her: the long forked streamer that told every sail she passed which ship carried
the commanding officer. Charlie had watched it fly for three years and better. The Navy List
still called the man under it a lieutenant. The pennant said commodore, and the pennant had
gone everywhere the ship had gone.

They had come into the bay in August, past a low huddle of adobe on the south shore that his
memoir would one day call a small Spanish settlement and leave at that. There was nothing on
that ground to make a sailor look twice — a customs house, a few hundred souls, cattle on the
brown hills. The city that stands there now, the one half the world would run to in
'forty-eight, when a man found color in the millrace up the very river the boats had just been
sounding, had not yet been imagined. The squadron surveyed the harbor it would want and moved
on.

Up the Sacramento the launch went as far as a launch could go, into a country of elk coming
down to drink and coyotes howling the camps awake and grizzly cubs the men swore were playful
as kittens. And in the bay a Boston ship, the *Alert*, gave them the news of the world:
President Harrison was dead. He had died in April. It was August. Four months for the word to
cross the continent to men who had been three years outside it, and they took it the way they
took all news now, which was as history.

The bay nearly kept one of them on the way out. Becalmed and rolling the first night in seven
fathoms — a fathom the span of a man's outstretched arms, and no great depth under her keel with
a sea running that high — the *Vincennes* was riding by three in the morning in breakers that
stood better than thirty feet, and at four a sea came in over the left bow and swept the spar deck —
the open upper deck — from end to end, staving the boats in the wooden cradles that held them
fast to the deck. A spar fetched loose
and struck Joseph Allshouse, a marine, and within the hour he was dead. They buried him off
Monterey the next day, wrapped in the flag on a rough plank, and Hudson read the service and
three volleys went out flat over the water. A deathlike stillness pervaded the ship, Charlie
wrote. He kept by then a long unofficial list of the ways the voyage spent men, and not one of
them was in the orders.

## 2 · The prisoner, and the ships

There was a man aboard the orders had never meant to carry home.

Vendovi — a chief of Rewa, a brother of its king — had been taken in irons off Fiji a year and
a half before and moved to the flagship, and he had crossed the Pacific twice on her since,
given the run of her decks at a chain's distance, sketched by the artists — sitting for them
the way a chief sits, without seeming to see the hand that drew him — studied by the
naturalists, and known to the forecastle simply as a shipmate it had not chosen. The Fourth of
July past, at the company farm on Puget Sound, he had walked in the procession dressed in the
fashion of his own country, leading the ship's Newfoundland dog on a short length of tarred line
while the fifes went ahead of him — a king's brother lent to an American holiday. He kept the
bearing of his rank in a situation his captors had never thought past the arrest. He was going
toward the country that had sent for him, and he crossed the last of the voyage the way he had
crossed all of it: quiet, watching, the one passenger no one could quite meet in the eye.

The squadron that carried him was thinning under him. It had sailed as six and it was coming
home as two. The *Sea Gull* had foundered off the Horn with her fifteen; the *Relief* had been
sent home years back; the *Peacock* had left her bones on the Columbia bar. From Honolulu the
two that were left had stood west into the emptiest quarter of the ocean, raising bare Wake
Island in December — a low ring of coral a thousand miles from any other land, sea-birds and no
water — for no reason but to fix it on a chart, dining under Spanish chandeliers at Manila in the
new year, working down through the Sulu Sea where Wilkes closed out the last item in his orders
across a cabin table: a treaty of trade, signed and sealed with the ship's guns in plain view of
the shore that put its mark to it — the whole enterprise closing the way it had opened, on
business, with guns behind it. And at Singapore, in February, the last of the small ships went:
the *Flying Fish*, ninety-six tons, the little schooner that had driven farther south than
Cook, condemned and sold off under a foreign flag because Wilkes would not trust her round the
Cape. The men who had frozen aboard her stood and watched her go under stranger's colors, and
every bosom, Charlie wrote, was filled with sadness.

The commander was still both of the men he had always been. At Honolulu on the way west the
boarding-house keepers came off with some two thousand dollars in bills run up against his
crew, and Wilkes told them he was only sorry the sum was not fifty thousand, since he would not
see one cent of either paid. It was the same port, a year on, where he had flogged men at the
gangway past the lawful count for asking the discharge their signed time owed them. The same
port, and the same man.

Then the sea took the one who was to have kept charge of Vendovi. In the Indian Ocean, in
March, Benjamin Vanderford died — the old Salem shipmaster who spoke Fijian and had been named
to hold the chief in America. Nothing could induce Vendovi to look at the corpse of his friend.
His spirits went out of him from that day. He had been failing already, the salt rations of a
man-of-war never having agreed with him; after Vanderford he failed faster, in his cot, while
the ship ran west with the trades and the men around him counted the days to a home he did not
have.

## 3 · St. Helena

They watered at St. Helena in the first days of May, the mid-ocean rock every homebound sailor
wanted to set his foot on, and climbed the valley to the grave where the age's great prisoner —
Bonaparte — had been put down twenty years before under a willow beside a spring. The grave was empty.
France had come for him the same season the squadron was in the ice; the Emperor had gone home
to Paris more than a year ago, and what was left for the sailors to look at was an oblong of
iron railing around a hole. They looked, and went back down to the boats, and the *Vincennes*
carried her own prisoner past the island, west and north, toward the far country he had been
fetched ten thousand miles to be shown.

Near the Tropic of Cancer, at the end of June, with the men building castles in the air and
the chief failing below, Charlie squared his own account. This was his sixth year at sea. He
had made up his mind he was done with the Navy. And here the pen stops in Boston, half a
century on, and the old man's other voice comes up through the page — the one that never did
forgive it. Men lashed to the rigging, made spread eagles of, and flogged. *Fifty years have
passed,* he wrote, *and how little reform has been made in the treatment of sailors!* He was
thinking of a drummer-boy at Sydney, no one of theirs, a stowaway of a British regiment
court-martialed and sentenced to a hundred and five lashes, of which he took ninety-three,
*five of them applied after the breath had left his body.* He had watched navies flog for four
years, his own among them, and he set the whole of it down at the last as a cry: *My God! is
this our civilization?*

## 4 · The Narrows, 1842

Four years all but two months out, the *Vincennes* felt for the bottom of her own coast in a
fog. Eighty fathoms, and the lead came up with gray sand on the tallow — the lump of fat pressed
into the foot of the lead so it would bring back whatever it touched on the bottom — and she
fired a gun into the murk for a pilot, and a pilot answered and came off to her. On the morning of the
tenth of June the fog lifted on the Highlands of Neversink — the first American land any of
them had seen since 1838 — and past the Highlands lay the long low arm of Sandy Hook, and past
the Hook the gate they called the Narrows, opening slowly between its two green shores with the
harbor and the city standing up beyond.

Somewhere below decks was the letter-bag. Four years before, a boy had carried a hat full of
other men's letters over a Boston drawbridge and gone into the water after them when they fell;
whatever it held now was his own, or would be — home come round in front of him at last instead
of dropping astern. Whether he could yet read it with his own eyes the record does not quite
say.

The broad pennant still stood out at the masthead, the fiction that had circled the earth, and
it had a few hours left to fly. The city came on across the water, and the *Vincennes* stood in
for it, and for one turn of the tide the whole voyage hung there in the gate: behind her
eighty-seven thousand miles, ahead of her the Narrows opening and New York rising, and no one
aboard yet knowing that the country had forgotten they were coming.
