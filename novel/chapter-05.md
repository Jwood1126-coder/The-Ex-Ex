## 1 · At sea, September 13th, 1838

On the thirteenth of September, three days short of the voyage's first landfall, the
commander published to the squadron an order from the Secretary of the Navy. Every officer
was to keep a journal of the cruise — the occurrences of each day, set down as they
happened — to be sent up weekly for inspection, and at the voyage's end surrendered to the
Navy Department, *to be used, if they possess any merit.* Journals kept privately were
forbidden.

The government had bought the ships, the instruments, and four years of the men's lives.
Now it claimed the words.

Aboard the flagship the order came down to William Reynolds, passed midshipman — an
officer who had stood his examination for lieutenant and passed it, and now waited in the
Navy's long line for a vacancy to open somewhere above him. He still messed in the steerage
with the other midshipmen, not aft in the wardroom with the lieutenants; that one door was
the whole distance his rank had left to close. He was twenty-three. The sea
had had seven of his years already — a first cruise to Rio while he was still a boy, and
everything since — and none of it had worn the shine off him. He had gone to sea for the
reasons in the books, and he was not ashamed of a single one of them. Seven years had not
yet shown him a thing he could not make a sentence of.

He was keeping a journal before the order existed; he had opened it the day the squadron
sailed. *Farewell home! Welcome the stirring scenes of strange Lands*, the first entry
ran — and then, farther down the same page, with the land sinking astern, the pen turned
around to face the people it was leaving: *oh! shield them from harm, give them health &
peace, grant them every blessing, and in mercy to us, allow them all once more to see the
wanderers from the firesides.* The stirring scenes in one breath, the firesides in the
next. That was the whole man, at twenty-three.

The order made his book contraband. A private page was insubordination now, and the Navy
answered insubordination on the body.

A passed midshipman waited on the Navy's good opinion for everything — the lieutenancy,
the next cruise, the shape of the life to come — and Reynolds now set the whole of it,
entry by entry, against a book.

The Navy got its journal. Reynolds ruled fresh pages and commenced the record required of
him — the courses made good, the weather, the occurrences of the day — correct enough to
go up weekly and come back without remark. And the other book went on. He set its terms
down inside it, plainly, on a page any inspector who ever caught it would read: it was
meant for no eyes *but those few at home, who will be kind enough to excuse inaccuracies
of thought or expression, & who will feel an interest in the writing, because of their
love for him who writes.*

After that the writing kept the hours the ship left over. Privacy on a crowded ship was a
courtesy, not a fact. He wrote when his watch was done — the four-hour turn of duty over, his stretch on deck
handed off to the next men — and the lamp was his, the pen learning to stop mid-word and
wait out a roll. The two books shared an inkwell and nothing
else. On inspection days the Navy's journal went up the companionway to the wardroom and came
back down with an officer's initial fresh in the margin; the other went
into his sea chest, under the linen, where every new entry was a fresh count of the same
offense.

## 2 · Funchal, September 16th–25th

On the sixteenth, Madeira stood up out of the sea ahead: volcanic ridges over clear rocky
bays, the island terraced green from the shore to the summits, the air off it seventy
degrees and full of land smells after a month of salt water. He had asked, on page one,
for the stirring scenes of strange lands. The first one was a garden.

The *Peacock* came in the next morning and anchored in Funchal Roads, off the town at the
head of the bay, and the boats went back and forth all day with four weeks of news. She had crossed the same ocean in the same weeks, bound for
the same island, and in all that water the two ships had never once sighted each other.
Reynolds turned it over in the journal: *strange that we should have crossed the Ocean
without seeing each other & yet have been so near.* An ocean was wide enough to hide a
squadron from itself, and the age had nothing to throw a voice past the horizon.

Better still, an American brig lay in the anchorage, homeward bound. A ship bound home
would carry mail — the first chance of the voyage to send a word back. *Our hearts were
full of gladness*, Reynolds wrote, and gave his evenings to pages that, for once, were
meant to travel — to go into the brig's mail-bag before she weighed. Whatever he sealed now would be months old before it was read; whatever
answered it would have to hunt the squadron from port to port, a year behind. Four years
had begun to show its length.

The squadron lay nine days at the island. The scientific gentlemen were up the mountain
paths almost before the anchors held, with their hammers and their plant presses; the
boats went about the Navy's work in the bay; and Reynolds got his hours ashore with the
rest, up through the terraces, where the island's business was wine and the wine began
with men.

In a treading shed above the town of Funchal he stood at the lip of a vat of grapes, treaders in it
to their shins, going round and round under a low roof, hands on each other's shoulders
for the balance of it. The heat pressed close, and the
shed smelled of crushed fruit gone warm. Sweat ran off the men — arms, faces, the working
legs. At the vat's edge an old man moved from leg to leg with a scraper, cleaning the
crushed skins and pulp from the treaders' calves and returning it to the press, so that
nothing should be lost. The must came up dark around their shins, and the vat gave back a
steady wet sound under the feet, tread over tread. Two bushels of grapes went to make a
single gallon.

The wine of Madeira was famous the world over, and famously hard to kill — a long hot
voyage through the tropics did not spoil it but improved it. Wilkes laid in casks of it
for the officers.

## 3 · Funchal Roads, before the mail closed

Word of the homeward brig went below as fast as it went aft. On the berth deck — the
crew's deck, one down from the guns, low-beamed and lamplit even at midday because no
daylight reached so far below, where two hundred men slung their hammocks — men who
could write, wrote, and men who could not went looking for a shipmate who could. The pens
aboard were few, and the line for them was not. All week the same scene ran along the mess
cloths: one man's words going down in another man's hand.

Charlie Erskine could not write his mother's name. He could not write his own.

There was a man in his mess — the twelve he ate with off a canvas cloth — who wrote a fair
hand. They found a half hour between watches and a cleared corner — cleared but not quiet: down
the mess cloth another pair argued over the spelling of a word, and a man stood off at
Charlie's shoulder, waiting his turn at the same pen. Charlie gave him
the letter a sentence at a time, standing, turning his hat in his hands, watching the pen
turn what he said into marks he could not follow. The man wrote slowly, saying each word
half-aloud as he set it down, the way careful writers do. What Charlie had to say was what
he always had to say. It was always mother, mother, mother with him — his own account of
himself, given years afterward — and the letter was hers.

When it was done the man read it back, pointing along the lines as he went, as if Charlie
could follow the finger if not the words. Charlie took his own letter the way he took
everything that lived on paper: at second hand, on trust, every word. Then the man laid
the sheet flat and held out the pen, and at the foot of the page, where a name goes,
Charlie made his mark.

Whatever came home for him in return — next year, the year after, in some port on the far
side of the Horn — would reach him the same way: folded words he could not open without
borrowing another man's eyes.

The letter went into the bag with the squadron's hundreds. If the bag had been spilled out
on the deck in front of him, he could not have told which one was his.
