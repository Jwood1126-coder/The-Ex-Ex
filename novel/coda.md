## 1 · Boston, 1890

Writing hurt. Not the remembering — the writing, the plain work of it in the hand. He
built his letters one stroke at a time, each bent and closed like a knot before the next
was begun, and fifty years of practice had put trueness into them and no speed at all.
Every few lines the hand set itself into a cramp around the pen, and he would lay the pen
down, work the hand open against his knee, and take it up again.

The pages did not go forward clean. Words went down and were hauled down again, each
struck through with a single bar. Here and there a whole passage lay dead under its
crossing-out, the repair standing in the margin in smaller letters — the letters of a man
saving paper.

Below decks on a ship at sea the hand had been taught, and taught late, and for the
first year of its life its whole fortune was one word: *mother*. There had been a season,
between the ice and Fiji, when its owner could count out everything it knew how to set
down — *mother*, *home*, *sisters*, *brothers*, *Roxbury*, *Boston*, *Big Dick*,
*Torrent 6*, *Hurrah for Jackson, all nations!* — the whole cargo, and proud of every
item in it. The same hand had now put down a book three hundred pages long, and was near
the end of it.

The manuscript on the table had left the twenty-ninth of August, 1838, far astern. It had
crossed its oceans. It had come nearly home.

## 2 · The third day ashore

He came to the third day ashore: New York, June of 1842, the crew paid off and
scattering into the boarding-houses, and word going among them that Vendovi was dead.

He laid a fresh sheet on the table, and put the pen down, and went after the face.

Two years the man had been aboard — a chief of Rewa, in the Fijis, carried the width of
the Pacific as a prisoner entered on a man-of-war's books — and for the better part of
those years he had stood within a boy's plain daily sight. Charlie shut his eyes and
looked.

What came was the club. The little iron-wood war-club out of the capture story, the
*handy billy*, a thing he had never once seen except in the telling. Behind the club
came the teller — old Tom Finer at his yarns, eighteen months of them — and behind the
yarns came the name the forecastle had finally hung on the prisoner, *the old Christian,
cannibal, man-eater*, the crew's own words, worn smooth with handling. Behind the name
came the one thing he had witnessed for himself: the passage home, old Vanderford the
Fiji pilot dead in the Indian Ocean, and the chief refusing to look at the body of his
friend, and his spirits going out of him from that day. Even that memory came with the
man turned away. And behind it all, nothing. He sat a long while with his eyes shut. The
face did not come.

He tried it sideways. The Fourth of July at Nisqually, on Puget Sound — the far
Northwest coast, half the world from Rewa — came back to him whole: the fife and drum,
the procession on the trading company's farm, and the chief walking in it dressed in the
fashion of his own country, leading the ship's dog on two fathoms of marling, a dozen
feet of thin tarred twine. Nobody had asked the chief whether he would march. He could
see the dog. He could see the marling, and the dress, and the bright day standing around
them. Where the face hung in the middle of the day there was nothing at all.

The sheet stayed white in front of him. He had given the man his pages back, and his
death in four words; what he could not give the book was a face he no longer owned. He
dipped the pen and wrote the day the news came, and it made one sentence:

*The third day on shore we heard of the death of Vendovi, the Fiji chief.*

The sentence ended, and in all the pages that remained, nothing about Vendovi ever
followed it.

He read the passage over. It held everything the four years had kept of the man: a
story, a club in the story, a name off the yarns, a date, and a blank where a face
belonged.

The blank was part of the collection.

## 3 · The page that had waited

One page in the pile was older than the pages around it and shorter than all of them. He
had written its first line on the night the manuscript stood at the twenty-ninth of
August — one sentence promising a secret, and under the sentence, white paper. The book
had gone on around it, Madeira and the Horn and the ice and Fiji and home, and the page
had lain in its place with its one sentence, keeping the spot open.

Now the book was done all around it, and the hole in it was one page big.

He turned it up and dipped the pen, and the pen went dry twice over the paper before
anything came. What came first was a prayer:

*Would to God I could blot it from my memory!*

What had to come next was an accounting: who knew. He wrote it slowly, knot after knot.

*Through all these years it has been known to none save myself and to Him whose
all-seeing eye is ever upon us.*

The all-seeing eye was not his phrase. It was the chaplain's, preached over the
squadron's heads the first Sunday at sea, with the land going down astern and a boy of
sixteen listening. He had kept that sentence fifty-two years, the way he had kept the
oath, and now he spent the one to close out the other.

Then he told it — the skylight, the man beneath it, the pin — and what had ended it: the
upturned face of his mother, seen, or fancied seen, between his hands and the commander;
the pin going back into the rack; how long it had taken his hand to come off the iron.
The house was quiet around him; the lamp burned steady; nothing rolled. Halfway down the
page the hand shut into its cramp, and he worked it open against his knee and went on.
It all went down in the plain order it had happened, and it took fewer lines than the
flogging had. At the bottom of the page he sentenced himself:

*I felt as if I had committed the act, and were a criminal in thought, if not in deed.*

The page filled. He laid it into the pile where it had always belonged and squared the
pile, and the pile was a book, and the book was bound for the printer's.

## 4 · The leaf

It wanted its dedication.

He tried the shipmates first — *To my shipmates of* — and drew the bar through the line
before it was whole. The book itself had already said, a long way back, what he believed
had become of the six hundred and eighty-seven; that letter had no one left to receive
it but the man writing it. He tried his mother, and the bar went through that too. Her
blessing stood in the book twice already, at the setting-out and at the end of
everything. He let her keep those.

The commander needed no considering. The title page already carried him.

He sat with the pen up. Then he set down six lines.

> To THE CREW OF THE
> *Ship Universal*
> THIS BOOK IS
> RESPECTFULLY DEDICATED
> BY ONE OF THEIR SHIPMATES
> THE AUTHOR

He entered himself on her books — one of their shipmates — and he signed. The first
ship's articles that ever asked him for his name had got a mark. These he signed *The
Author*.

It was the last page written, and it would stand first in the book.

*  *  *

The manuscript's own last page was already done. His dead he had counted pages back —
some of them asleep, he had written there, in *old Ocean's sepulcher*. The last page
reckoned the time at sixty years since his mother's parting blessing, and said plainly
that he found himself *nearing the end of life's voyage*. And it promised one more ship:
a Heavenly Flag Ship, under *one loving Lord High Admiral*, who is *no respecter of
persons* — and mustered for her every hand the cruise had scattered, the living and the
drowned together.

Below that stood one question, and the book closed on it.

A hail is the plainest signal a sailor owns. It goes out over dark water to a deck the
hailer cannot see — and every hand alive stands somewhere on the one deck the leaf had
named. The last sentence Charles Erskine ever printed is a hail, and it is still
carrying.

*Shipmates, shall we meet together there?*
