## 1 · New York, June 1842

On the eleventh of June the papers gave the return of the largest voyage the Republic had ever floated a column, and set it on the page beside a resolution touching the cultivation of hemp. In Washington a bill was already moving to print the expedition's discoveries in a style worthy of the great voyages of Europe — the work done, the legislative record said, *under the command of Lieutenant Wilkes.* Lieutenant. The rank stood in the nation's paper on the first day home exactly as it had stood on the muster roll on the first day out; four years and eighty-seven thousand miles had not moved it a grade.

Two witnesses had gone around the world and kept their testimony to themselves. One was a sailor's back, which remembered in scars and would not publish for half a century. The other was the secret journal of a passed midshipman — an officer examined and found fit for lieutenant, still waiting for the rank to be handed him — shut in a sea chest, a private record that ranked its keeper four hundred and somethingth in the chain of command and gave its verdict on the man at the top of it in words no service would ever have let him say aloud. Now a third witness took the floor, and this one anybody could read. The newspaper had come home with the fleet.

It had not waited for the fleet. All spring, while the squadron was still crossing the South Atlantic with the trade winds doing the work, the papers had been printing the expedition's quarrels ahead of its discoveries — the officers' grievances, the commander's severities, the four-year argument leaking into type out of the letters men had posted home. By the time the *Vincennes* came up the harbor to no salute, the argument was the story. And most of the fleet was not even in yet: the brigs *Porpoise* and *Oregon* were still at sea, "expected early in July." The nation was reading the voyage before the men who had made it were all ashore to be asked about it.

## 2 · Washington, on or about the twentieth of June

Before the court could sit, the commander went to a lectern.

At a meeting of the National Institution in Washington — a society of gentlemen got up for the promotion of science, the kind of hall where a man stands at the front and reads his paper aloud to the seated members and is thanked for it — Lieutenant Wilkes gave, the notice said, "a brief narration of his late voyage," and "vindicated the American discoveries." Among the members sat Mr. Poinsett — the man who had given him the squadron, and two administrations out of the government now — hearing what had become of it.

There was a good deal to vindicate. Whether there was a continent at the bottom of the world at all, and whose eyes had first raised its coast, was not a settled question, and would not be settled for a generation and more. The Englishman Ross had sailed south after Wilkes, through water where the American charts showed land, and disputed the land; and the quarrel was already in the papers, conducted in the flat vocabulary of proof — Wilkes's chart of the Antarctic ice, and the reprinted letter "which was furnished him by Captain Wilkes (previous to Captain R.'s antarctic cruise)," and a coast marked "Bellamy 1839," and a report "published in 1840" — dates and documents wheeled up like guns, each side firing to prove it had seen the shore first.

The court that would weigh the whole voyage had not yet convened. It was still a rumor of a docket, months from a first day. And the commander, not waiting on it, put his own account of the four years into the one instrument that reached the entire country at once and turned it up as far as it would go — the lectern, and behind the lectern the column, and behind the column every parlor and wardroom in the Republic. He was a lieutenant with a continent to his name and a reprimand that would come, and for the moment only the first of those was in the papers. Whatever a court might one day rule, the nation would hear this first, and hear it in his voice.

## 3 · The machine

What the country read next was a docket.

A court-martial is the service putting itself on trial. There is no jury: the judges are a panel of senior officers, judge and jury in one bench; a single officer called the judge advocate prosecutes the charges and keeps the record; and the whole of it sits not in a courthouse but aboard a ship — here the *North Carolina*, a ship of the line moored in New York harbor, a wooden fortress three gun decks tall that could have stowed the squadron's little brigs below like ship's boats. Her wardroom — the officers' long mess-room down under the spar deck — was cleared and fitted out as a court, a table at the head and the chairs ranked behind it. What such a court could do ran the length of the scale. It could reprimand a man on paper, or suspend him from duty, or break him out of the service for good, or hang him from a yardarm — the end of a spar high on the mast, in sight of the whole crew. The Navy carried its law aboard the way it carried its powder, and turned both on its own when it had to.

The papers laid the structure out in a sentence. The court would try "Pinckney, lieutenant Johnson, assistant surgeon Gil[lou?], passed midshipman May, lieutenant comd't. Reynolds, captain Walker, and others, on recriminating charges," and then "Lieut. commandant Wilkes will be tried, by the same court, on charges preferred by some of his late officers." Recriminating: each charge answered by a counter-charge. One court, and everyone in turn its prisoner: the commander prosecuting the officers who had defied him, the officers prosecuting the commander, each man at once accuser and accused. The paper printed the names as it had them and had some of them wrong — a surgeon's name broken in half, a rank hung on the wrong shoulder — the record garbling its own roll before the reckoning had begun.

The bench was named too: "com's. Barron, Stewart, Biddle, Downes, Tatnall, and five lieutenants" — a row of epaulettes behind the table, the gray and senior of the service, men who had held their own commands when Wilkes was a midshipman, and would rule on his now.

And the court had been convened — the item credited the New York *Journal of Commerce* — "by the special request of lieut. Wilkes." He had asked for it. The one man the machine could grind finest had wound it up and set it turning, to look into, in the paper's careful phrase, "some misunderstanding which arose between lieut. Wilkes and some of his officers."

A misunderstanding. It had taken four years, and gone around the world.

## 4 · The paper walking

That summer the chests of paper came down to the boats — four years of it, journals and letters, ferried out across the harbor to the ship where the court would sit. The evidence did not have to be gathered. It had been accumulating for four years, by order.

Every officer in the squadron had been required to keep a journal, and required to give it up; the commander had demanded their private correspondence besides. The daily record kept under compulsion — where the boats went, what the weather did, who said what to whom on which forenoon — was written each evening because the service commanded it be set down, and signed, and surrendered. So when the court convened there was nothing to reconstruct. The voyage had deposed itself as it sailed, in the deponents' own ink, four years deep.

At home in Lancaster that summer, a passed midshipman kept two journals. One was the official record the service had required of him and could send for at will. The other was the secret one no order reached — the one in which he had written, in his own hand, on nights the reader may guess at, that he wished to see the man hanged. Only one of the two was walking toward the *North Carolina*. It was the poorer book, and it was the one that would be read aloud.

So the summer's real procession was not the officers going down to the gangway. It was their paper going with them: four years of required journals and demanded letters, every man's own hand lying ready to be turned against another man, and against himself. The squadron had written itself down mile by mile because it had been ordered to. Now the order came due, and the writing would be read back to the men who wrote it — read out, page by page, in the wardroom, by the judge advocate whose whole office was to prosecute from the record.

The country had sent a squadron to chart coasts no one had seen. It got home a library, and convened a court to try it.
