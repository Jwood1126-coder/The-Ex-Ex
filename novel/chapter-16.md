## 1 · The *Vincennes*, 28 January 1840

For two weeks they had felt their way west along the edge of the ice, and the work had
settled into a grim housekeeping — stand into every opening, sound it, take the
bearings, come out, go on. Then the wind came down off the land to the south, all at
once, the way water comes off a roof, and caught them with sail set.

They sent the topmen up to take it in. Charlie went with them, up the mizzen — the
aftermost of the three masts — to the top, the platform where the flagship's youngest
hands kept their station. The reef-points had frozen. A reef-point is one of the short
lines sewn in rows across a sail, that a man knots to gather the canvas up small when he
shortens it; that night they had gone stiff as wire, and no fingers could bend them to a
knot. The gaskets were the same — the longer lines that lash a furled sail down along
the yard it hangs from — and a sail that will not be gasketed will not stay furled.

So the men stayed up in it, working bare hands on iron-hard rope, until their hands
stopped answering them. Then they were brought down the only way left. A bowline is a
loop that will not draw tight, whatever weight is hung in it; they slung a man in one
under the arms and eased him off the yard. His mittens had iced fast to the wood, and it
was the ice they broke to free him and not his grip, which had gone hours before. Then he
swung clear — a man hanging in a rope high over the pitching deck and, past the rail, the
sea and the ice, while below him the hands held the fall against the roll and walked him
down through the wind hand over hand, like cargo, one at a time, and sent up the next.

Through the snow they began to hear surf. There was no shore. It was the sea breaking
against ice — on the weather bow first, then the lee bow, then abeam, close, on both
hands at once. Two bergs, and the black water narrowing between them as they drifted
together. The ship ran the gut between the walls with every man who could still grip a
rope on deck. *We felt that we were death-doomed*, Charlie wrote fifty years after, and
did not improve on it.

She came through. The walls fell astern into the snow and never touched her. No one had
made them close, and no one could have made them open.

## 2 · Piner's Bay, 30 January

Two mornings later a bay opened to the south and let them a little way in before it
showed its back wall: a cliff of ice a hundred and fifty feet high, running out of sight
both ways, with mountains standing over it and, inland, two columns of what looked like
smoke. There was land under all that white. Every man aboard had stopped pretending
otherwise.

The wind came down off it the same as before — no warning in the sky, only the weight of
it arriving — and the snow in it that day seemed to have needles set in every flake. The
ship wore short round on her heel to get her head out. To wear a ship is to turn her by
bringing her stern through the wind, the slow safe way when there is no room for the fast
one: her stern swung across the eye of the gale, and her bow came round the long way after
it and crawled back down the water she had just made good. There was no room for anything
better. She stood off.

The bay went onto the chart with a name. Not the commodore's, and not an officer's. It
was called for Piner, the signal quartermaster — the man whose whole work was to bend
the commodore's orders onto a halyard and run them up in colored flags for the other
ships to read. It is on the charts yet. A man who never in his life gave an order of his
own has a bay of the last continent under his name, and I have looked for it there, and
it holds.

## 3 · The ship's books

Between the blows the ship kept her books like any other, which was its own kind of
madness. On the berth deck — the low space forward where the men slung their hammocks —
the glass stood at forty degrees, and there was nothing to do about it but drink the
coffee scalding and stand the next watch. The watch bill said watch and watch, four
hours on deck and four below; the ice said all hands, and the ice was the senior officer.

By the sixth of February the wet and the salt had raised boils on men's wrists and
necks, where the frozen collars and cuffs sawed at them. The sick list, which the
surgeons kept in a fair round hand, got longer every day.

And the commodore was always on deck. That was the thing the men could not get to the
bottom of. *The commodore seemed to be on deck all the time, and how he managed to sleep
was a mystery.* He was there through the white night and into the next day, in the
weather that was breaking them, and it was on his order that they went west into more of
it. The men who cursed him hardest never once caught him going below to get warm.

Then the surgeons put it in writing. The crew was breaking; the ship should turn north.
Wilkes called his officers together and required their opinions on paper — every man's,
signed. They came back agreeing with the surgeons, very nearly to a man. In the cabin, by
the lamp on the chart table, he read them through, every sheet, and took his time about
it. Then he squared them and put them away in the drawer where the ship's papers were
kept, and closed it, and gave the course west. What he made of them he kept behind his
face, as he kept most things.

## 4 · The ice, at play

Not all of it was dying. On the twelfth of February they came on a berg three miles long
— three times the size of Boston Common, longer than a man could walk end to end in an
hour — and found, when the boats went alongside and
men climbed up onto the flat of it, a pond of fresh water lying in the middle of the ice.
They watered the ship off a floating island. They filled the leather bags at the pond —
each one a two-man load full, a slack dead-weight that soaked the arms of the man who
hoisted it — and passed them down a line of hands strung across the ice to the boats at
the berg's foot, bag after bag after bag, the far south going into the casks a few gallons
at a time. And while the watering went on, the rest of the hands were simply turned loose
on the roof of the world to be boys.

*We had a jolly time while on this iceberg*, Charlie wrote, *sliding, snowballing one
another, and playing with the penguins and seals.* They slid. They pelted each other. A
sea-elephant lay and let itself be taken by the tail, and men were dragged across the ice
behind it, shouting. The penguins came in companies, marching in step behind one tall
king bird as a regiment marches behind its drum-major, and when the sailors carried one
off toward the boat its fellows threw themselves in after it, over the gunwale and into
the sternsheets, until the boat was near swamped with birds trying to rescue a shipmate.
Some of them went home in the end to the Smithsonian in glass. That afternoon they had
the better of the United States Navy.

The southern lights came twice that month. On the ninth they stood low along the northern
sky; on the seventeenth they took the whole of it. The men lay down flat on their backs on
the deck to watch — fire running from one edge of the world clean over the mast-trucks to
the other, folding in on itself like a fan and shaking loose again, orange at the fold. We
can say now what it was: the sun's own wind, thrown across ninety million miles of nothing
and striking the high cold air over the pole, lighting it the way current lights a lamp.
They had no such sentence for it. They lay on the freezing planks and looked up and said
nothing worth writing down, which was the honest response.

## 5 · 21 February 1840

John Sac was the happiest man aboard. *The happiest man on board our ship was John Sac, a
New Zealander* — a chief in his own country, Puatti by his own name, who had rounded half
the world in whaleships and could hand and reef and steer with any man in the crew, and
who, when the ship's head finally came round to the north, stood up in the bows and sang
out *New Zealand, New Zealand, no place like New Zealand!* to the whole gray ocean. He had
his reasons. They were turning for home.

They turned on the twenty-first. At the last headland, with the new season's ice already
making in the bays and the sick list no shorter for the turning, all hands were mustered
aft. Wilkes thanked them, and told them the government would not forget what they had
done, and they gave three cheers for the Antarctic Continent — fifteen hundred miles of
new coast laid down astern from a wooden ship that had gone to the ice with paint for
armor.

Then the order went round that the crew had waited on since Sydney: splice the mainbrace,
the old command to serve out an extra ration of spirits, grog all round, the one
indulgence a ship of that navy allowed itself for the plain feat of having lived. *Up to
this time we had had only hot coffee.*

They drank it standing, in the cold, out of tin cups, in hands that had left some of their
feeling on the yards and would never get all of it back. Off the lee quarter the coast
they had bought with those hands went gray, and then went into the snow, and then was only
on the chart.
