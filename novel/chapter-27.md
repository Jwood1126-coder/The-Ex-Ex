## 1 · New York harbor, the summer of 1842

The ships came home in June, and by August the country had put them on trial.

The court sat aboard the *North Carolina*, a ship of the line at anchor in New York
harbor, a wooden mountain that no longer went to sea. She was kept on as a receiving ship
for the room she had below, whole gun-decks of it, cleared and quiet, wide enough for the
benches and tables a court-martial wants. The business of the season was reciprocal. Wilkes
had preferred charges against the officers who had crossed him on the voyage; the
officers, their evidence led by the surgeon Charles Guillou, had preferred charges
against Wilkes. One court would hear both, the accusers of one case the accused of the
next, four years of a squadron's grievances sworn and dated at last across a single table.
The newspapers, which had spent the spring printing the expedition's quarrels ahead of its
discoveries, now had the quarrels under oath.

The charges against the commander gathered up the whole voyage and set it in a
list: the loss of the *Peacock* on the Columbia bar, the smother of breakers that guarded
the river's mouth; oppression and ill-usage of his officers; illegal punishment of his men,
the floggings carried past the dozen the law allowed. Each was its own count, to be proved
or not proved on its own paper.

So the whole squadron came back together that summer, and not at a wharf. It reassembled
in a courtroom — officers who had not stood in one room since Fiji or the Horn, brought in
to be sworn, to depose, and to be deposed against. The voyage had ended in June to no
cannon and no crowds. It went on ending, all August, in the measured language of charges
and specifications, a squadron of witnesses filing past a table.

The court had been convened, the press was careful to note, "by the special request of
lieut. Wilkes."

He had asked for it.

## 2 · Lancaster, the middle of August

Lieutenant William Reynolds read the trial the way the rest of the country read it: in
the newspapers, a week stale, carried inland by the slow mail. He was a lieutenant of one
year's standing — the commission had chased him across the Pacific and caught him near the
end of the voyage — and on the sixteenth of August he had married Rebecca Krug, of
Lancaster, who had waited out the voyage as faithfully as the commission had. He read the
New York papers at a garden table a long way from any ocean.

He had kept a second journal on that voyage, below the first, in a hand meant for no one,
and in it he had once, in so many words, wished the commander hanged. Now the commander
was a column of newsprint, and the wish had been overtaken by something slower and colder
than the yardarm he had once wished him from.

He had been the squadron's unofficial witness, the man who wrote things down where the log
would not. The witnessing was official now, sworn and notarized and printed, and it moved
at the pace of a thing done strictly by the book.

Niles' National Register for the thirteenth of August printed, entire, a letter from the
Secretary of the Navy to one of the officers Wilkes had accused — a reprimand, the
finished article, read into a man's record for good. The officer had been found guilty of
"disrespect to your superior in the execution of his office." The letter allowed that the
offence "involves no moral turpitude." It held, even so, that such disrespect "strikes at
the foundation of all discipline," for "A respectful deportment is part of the duty of
obedience, and obedience is the first law of military service."

Reynolds knew the liturgy from the inside of it. He had heard those same clauses read
before, over other men's names; only the name ever changed from one reading to the next.

## 3 · The papers of the twentieth

A week on, the Register for the twentieth of August carried two items a column apart: a
charge that had failed, and a sealed order pried open.

The first was an acquittal. Lieutenant Robert E. Johnson — the officer who had taken a
handful of men and their horses over the Cascade mountains and down to the beaver country
beyond, the expedition's one true march into the interior — had been tried on charges
preferred by Charles Wilkes: "disobedience of orders and wasting the public property." The
court acquitted him. The acquittal came over the signature of the Secretary of the Navy,
A. P. Upshur.

Wasting the public property — and the court would not have it.

The second was the sealed order. The same paper printed extracts from the
"private instructions issued to lieut. Wilkes by James K. Paulding" — the confidential
orders the Secretary had written him in December of 1839, the sealed guidance of the whole
enterprise, now set in type for the price of the paper it was on. Wilkes was a man of
documents. The Navy had first kept him not for his seamanship but for his instruments and
his charts, and he had spent his life as the keeper of the paper other men generated. Now
the most private paper of the voyage was public, entered into evidence by the same
machinery built to hold it. Paulding himself was more than a year out of office and the
administration that launched the voyage two administrations gone; the orders had outlived
the government that sealed them, and surfaced now as exhibits. The documents had always
been his to keep. They were keeping themselves now.

## 4 · The verdict, sealed

The defence was read on a Tuesday.

Niles' for the tenth of September reported it in four flat sentences: "The defence of
lieut. Wilkes was read before the Naval Court Martial at New York, on Tuesday. At the
conclusion of the defence, the Court remained in secret session for some time making up
its verdict, which when decided upon was transmitted to the Navy Department at
Washington."

So the verdict existed. It had been decided, written down, closed up, and put on the road
to Washington — and no man who had sailed in the squadron, not Wilkes, not Guillou, not
the lieutenant reading the paper in Lancaster, knew a word of what it said. It traveled at
the speed of the mail, which was the speed of a horse, toward a Secretary who would open
it when he chose. For the rest of the country the outcome was still an open question,
argued in the papers as it had been argued all summer. For the officers behind the closed
door on the *North Carolina*, it was already settled.

In the same issue, in the same close columns, the Congress finished its own piece of the
business. The act to publish the discoveries of the expedition had become law — the great
voyage authorized into print at last, "under the command of lieutenant Wilkes," the rank
in the statute still the rank he had sailed with, four years and a continent ago. It was
set a little way down the page from "An act to regulate the pay of pursers."

## 5 · Two courts

A week after the verdict went south, a second court opened a second front.

The Register for the seventeenth of September: "Four marines, who allege themselves to
have been maltreated by Lieut. Wilkes, on board of the Vincennes, and other vessels of the
exploring expedition, have brought several suits against that officer, in the U.S. Court,
New York, and laid their damages to a large amount."

Not a naval court this time, and not officers. Four enlisted marines, suing their late
commander in a civil court, as citizens, for money — the men who had owned none of the
ship stepping into the one room in the Republic where a private's word weighed the same as
a commodore's. What the U.S. Court in New York did with their suits, this account cannot
say; the papers that carried the filing did not carry the finish.

But the suit said what the whole summer had been saying under its oaths and its
specifications: the charges were never paper. They stood on backs. The count of illegal
punishment the court had weighed against Wilkes did not rest on a principle. It rested on
men who had been stripped and bound and struck by lawful order and unlawful measure, at the
gangway gratings, the wood grate they were lashed against, and over the breech of a gun —
many of them at Honolulu, flogged past the lawful dozen when their three years were up and
they had asked for nothing but the discharge their contracts owed them. They were ashore in
the country now, alive, carrying the evidence where they carried everything else.

The verdict was on the road. The men whose backs had made the case would hear it no sooner
than anyone else.
