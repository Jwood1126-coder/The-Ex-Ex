## 1 · Tutuila, October 1839

Five days in the boats had gone into his back. From before daylight Reynolds sat cramped
on a hard thwart — the plank seat laid across the boat — while the crew pulled along the
reef and the lead, the marked line with its weight, went down and came up, one depth at a
time, the coral wall dropping away under the boat into water no line reached. By noon the
sun stood straight overhead, tropical and near-vertical, and there was no shade to be had
in an open boat. He took his notes and his angles when they were wanted, and he did not let the
men see him flag; he saved that for the page. "Even my bones were sore," he wrote of the
stretch, "…To quail or shrink or tire would not do."

This was the work the whole enterprise had been built to do, and none of the engravings
would ever show it: boats down at first light, men rowing and sounding along one reef and
then the next while the big ships stood off and on, angles taken from ship to boat to
shore, a coastline coming up on paper a fathom at a time. Reynolds was serving his full
apprenticeship at it. The charts came out clean, and the surveys ran clean, and the harbor
they carried away on paper — a drowned crater with one wall broken open to the sea, still
water inside cliffs that climbed straight into cloud — was worth every sore hour in the
boats.

Ashore, David Smith carried the instruments up the beach for him and set them so the sun
would not strike the glass of the sight. The record would grant Smith exactly one phrase in Reynolds'
hand — *the valuable attendance of my black valet* — and not a word past it. The man did
the work; the sentence kept the credit.

The work itself was clean, and the islands paid the young officers in evenings they would
keep the rest of their lives. One night Reynolds was guested in a chief's house and sat by
firelight, the fire crackling and its smoke hanging in the thatch, while the dancers moved
in their girdles of leaves, the whole figure of them changing at once, slow at first and
then faster until they stopped from plain exhaustion and began again after a rest. He was twenty-three, half a world from Lancaster, and the
journal that night glows off the page. There was still a great deal in his commander he
was prepared to admire.

## 2 · The great chief of the ships

An old chief of Leone — a village on the far side of the island — one of the principal men
of Tutuila, had shown the survey party a steady kindness across those weeks.
Tutuila is a spine of steep ridge running the length of it, the paths going up out of one
valley and down into the next, no easy walk for a young man and a hard one for an old one;
Leone sat over that ground from the anchorage. And the chief made the party a promise: he
would cross the island's mountains to pay his respects to the great chief of the ships. He
kept it.
An elderly man of rank came over that ground with his son beside him to honor a foreign
captain he had never laid eyes on, and presented himself at the anchorage in good faith.
Lieutenant Joseph Underwood carried word of it aft, with an account of the old man's
attentions to the American officers.

Wilkes refused to see him.

No reason was given, and none that the officers could afterward discover existed. Every
man who heard of it knew the shape of the thing, because they had watched the same
commander come apart, days at a stretch, over a letter that had addressed him by his true
rank and allowed him nothing above it. The injury a withheld honor had once done him he
now did to a chief who had crossed a mountain range to bring one. Whether the two sat level
in his own reckoning, no one aboard was in a position to say. What they could see was a
door, and an old man turned from it.

The junior officers did the one thing juniors could. They loaded the chief and his son
with presents out of their own sea chests until the visitors went away, in Reynolds' words,
"rich & happy & with a high opinion of our generosity" — a captain's rudeness paid off out
of midshipmen's stores. Then Reynolds wrote it down: "…much to our surprise & mortification.
Why, we could not tell! 'Twas not politic & was rude & insulting."

Underwood had a way of ending up in the doorway when his commander's failures with island
men came due, holding another man's request in his hands. The worst of those errands was
still eight months ahead of him, in Fiji.

## 3 · Five minutes

His own turn came within days.

The ship's company was mustered — every man called up from his work to stand in his place
and be counted, the crew fallen in by divisions along the open spar deck with the sun
already up — and Reynolds came late to it. How late became, within the hour, the most
carefully measured quantity on the ship. He was suspended from duty where he stood. The
order came stripped of even its customary courtesy: no *go below* to carry it off, none of
the small forms that let a punished officer keep his feet under him while the deck is taken
out from beneath them.

He took a turn or two to leeward — a few paces along the downwind rail, out of the wind —
and worked at the thing, trying to account for it, checking himself the way he would check
a doubtful sight through the glass. He could find nothing to charge himself with. He was
still at it when a second order reached him: to confine himself, in his own word, to his
*apartment* — his own cabin, a door he could shut and a bulkhead he could not see past.

Before he went down he put the plain question to the two officers who had stood near him at
the muster, Lieutenant Case and Mr. Knox, out on the forecastle at the head of the ship.
How late had he been? Not five minutes, they said. Not five minutes.

He wrote afterward that he could not lay his suspension to any other cause, and there was
no other cause to lay it to. There was a clock, a commander, and five minutes.

So he went below. A passed midshipman's cabin was a boxed-in space aft — back toward the
stern, a deck down — the width of a cot and a writing shelf, low enough overhead that a
tall man stooped, dim through the day and lit by a swinging lamp after dark, a place to
sleep and stow a chest and not much besides; confined to it in the tropics, with the ship's
routine going on without him overhead — the bells at every half hour, feet on the deck
planking, the muster he was no longer part of — a man had a great deal of time and one
small table to spend it at. The wardroom's talk found its way to
him through the bulkhead all the same. The other young men had been keeping their own
accounts of the commander for months, comparing them quietly, and their private verdict had
long since crossed a line it would never re-cross. The trouble was no longer the man's
moods. The trouble was the man.

## 4 · The pen

Below, with the door shut on him, he opened the journal.

Fifteen months earlier the same hand had written home that Lieutenant Wilkes was a wonder
— had talked the brilliant, exacting officer up to his family until they half loved a man
they had never met. The admiration had been real. So, now, was its opposite, and the
exchange had gone through at a fixed rate: a mountain crossing snubbed, an old man sent off
with a boy's presents, five minutes on a fair morning, a shut door. From here on the
entries about the commander stop reading like weather set down at the end of a watch and
start reading like a verdict being drafted in secret.

The word for him came some six weeks on, at another anchorage, over a party the officers wished
to give and the commander would not let them. Reynolds sat down to the page with no patience
left and set it there in the plainest terms his pen owned: "The foul scamp — he should be
hung… Mercy, how he is hated. Four sycophants cling to him—"

The sentence broke off at the dash, the four names still to come.

Two fixes in one hand, fifteen months apart: a wonder, and a foul scamp. Lay them on the
chart and draw the line between, and it is the truest course the expedition ever ran —
steeper than any it made good over the ground. It had been laid off, in the end, in five
minutes.
