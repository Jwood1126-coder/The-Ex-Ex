## 1 · The desert coast, the last week of January 1839

The sea turned brown a day before the land showed. The lookouts called it mud, and mud
it was: the Río Negro comes down out of the Patagonian desert carrying half the country
with it, and the tide spreads the stain miles offshore. The squadron was sailing over
the river before any man aboard had seen the river.

Halfway between Rio de Janeiro and Cape Horn, on a coast that is mostly desert, the
Río Negro lets the interior out into the Atlantic; there was a settlement up the
river, a bar — a ridge of sand drowned at high water, bared at low — across its mouth,
and no honest chart of either. Making the chart was
exactly the work the expedition had been invented for; the errand was a survey, and
nobody expected anything from the week but tedium.

The water ran shoal a long way out, and the
tide at the mouth rose and fell twelve feet — twice the height of a man — and did not
ebb and flood so much as run, a second river laid over the first, reversing itself four
times a day. For a strange bar the remedy is a pilot: a local man who keeps the
channels in his head and comes off in a boat to carry strangers in.

No pilot came off.

The squadron went at the bar with its own boats and its own leads. The reason was
ashore, waiting for dark.

## 2 · The flags

In the afternoon a heavy squall came off the land, and the schooners — the squadron's
two smallest, pilot boats bought in New York and sent around the world — ran aground
on the shoals. Boats went away from the bigger ships to help them. Up the river and out
of sight around the bend, the *Sea Gull* had the survey party aboard, Ringgold and a
knot of officers and several
of the scientifics, their day's work ending with the light going bad and the weather
getting worse.

A squadron talks across water in flags: hoists run up a line, read through a glass,
turned into sentences by a signal book. The whole language depends on being seen
plainly. In the last of that evening, through squall and haze, with the dusk coming
down as brown as the water, a hoist from the river was read wrong, and the sentence the
ships made of it was: *the natives are attacking.*

What followed took minutes, and none of them were wasted. Arms chests came open.
Cutlasses — short, heavy swords kept aboard for boat work — went over the side into the
boats by the armful, and five boats of armed men shoved off and pulled hard at the oars
for the river mouth: a
rescue, flying to men who did not know they needed one, from an enemy no one had seen.
Reynolds watched the boats go from the rail. The stroke was beautiful. Everyone pulling
believed it.

On the flagship the commander had his glass up. Wilkes had watched the squadron through
a spyglass since Madeira — his officers, his boats, the set of every sail, hunting the
fault before it could hatch — and now, in the worst light of the day, he read the
hoists over again and got them right. The recall went up. The five boats came back with
their cutlasses dry.

## 3 · The town

The other half of the evening the Americans got afterward, from the shore.

France was blockading Buenos Aires that season, and on that coast a French landing was
not a rumor; it was a contingency, with plans made against it. Strange men-of-war
standing in from the sea, answering nobody's expectations, meant the contingency had
arrived. The governor used the plans. The pilots
were called in off the river, so the enemy could grope for the channels himself.
The militia stood to its arms. The women and children went inland into the desert with
what they could carry, and the men who stayed watched the ships.

What the watchers at the landing saw, in the last light, was the warships putting five
boats of armed men into the water and sending them fast for the river mouth.

They had been right all along, then. Here was the descent, coming with the dark. The
militia gripped its muskets and waited to defend its families from the boats; the boats
pulled to rescue their shipmates from the militia; and the tide ran on, brown as ever,
under it all. Then the boats stopped, and turned, and went home to
the ships, and the night passed under arms on both shores, every musket in the country
awake because every other musket in the country was.

## 4 · The morning

Daylight sorted what dusk had scrambled. The colors could be made out for what they
were, the flag of a republic the settlement had no quarrel with; boats met;
explanations crossed; and the relief was so large on both sides that it turned, almost
at once, into hospitality. The whole affair had cost one evening's fright and one
night's sleep, and no one had to bury anything.

There was a standing procedure for the next part, and it needed no negotiation, because
every sea officer and every governor of every frightened port in that age carried it
already in his head. When armed strangers want each other's trust faster than
acquaintance can grow it, they exchange men. One of the town's people comes aboard and
dines at the cabin table; one of the ship's goes ashore and sleeps in the town; and
each man, well fed and courteously held, is the pledge of the other's safe return.
Nobody called it hostage-taking. It was manners.

At the Río Negro the pledge was never asked for. The governor sent down gifts of the
country instead, ostriches and armadillos among them; the ostriches never learned to live
aboard a ship, and died there; the armadillos were eaten. Reynolds kept one of the
shells. A brisk trade ran alongside the diplomacy — sailors' cutlery and pocket
compasses going ashore, guanaco skins coming off, the guanaco being the llama's wild
cousin — and the scientifics filled their crates. Men who had spent the evening sighting at one another's shadows spent the
forenoon arguing prices.

## 5 · South

Reynolds got his own pages out of that coast. Surveying along the beach he watched two
gaucho horsemen course the dark sand, and gave the journal what he could not give a
sketchbook: *"horse and rider in clear relief against the sky... How I wished for a
sketch of the fierce and mobile features and graceful costume of the men, and of the
Arab-looking steeds."*

Then the coast took its turn at him. The tide that had run like a river all week caught
his boat on the ebb — the tide running back out to sea — in a rising sea, and set about
penning it between the breakers and
the shore. From the ship they saw it and fired a gun — the loudest word a ship owns,
and it meant *come back.* He obeyed it, and came in over the side with the boat half
full of water. *"It is the most comfortable feeling in the world,"* he wrote, *"when a
man steps from danger into the midst of companions and security."*

Wilkes weighed what was left of the survey against the calendar, and the calendar won.
February was coming on; the southern summer had a bottom to it; and between the
squadron and everything it had been sent to do stood Cape Horn. The work was cut short.
The anchors came up, and the ships stood south along the desert.

On the run down, storm gear was served out at the mast, watch by watch, and after the
storm gear, up out of the hold, came fur — for men who had been trading in shirtsleeves
a week before. No order had ever named the squadron's next latitude to the men before
the mast; the movements were kept mysterious even from the crews who made them.

The fur named it.
