# STYLE GUIDE — The Ex. Ex.

Distilled from the strongest passages of the 2015–2020 drafts. This is your voice with the engine tuned.

## What we keep (because it works)

**1. The wonder aside.** Your signature move: standing beside the sailors in 1838 and quietly telling the reader what they were actually looking at. The bioluminescence resolved into dinoflagellates; the Magellanic Clouds resolved into galaxies by Hubble a century later. Rule: one per chapter, maximum. Filter it through a witness when possible — *Reynolds* wonders, and the narrator answers across time.

**2. The scale shock.** "Its deepest depths are as far down as cruising altitude for commercial planes are up." Concrete, physical, modern comparisons for 19th-century enormities. Keep them rare and keep them physical.

**3. Dark irony, played dry.** Porter wakes from his hanging worried about his ration. Reynolds muses that hanging would have been an easy death. The narrator never underlines the joke. This restraint is the best thing in your drafts — protect it.

**4. The direct address, sparingly.** "And so, the magic began." Once or twice an act, at a turn. It's a hand on the reader's shoulder, not a habit.

**5. Sensory ground truth.** Creaking ladders, bleating livestock, broken crockery, the smell of a slave ship's hold. When in doubt, go to the deck.

## What we cut (because it doesn't)

**1. Hedge stacking.** "Must have felt... likely... perhaps... probably... one might suspect" — the drafts use these as scaffolding. In the base narrative, hedge once and move on, or attribute the feeling to a source. In the novel, never.

**2. The apology preface.** We do not send readers to Philbrick on page one. We acknowledge our debts in a note at the back, like everyone else.

**3. Encyclopedia detours.** The Madeira wine history, the aqueduct engineering, the Krakatoa statistic — trimmed to one resonant beat each or cut. If a fact doesn't touch a character or the voyage, it goes.

**4. Editorializing on the sources' behalf.** Period racism appears in quotes, framed; the narrator's own sentences never adopt it.

**5. Redundancy.** The drafts tell the Georgia Banks story twice and the flogging three times. Everything happens once, in its best position.

## The tells to kill (anti-patterns)

These are the habits that make prose read machine-made. They are banned in the base narrative and doubly banned in the novel:

**1. Curation directives.** "File this scene." "Write the moral down." "Hold this image." "Keep X in view." The narrator does not hand the reader index cards. If a moment matters, its placement and weight say so. (Rare direct address — rule #4 above — survives; instructions don't.)

**2. Aphorism stacking.** "It was X, and it was Y. Both facts are permanent." One balanced antithesis per chapter is a spice; three is a metronome. When two truths must stand together, prefer *scene, then one dry line* over paired abstractions.

**3. The "not X but Y" reflex.** Fine occasionally; deadly as a rhythm. Same for triads ("the A, the B, and the C") — real prose varies its list lengths.

**4. Meta-commentary about "the book."** "This book will return to..." earns its place at most twice per act, at genuine load-bearing joints. "Where this book needs him"-type stage management: never.

**5. Explained irony.** If the sentence after the irony explains the irony, delete the sentence. Trust the reader; that trust *is* the voice.

**6. Uniform paragraph rhythm.** Vary hard: one-sentence paragraphs against ten-line ones. If three consecutive paragraphs open with the same shape (subject-verb declarative, or a fronted clause), rebreak them.

**7. Thesaurus elegance.** "Utilize," "myriad," "plethora," "tapestry," "testament to," "underscores," "stands as." The vocabulary is a sailor's and a workshop's: plain nouns, exact verbs.

## Register

Narrative nonfiction, past tense, close to the deck. Philbrick's discipline, Grann's momentum, and — for the interior weather of Wilkes — a drop of Mantel. Sentences average short; the long ones are earned and rhythmic. Chapter openings drop us into a moment, not a summary. Chapter endings land on an image or a cost, never a recap.

## Naming conventions

Wilkes is "Wilkes" (never "Charles"); Erskine is "Charlie" below decks and "Erskine" in formal beats; Reynolds is "Reynolds." Ships take *italics* and feminine pronouns, per the period. The expedition is "the Ex. Ex." after first full use per act.

## The two engines (never let a chapter idle)

Every chapter advances at least one of:
1. **The voyage** — miles, discovery, danger.
2. **The wound** — Wilkes' unraveling as witnessed by Charlie (the body) and Reynolds (the pen).

If a chapter advances neither, it's a detour. Cut or fold it.
