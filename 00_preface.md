# PREFACE — What Causes Mankind to Explore?

We like to believe exploration is written into us — that some inherited restlessness sends ships over horizons for the pure joy of finding out what's there. It is a beautiful belief, and the ledgers do not support it. The astrophysicist Neil deGrasse Tyson, surveying the great and expensive undertakings of our species — the pyramids, the voyages of Columbus, the race to the Moon — boiled the ledger down to three patrons who have ever actually paid for such things: the worship of a god or a king, the fear of ruin or war, and the promise of return on investment. The pyramids rose for a deified pharaoh. Columbus sailed on Spanish money that expected Spanish profit. Apollo flew because Sputnik's rocket could just as easily have carried a warhead, and everyone in Washington knew it — the Soviets had fired a single, brilliant blank, and the Moon was America's answer.

Wonder rides along. Wonder is the passenger, never the purchaser of the ticket.

This book is about a voyage that proves the rule and then, aboard six wooden ships, quietly breaks it. In 1838 the United States — a young country whose navy was less than half the size of Britain's, whose true frontier was not yet the West but the sea — launched the largest exploring expedition the Western world had ever attempted: six vessels, three hundred and forty-six men, and orders that amounted to a dare. Chart the Pacific for the whalemen. Survey the rivers and sounds of a Northwest coast that did not yet belong to anyone on paper. And in the brief violence of the southern summer, sail toward a place at the bottom of the world that Captain Cook himself had turned back from, pitying anyone who went farther.

Four years and eighty-seven thousand miles later the squadron came home to a country that barely looked up. It brought back charts so accurate that a hundred years on, in another war, American forces were still steering by some of them. It brought back specimens by the ton — enough that the nation would need a new institution to hold them, and the institution became the Smithsonian. It brought back the survey of a coastline its commander named, on his own authority, the Antarctic Continent — fifteen hundred miles of it, traced by men in unheated wooden ships that were never strengthened for ice. And it brought back two fewer ships and twenty-eight fewer men than it sailed with, and a set of courts-martial that put its own commander on trial before the varnish was dry on the charts.

The United States Exploring Expedition — the Ex. Ex., as its own sailors called it — should sit beside Lewis and Clark in the national memory. It does not, and this book is partly about why: the story's hero was its own worst enemy, and a country that loves discoverers did not know what to do with one it had convicted.

But mostly this book is about what the voyage felt like from the deck. Its guides are two young men who were there and wrote it down. One was a Boston cabin boy named Charlie Erskine, who could not yet read or write when the squadron sailed, who loved his commander like the father he never had, and who came to hate him with a completeness that nearly changed American history one dark night in the Atlantic. The other was a passed midshipman named William Reynolds, twenty-two years old at the sailing, ordered like every officer to keep a journal for government inspection — who obeyed, and also kept a second journal, a secret one, against orders, where the truth went. Nearly everything alive in the record of this voyage — everything with blood and salt in it — comes from an illegal book and the memory of a boy.

They sailed into a world where the map still had blank spaces and the sea still had monsters, where a glowing wake was a mystery and the two pale clouds in the southern sky had no explanation on Earth. Knowledge in 1838 traveled at the speed of a ship, and how little these men were permitted to know is easy to forget and worth the effort of remembering. What follows is what they found — the continent, and the man.

And so, the voyage began — the way all of them begin: with money, and politics, and one loud believer from Ohio whom nobody could shut up.
